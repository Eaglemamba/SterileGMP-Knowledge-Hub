Learning Objectives

  • Understand the origin and legal status of PIC/S PE 011-1: it is guidance, not a mandatory rule, but the competent authorities of PIC/S member countries may convert it into legally binding standards

Document History

Introduction

Original Text

This Guide is based on the EU Guidelines on Good Distribution Practice (GDP) of Medicinal Products for Human Use (2013/C 343/01). The EU Guidelines have been adapted by the Expert Circle on GDP for PIC/S purposes. However, the EU specific references have been deleted in this Guide.

This Guide has been adopted by PIC/S as a guidance document. It is up to each PIC/S Participating Authority to decide whether it should become a legally-binding standard.

The wholesale distribution of medicinal products is an important activity in integrated supply chain management. Today's distribution network for medicinal products is increasingly complex and involves many players. These guidelines lay down appropriate tools to assist wholesale distributors in conducting their activities and to prevent falsified medicines from entering the legal supply chain. Compliance with these guidelines will ensure control of the distribution chain and consequently maintain the quality and the integrity of medicinal products.

Wholesale distribution of medicinal products is all activities consisting of procuring, holding, supplying, importing or exporting medicinal products, apart from supplying medicinal products to the public. Such activities are carried out with manufacturers or their depositories, importers, other wholesale distributors or with pharmacists and persons authorized or entitled to supply medicinal products to the public.

In the territories of some PIC/S Participating Authorities importation may fall under GMP and a manufacturer's licence may be required.

Any person acting as a wholesale distributor has to hold a wholesale distribution licence in accordance with national legislation.

Possession of a manufacturing licence includes authorisation to distribute the medicinal products covered by the authorisation. Manufacturers performing any distribution activities with their own products must therefore comply with GDP.

The definition of wholesale distribution does not depend on whether that distributor is established or operating in specific customs areas, such as in free zones or in free warehouses. All obligations related to wholesale distribution activities (such as importing, exporting, holding or supplying) also apply to these distributors. Relevant sections of these guidelines should also be adhered to by other actors involved in the distribution of medicinal products.

A glossary of some terms used in the Guide has been incorporated as Annex 1.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Legal Status of GDP and the CDMO's Responsibility

EU GDP vs. PIC/S PE 011-1: This guide is rooted in the EU GDP Guidelines (2013/C 343/01), but EU-specific provisions have been removed so that it can apply to all PIC/S participating authorities (including Taiwan, under TFDA jurisdiction). It helps to understand this "translation": the PIC/S version carries over the spirit of the EU guidelines and the core requirements are the same, but implementation details are left to each national competent authority.

Guidance vs. Legally Binding: PE 011-1 is itself "guidance", not a "regulation". However, TFDA may incorporate its content into Taiwan's regulatory requirements. In practice, if you are asked during an inspection to explain how you comply with GDP, this document is the inspector's frame of reference.

The manufacturer's GDP obligations: The key point is that a holder of a manufacturing authorisation must comply with GDP whenever it carries out any distribution activity for its own products (including release from the site, transfers and export). For a Taiwanese CDMO, this means the entire logistics chain, from release of the finished product to delivery to the customer, falls within the scope of GDP.

Analogy

Imagine a restaurant (the CDMO) that prepares an exquisite dish (the medicinal product). GMP governs how the dish is made in the kitchen; GDP governs the journey from the kitchen to the customer's hands. Even if the dish was made flawlessly, if the temperature runs out of control during delivery, or the dish is swapped (falsified product enters the chain), what finally reaches the customer is no longer the same dish.

GDP is the quality control mechanism for this "last mile". For a CDMO, GMP is the core competence, but GDP is what keeps that core competence from being undone during delivery.

Key Note

Free zones are not exempt: The document states explicitly that operators distributing medicinal products in free zones or bonded warehouses are also subject to GDP. This deserves particular attention if a Taiwanese CDMO moves products in and out of export processing zones or bonded areas.

The boundary of the definition of wholesale distribution: "Supplying directly to the public" is not wholesale distribution, so retail activity at the pharmacy (the last mile) is outside the scope of this guide. A CDMO's customers, however, are pharmaceutical manufacturers or other wholesalers, so the whole chain falls under GDP.

Practical Application

Suppose your CDMO finishes filling and packaging a batch of sterile injectables, and then delivers the product in the company's own refrigerated truck to the customer's warehouse in Hsinchu, from where the customer arranges export.

Under this section, your company holds a manufacturing authorisation and carries out the distribution itself, so you must confirm: (1) whether the company has obtained a wholesale distribution authorisation or qualifies for a relevant exemption; (2) whether temperature monitoring and records for the refrigerated truck meet GDP requirements; (3) whether the handover documents are complete enough to trace responsibility along the whole distribution chain.

Practice Questions

  1. If a Taiwanese CDMO holds a GMP manufacturing licence, does it automatically qualify to carry out wholesale distribution? What additional licence is needed?
  2. If a customer appoints a third-party logistics provider (3PL) to collect finished product from the CDMO site, does the CDMO still bear GDP responsibility for that transport? How is responsibility allocated?
  3. "Annex 1 (Glossary)" appears at the end of this guide. When should you, as a learner, consult it?
Purpose

Original Text

In order to ensure the maintaining of high standards of quality assurance and the integrity of the distribution processes of medicinal products, to promote uniformity in licensing of wholesaling of medicinal products and to further facilitate the removal of barriers to trade in medicinal products, the following Guide to Good Distribution Practice (GDP) for Medicinal Products has been adopted.

Administrative measures of national health authorities should be directed towards the application of these standards in practice, and any new or amended national regulations for good distribution practice should at least meet their level. These standards are also intended to serve wholesale distributors as a basis for the elaboration of specific rules adapted to their individual needs.

It is recognised that there are acceptable methods, other than those described in this Guide, which are capable of achieving the principles of the Guide. This document provides guidance for preparation for inspections and may be used for training purposes.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Three Purposes of GDP

This section opens by stating that GDP has three parallel aims:

  • Quality Assurance & Integrity: Ensure that the quality of medicinal products is not compromised throughout the distribution process, from the manufacturer to downstream customers.
  • Uniformity in Licensing: Bring wholesale licensing systems in different countries closer together, reducing regulatory friction in cross-border trade.
  • Remove Barriers to Trade: Increase mutual recognition between markets through standardisation.

What this means for a CDMO: Your customers may come from many countries, and their downstream regulators will also require that distribution of the product complies with GDP. Understanding the purpose of GDP helps you explain your distribution management system clearly during customer audits.

Analogy

GDP is like the motorway standard in the "rules of the road". Motorways in different countries may differ in small details (lane width, speed limits), but the core safety standards (guardrail height, spacing of emergency exits) are the same. PIC/S GDP is the common standard that lets countries trust each other's "motorway quality".

Note that the document says "other acceptable methods" may be used. This means GDP is outcome-oriented, not a rigid checklist. You may innovate, as long as you achieve the same quality assurance outcome and can explain it clearly to the inspector.

Key Note

The practical implication of "acceptable methods": This wording gives companies flexibility, but it also brings responsibility. If you adopt an unconventional method (for example, IoT sensors in place of traditional temperature data loggers), you must be able to: (1) demonstrate that the method is equivalent or superior to the traditional one; (2) present the validation data clearly during an inspection. It is not enough to say "we have a better way"; you must bring evidence.

Training purpose stated explicitly: The document states explicitly that this guide may be used for training purposes, so citing PE 011-1 clauses directly in your training records is appropriate and encouraged.

Practice Questions

  1. If TFDA issues national GDP requirements that are stricter than PE 011-1, which document should the company follow? How does the "minimum standard" logic of GDP apply here?
  2. Your CDMO wants to replace traditional temperature data loggers with a real-time electronic temperature monitoring system. Under the GDP framework, what documents do you need to prepare to support this decision?
Scope

Original Text

The standards set out herein apply to medicines and similar products intended for human use. It is recommended, however, that the same kind of attention be given to the distribution of veterinary medicinal products. This guideline can also be applicable for Investigational Medicinal Products (IMP).

At the time of issue, this document reflected the current state of the art. It is not intended to be a barrier to technical innovation or the pursuit of excellence or to place any restraint upon the development of new concepts or new technologies, which have been validated and provide a level of Quality Assurance and integrity of the distribution processes at least equivalent to those set out in this Guide.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Levels of Applicability

Mandatory: Medicinal products for human use. This is the core target of the guide.

Recommended: Veterinary medicinal products. The document uses "recommended" rather than "shall", which makes this a best-practice recommendation.

Also applicable: Investigational medicinal products (IMPs). GDP applies equally to medicinal products used in clinical trials. This matters particularly for a CDMO that takes on clinical trial batch filling, because logistics control for IMPs is often stricter than for commercial batches (additional requirements such as blinding and randomisation).

Analogy

Think of a "Food Safety Act": it clearly regulates restaurants (medicinal products for human use), recommends that pet food manufacturers follow it too (veterinary medicines), and equally applies to new food formulations still under development in the laboratory (IMPs).

The statement that GDP should "not hinder technological innovation" is an open-ended declaration. Regulations often lag behind technology, and this wording gives companies room to introduce new technologies (such as blockchain tracking or AI temperature prediction), provided they have been validated and achieve equivalent or better quality assurance.

Key Note: Special GDP Challenges for IMPs

Distribution of investigational medicinal products is more complex than for commercial batches. In addition to GDP requirements, consider the following:

  • Blinding: Label design must not reveal the randomisation allocation, and logistics staff need special training
  • Traceability to patient level: IMPs usually require records down to the batch and quantity received by each patient
  • Impact assessment of temperature excursions: For IMPs, any temperature excursion may affect the validity of the trial results, not just product quality
  • Management of returned medicines: Returned IMPs generally may not be reused and require strict segregation and destruction procedures

Practical Application

Your CDMO is bidding for a Phase II clinical trial sterile filling project. The customer asks you to take charge of both cold storage of the finished product (2–8°C) and distribution within Taiwan to the clinical trial sites.

When preparing for the client audit, you need to show: (1) a GDP-compliant warehouse management SOP; (2) a cold chain validation report (including temperature mapping of the distribution routes); (3) procedures for investigating temperature excursions and assessing the product; (4) an SOP for managing returned product specific to IMPs. The clause stating that the guide also applies to IMPs is the regulatory basis you cite.

Practice Questions

  1. This guide says it should "not hinder technological innovation". If your company wants to introduce IoT sensors for real-time temperature monitoring throughout transport in place of traditional data loggers, what validation must you complete before you can say the method is "at least equivalent"?
  2. Your CDMO's customer has both commercial products and IMPs, and both are stored in the same warehouse. What segregation do you need to build into the SOPs or the warehouse design?
  3. The 2014 edition of PE 011-1 is now more than ten years old. What modern tools in cold chain technology and digital tracking were not foreseen at the time? How can these tools be brought into GDP compliance under the principle of "validated and equivalent"?

Learning Objectives for This Chapter

  • Understand the four building blocks of a GDP quality system (organisational structure, procedures, processes and resources) and be able to self-assess your CDMO warehouse operation against them
  • Understand the role and regulatory significance of the Designated Responsible Person

1.1 Principle

Original Text

Wholesale distributors should maintain a quality system setting out responsibilities, processes and risk management principles in relation to their activities. All distribution activities should be clearly defined in procedures and systematically reviewed. All critical steps of distribution processes and significant changes should be justified and where relevant validated.

The quality system is the responsibility of the organisation's management and requires their leadership and active participation and should be supported by staff commitment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Three Cornerstones of a GDP Quality System

The 1.1 Principle paragraph distils the essence of the whole of Chapter 1. It can be broken down into three layers:

  • Systemic: The quality system must document responsibilities and processes in writing. Verbal agreements do not count, and neither does simply having a handful of SOPs
  • Risk-based: The quality system must incorporate risk management principles. This means the key decisions behind your SOPs should be supported by documented risk assessments
  • Leadership-driven: The quality system is not a one-man show for the QA department. It is a management responsibility and requires the leadership and active participation of senior management

Identifying "critical steps": In GDP, critical steps typically include: receipt inspection of temperature-sensitive products, temperature monitoring of cold storage, document checks before finished product release, and segregation of hazardous goods. Confirming which steps are critical is the starting point for building your GDP validation plan.

Analogy

Think of the GDP quality system as the service quality system of a star-rated hotel. The hotel owner (management) must personally set the service standards (the quality system) and make sure every employee understands and follows them. If the service manual is merely printed and left in the staff lounge, guests (patients) may still be served a meal at the wrong temperature.

The term "staff commitment" was deliberately placed in the regulatory text. It signals that in a GDP inspection, inspectors do not only look at documents; they also interview operators to confirm that they truly understand and apply the procedures.

Key Note

The scope of validation in GDP: Validation in GDP mainly targets warehousing and distribution processes, not manufacturing processes. Examples include temperature mapping of cold storage, qualification of cold chain distribution routes, and thermal performance testing of packaging systems. These activities need a plan, a report and approval for release, following the same logic as GMP validation but applied to different subjects.

Practice Questions

  1. In your CDMO warehouse operation, which three steps do you consider to be "critical steps" as defined under GDP? Have you formally validated these steps?
  2. If an inspector asks, "Please explain how your management actively participates in the operation of the GDP quality system," how would you answer? What concrete documented evidence could you provide?
1.2 Quality System

Original Text

1.2.1 The system for managing quality should encompass the organisational structure, procedures, processes and resources, as well as activities necessary to ensure confidence that the product delivered maintains its quality and integrity and remains within the legal supply chain during storage and/or transportation.

1.2.2 The quality system should be fully documented and its effectiveness monitored. All quality system related activities should be defined and documented. A quality manual or equivalent documentation approach should be established.

1.2.3 Designated responsible person(s) should be appointed by the management, who should have clearly specified authority and responsibility for ensuring that a quality system is implemented and maintained.

1.2.4 The management of the distributor should ensure that all parts of the quality system are adequately resourced with competent personnel, and suitable and sufficient premises, equipment and facilities.

1.2.5 The size, structure and complexity of distributor's activities should be taken into consideration when developing or modifying the quality system. The quality system should be proportionate.

1.2.6 A change control system should be in place. This system should incorporate quality risk management principles, and be proportionate and effective.

1.2.7 The quality system should ensure that:
(i) medicinal products are procured, held, supplied, imported or exported in a way that is compliant with the requirements of GDP;
(ii) management responsibilities are clearly specified;
(iii) products are delivered to the right recipients within a satisfactory time period;
(iv) records are made contemporaneously;
(v) deviations from established procedures are documented and investigated;
(vi) appropriate corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) are taken to correct deviations and prevent them in line with the principles of quality risk management.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Four Elements of a GDP Quality System

1.2.1 explicitly lists the four components of a quality system:

  • Organisational structure: Who is responsible for what? Are authorities and responsibilities clearly documented?
  • Procedures: Does the SOP system fully cover all distribution activities?
  • Processes: Are actual operating processes consistent with the SOPs and effectively monitored?
  • Resources: Are personnel competence, facilities, equipment and system tools adequate?

Quality Manual: 1.2.2 requires a quality manual or equivalent documentation. For a large CDMO, this is usually a top-level document describing the architecture of the whole quality management system, linking down to the individual SOPs and work instructions. "Or equivalent" means that a purely electronic document management system is also acceptable, as long as its structure is clear and traceable.

Key Concept: Designated Responsible Person

1.2.3 requires the formal appointment of one or more persons with clear authority who are responsible for ensuring that the quality system is implemented and maintained. In GDP, this role is comparable to the Qualified Person (QP) in GMP, or to the head of quality in Taiwan's GMP system.

Key point: The person's responsibilities must be designated in writing, and the role cannot be a title in name only. During an inspection, the inspector will ask what this person has specifically done to ensure that the quality system works effectively.

Common CDMO blind spot: Many CDMOs have a complete QA organisation for GMP, but the person responsible for GDP (warehousing and distribution) is often the warehouse manager rather than someone with a QA background. This can leave the quality system lacking a quality perspective.

Analogy

What proportionality means (1.2.5): It is like choosing fire protection equipment. A few fire extinguishers are enough for a small office, but a large warehouse facility needs a full automatic sprinkler system and a fire management plan. The scale and complexity of your GDP quality system should be commensurate with the size of your business. It need not be over-engineered, but a rudimentary system should not be used to cover a complex operation.

1.2.7(iii) "Right recipients within a satisfactory time": This is especially important for the CDMO's cold chain business. Delivering medicines to the right recipient but with a temperature excursion beyond limits, or at the right temperature but to the wrong location, are both GDP deviations.

Key Note: The Regulatory Significance of Contemporaneous Records

"Contemporaneously" in 1.2.7(iv) is a key regulatory term. It means that:

  • Records must be completed at the time the activity takes place or immediately afterwards, and must not be back-filled later
  • The timestamps of electronic systems must be reliable and must not be easily altered
  • If records are back-filled, even if the content is correct, it is a major finding in an inspection (a data integrity issue, as addressed by 21 CFR Part 11)

For CDMO warehousing, this means that all goods-in/goods-out records, temperature monitoring records and deviation records must be completed in real time, not compiled daily or per shift.

Practical Application

Your CDMO is preparing for a TFDA GDP inspection. The inspector asks to see the records of your GDP change control system.

Under 1.2.6, you need to demonstrate: (1) the existence of a change control SOP and its version history; (2) at least one change control case actually completed within the past year (for example, replacing the temperature recording equipment of a cold store); (3) how risk assessment was performed for this change and how the decision on whether revalidation was needed was made. If your CDMO has no change control records at all for the past year, the inspector may question whether your quality system is genuinely operating.

Practice Questions

  1. 1.2.7 lists six matters that the quality system must ensure. For your CDMO warehouse operation, give one specific SOP or procedure that corresponds to each of them.
  2. If your CDMO has both a GMP Designated Person and a GDP Designated Responsible Person, can these two roles be held by the same person? What are the advantages and disadvantages of each approach?
  3. How is "proportionate" applied in practice? Where should the difference in GDP quality system scale be reflected between a small CDMO (fewer than 100 distributed batches per year) and a large CDMO (more than 1,000 distributed batches per year)?
1.3 Management of Outsourced Activities

Original Text

The quality system should extend to the control and review of any outsourced activities related to the procurement, holding, supply, import or export of medicinal products. These processes should incorporate quality risk management and include:

  • (i) assessing the suitability and competence of the Contract Acceptor to carry out the activity, preserving the integrity and security of the medicinal products, and requesting, preserving documentation, and checking authorisation or marketing status, if required;
  • (ii) defining the responsibilities and communication processes for the quality-related activities of the parties involved;
  • (iii) monitoring and review of the performance of the Contract Acceptor, and the identification and implementation of any required improvements on a regular basis.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: Outsourcing Does Not Transfer Responsibility

The core message of 1.3 is: you can outsource activities, but you cannot outsource your responsibility.

When a CDMO outsources cold chain transportation to a third-party logistics provider (3PL), GDP responsibility is not transferred from the CDMO to the 3PL. The CDMO must still ensure that the 3PL is competent, has appropriate procedures, and is monitored periodically for its performance.

Three requirements for managing outsourced activities:

  • Prior Assessment: Before outsourcing, the qualifications and capability of the Contract Acceptor must be assessed (i.e. vendor qualification / supplier qualification audit)
  • Contractual Definition: The quality responsibilities and communication mechanisms of each party must be clearly defined in writing (i.e. a Quality Agreement / Technical Agreement)
  • Ongoing Monitoring: The actual performance of the Contract Acceptor must be reviewed periodically, with improvement actions taken when problems are found

Analogy

Outsourcing is like the main contractor - subcontractor relationship in a renovation project. The owner (the CDMO's customer) awards the work to the main contractor (the CDMO), but if a subcontractor (the 3PL) causes a problem, the owner will still hold the main contractor accountable.

Therefore, the CDMO as the "main contractor" must: (1) select subcontractors carefully (supplier audit); (2) state in the contract who is responsible for what (quality agreement); (3) inspect the subcontractor's workmanship on site periodically (periodic audits / KPI monitoring).

Key Note: Why 3PL Audits Are Essential in GDP

Many CDMOs manage their 3PL suppliers only to the stage of "once the contract is signed, we are fine". Under 1.3(iii), you need to perform the following on the 3PL periodically (usually every year or every two years):

  • Document review: Validity of the GDP licence, SOP versions, temperature monitoring records
  • On-site audit: Storage environment, temperature recording equipment, personnel training records
  • KPI review: On-time delivery rate, number of temperature excursion events, complaint frequency

Inspectors will ask to see your most recent audit report on the 3PL and the follow-up CAPA implementation records.

Practical Application

Your CDMO uses one 3PL provider (Party A) for all domestic finished product distribution in Taiwan, and another specialist cold chain company (Party B) for the international cold chain leg for export to Southeast Asia.

Under 1.3, your GDP quality system should cover: (1) a separate supplier assessment file for each of Party A and Party B (including confirmation of the GDP licence); (2) separate quality/technical agreements with each, clearly defining the obligation to report temperature excursions and the division of investigation responsibilities; (3) an annual audit plan with on-site audits of A and B in rotation; (4) a monthly KPI report tracking the distribution performance of both parties. If one party performs poorly, your system needs a formal mechanism to initiate an improvement plan.

Practice Questions

  1. Which distribution-related activities does your CDMO currently outsource? Is there a corresponding quality/technical agreement for each of them? Does the agreement clearly assign responsibility for temperature excursions?
  2. If an inspector asks, "How do you confirm that the third-party logistics company is qualified to handle your products (which require a 2–8°C cold chain)?", what documents can you present?
  3. What is the background to the requirement of "checking authorisation or marketing status"? How does it relate to the aim of preventing falsified medicines?
1.4 Management Review and Monitoring

Original Text

1.4.1 The management should have a formal process for reviewing the quality system on a periodic basis. The review should include:

  • (i) measurement of the achievement of quality system objectives;
  • (ii) assessment of performance indicators that can be used to monitor the effectiveness of processes within the quality system, such as complaints, recalls, returns, deviations, CAPA, changes to processes; feedback on outsourced activities; self-assessment processes including risk assessments and audits; and external assessments such as inspections, findings and customer audits;
  • (iii) emerging regulations, guidance and quality issues that can impact the quality management system;
  • (iv) innovations that might enhance the quality system;
  • (v) changes in business environment and objectives.

1.4.2 The outcome of each management review of the quality system should be documented in a timely manner and effectively communicated internally.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Five Areas of Management Review

A GDP management review is not a perfunctory annual meeting. It is a systematic evaluation that needs to cover five areas:

  1. Achievement of objectives: Did you meet the GDP quality objectives you set (e.g. a temperature excursion rate below X%) this year?
  2. Performance indicators (KPIs): Complaints, recalls, returns, deviations, CAPA, outsourced activities, self-assessments, external audits
  3. Regulatory developments: Have any new GDP guidelines or TFDA interpretations this year affected your system?
  4. Innovation opportunities: Are there new technologies or methods that could strengthen your quality system?
  5. Changes in the business environment: Have there been major changes in your customer base, product lines or distribution regions that affect the design of the GDP system?

Analogy

Management Review is like an annual vehicle inspection combined with a mileage-based service. It is not only about checking whether the engine is broken (deviations), but also about the degree of tyre wear (long-term trends), confirming that service records are complete (documentation), learning whether new vehicle safety regulations have been introduced this year (regulatory developments), and considering whether to upgrade the navigation system (innovation).

"Timely manner" echoes the contemporaneous recording requirement in 1.2.7(iv). The conclusions of a management review must not be forgotten once the meeting ends. They must be documented and communicated internally. Inspectors often ask to see the minutes of the most recent management review and the follow-up tracking of action items.

Key Note: Management Review vs. CAPA Review

Two concepts that newcomers often confuse:

  • CAPA Review (individual event level): Corrective and preventive actions for a single deviation or complaint, part of day-to-day quality management
  • Management Review (system level): An evaluation of the overall health of the entire quality system, demonstrating management leadership

Management Review needs to bring together the trends from all CAPAs: "Over the past year we had 12 temperature excursion events, concentrated in summer and all on the same distribution route. Does this mean our cold chain packaging design needs to be adjusted?" This is the kind of question a management review should answer.

Practical Application

You are preparing the slides for the annual GDP Management Review to report to senior management. Under 1.4.1, your slides should cover at least:

  • Achievement of annual GDP KPIs (number of temperature excursions, number of complaints, deviation closure rate)
  • Audits completed this year (supplier audits and internal audits) and the main findings
  • CAPA completion rate and risk assessment of open items
  • Regulatory changes affecting GDP this year (e.g. new TFDA announcements, PIC/S updates)
  • Proposed quality objectives and improvement plans for next year

This report must be formally approved by management, and the follow-up action items must have an assigned owner and a due date.

Practice Questions

  1. List five GDP KPIs that you think best reflect the distribution quality of your CDMO, and explain how you would collect the data.
  2. If this year's management review finds that a 3PL's temperature excursion rate has increased by 50% compared with last year, what specific actions should management take? How should these actions be documented?
  3. What special significance does the review item "Changes in business environment and objectives" have for a rapidly expanding CDMO (for example, one that has added an mRNA vaccine product line)?
1.5 Quality Risk Management

Original Text

1.5.1 Quality risk management is a systematic process for the assessment, control, communication and review of risks to the quality of medicinal products. It can be applied both proactively and retrospectively.

1.5.2 Quality risk management should ensure that the evaluation of the risk to quality is based on scientific knowledge, experience with the process and ultimately links to the protection of the patient. The level of effort, formality and documentation of the process should be commensurate with the level of risk. Examples of the processes and applications of quality risk management can be found in guideline Q9 of the International Conference on Harmonisation (ICH).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Four Steps of QRM and Its Two-Way Application

According to 1.5.1, quality risk management comprises four steps, fully consistent with the ICH Q9 framework:

  • Assessment: Identify risks and evaluate their probability and severity
  • Control: Decide on measures to reduce risk (e.g. increase the frequency of temperature monitoring, switch to insulated containers)
  • Communication: Communicate the results of the risk assessment to relevant stakeholders (management, customers, regulators)
  • Review: Re-evaluate risks periodically and confirm that the control measures are effective

Two-way application:

  • Proactive: Perform a risk assessment before introducing a new distribution route or a new 3PL
  • Retrospective: After a temperature excursion, use QRM to analyse the root cause and assess the impact on the product

Key Concept: ICH Q9 Tools Applied in GDP

1.5.2 explicitly refers to ICH Q9, meaning that QRM in GDP should follow the same methodological framework. ICH Q9 risk assessment tools commonly used in GDP include:

  • FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis): Suited to analysing potential failures at each step of the distribution process (e.g. refrigerated vehicle breakdown, driver handover errors) and evaluating the RPN value
  • HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points): Well established in food safety; GDP cold chain management can borrow this framework to identify critical control points
  • Fishbone (Ishikawa diagram): Suited to deviation investigations, to identify the root causes of temperature excursions (man, machine, material, method, environment)

Analogy

Practical meaning of "commensurate with the level of risk": This is like the logic of choosing insurance. If a small screw comes loose (low risk), you simply tighten it with a hex key and do not need to ask an equipment engineer to write a formal deviation report. But if a refrigeration compressor makes an abnormal noise (high risk), you must immediately start a formal assessment, call in a contractor for repair and write a deviation report.

QRM makes your quality system more efficient: it puts effort into the risks that really matter, rather than applying the highest-grade investigation procedure to everything. This is also why the phrase "effort, formality and documentation should be commensurate with risk" appears repeatedly in GMP/GDP frameworks.

Key Note: The Most Common Risk Assessment Scenarios in GDP

In CDMO GDP practice, the following situations call for formal QRM:

  • Introducing a new 3PL: Assess its GDP qualification, facility conditions and deviation history
  • New distribution route: Assess transit time, temperature control risks and worst-case conditions (summer heat, transport delays)
  • Temperature excursion investigation: Assess the potential impact of the excursion on product quality and decide whether the product can be released or a recall is needed
  • Seasonal risk: The particular challenge of Taiwan's summer heat for cold chain distribution (ambient temperatures can exceed 35°C)
  • Power outage or equipment failure: Assess the quality impact on stored product after a power outage in a cold store

Practical Application

Scenario: This summer, your CDMO plans to ship a batch of 2–8°C monoclonal antibody drug substance (DS) to Singapore for the first time. The entire journey takes 14 hours (Taiwan site dispatch → Taoyuan Airport → Singapore Changi Airport → customer warehouse).

Under the QRM framework in 1.5, you should carry out the following before shipment:

  • Risk assessment: Use FMEA to identify potential failures in each leg (e.g. customs clearance delays leaving the shipment at the airport for more than 4 hours, insufficient dry ice in the refrigerated container)
  • Control measures: Ask the airline for priority transit service, use insulated containers with validated dry ice quantities, and place a data logger in the container
  • Communication: Confirm receiving procedures with the customer before shipment and inform them how deviations will be handled
  • Review: After receipt, review the temperature profile from the data logger to confirm that the whole journey met the specification, and include the results in your GDP quality records

Practice Questions

  1. "Protection of the patient" is the ultimate purpose of QRM. In the GDP context, where there are several steps between you and the patient, how would you explain to warehouse operators the direct link between their work and patient safety?
  2. If your CDMO has only one QA person responsible for GDP, how would you prioritise QRM work to ensure that limited resources go to the most important risk controls?
  3. ICH Q9(R1) is the revised version of Q9 (published in 2023). Which aspects of QRM does this revision strengthen? What does this mean for implementing QRM in your GDP system? (Hint: start from ICH Q9(R1)'s emphasis on subjectivity and formality.)

Section Summary

  • 1.1 Principle: The GDP quality system must cover the allocation of responsibilities, processes and procedures, and risk management principles; management must take leadership responsibility and cannot rely on the QA department alone
  • 1.2 Quality System: The four elements (organisation, procedures, processes, resources) must all be documented and monitored; the Designated Responsible Person must be formally appointed with clear authority; change control and CAPA are indispensable components of the quality system
  • 1.3 Outsourced Activities: Outsourcing does not transfer responsibility; the Contract Acceptor must be assessed, responsibilities defined in writing, and performance monitored periodically; auditing the 3PL is a core task of GDP compliance
  • 1.4 Management Review: Periodic review must cover five areas (KPIs, regulatory developments, innovation, business changes, achievement of objectives); conclusions must be documented in a timely manner and communicated internally
  • 1.5 Quality Risk Management: QRM is the underlying logic of GDP and follows the ICH Q9 framework; it can be applied proactively or retrospectively; the level of effort is proportionate to the level of risk; the ultimate purpose is to protect the patient
  • Key takeaway for CDMOs: A Taiwan CDMO's GDP quality system cannot be a mere add-on to its GMP system. It needs to be designed independently for the characteristics of warehousing, cold chain and outsourced logistics, with sufficient resources committed

2.1 Principle

Original Text

2.1 PRINCIPLE

The correct distribution of medicinal products relies upon people. For this reason, there must be sufficient competent personnel to carry out all the tasks for which the wholesale distributor is responsible. Individual responsibilities should be clearly understood by the staff and be recorded.

Tutorial Commentary

Key concept: people are the key to GDP

The very first sentence of the GDP Principle makes the point directly: the correct distribution of medicinal products relies upon people. This differs from the process-design-first logic of GMP. At the distribution end, the judgement, sense of responsibility and competence of personnel directly determine the quality integrity of the entire supply chain.

In a regulatory context, "competence" has three dimensions:

  • Knowledge: understanding GDP requirements and product characteristics
  • Skill: being able to carry out procedures correctly
  • Attitude: commitment to quality and compliance, and vigilance

Analogy

Imagine a logistics warehouse as a baseball team:

  • Too few players: the field is bound to be full of gaps (insufficient personnel numbers)
  • Players do not know which position they play: the ball rolls through and nobody fields it (unclear responsibilities)
  • Every player knows their own area of defence: only then can the team work together smoothly (responsibilities are recorded)

GDP's requirement that responsibilities be "clearly understood and recorded" essentially ensures that no distribution activity is left with nobody in charge.

Key Notes

The regulatory meaning of "recorded": Verbal assignment of responsibilities is not acceptable in a GDP inspection. Written documents such as job descriptions, organisation charts and authorisation matrices are the main evidence auditors use to verify that personnel responsibilities are clear.

Practice Questions

  1. If a CDMO warehouse department currently has 2 vacant positions because of staff turnover, what does GDP require in this situation?
  2. Is "competence" the same as "training completed"? Under the GDP framework, how can you demonstrate that a new warehouse operator is competent to perform the tasks?
2.2 General

Original Text

2.2 GENERAL

2.2.1 There should be an adequate number of competent personnel involved in all stages of the wholesale distribution activities of medicinal products. The number of personnel required will depend on the volume and scope of activities.

2.2.2 The organisational structure of the wholesale distributor should be set out in an organisation chart. The role, responsibilities, and interrelationships of all personnel should be clearly indicated.

2.2.3 The role and responsibilities of employees working in key positions should be set out in written job descriptions, along with any arrangements for deputising.

Tutorial Commentary

Key concept: three layers of documentation for the organisational structure

GDP 2.2 requires documentation of organisation management at three levels:

  • Organisation chart: the macro level, showing the reporting lines and lateral interactions between departments and positions
  • Job description: the individual level, clearly listing the scope of responsibilities and decision-making authority of a specific position
  • Deputising arrangements: the continuity level, ensuring that key positions do not leave a gap in operations when the post-holder is absent

Analogy

The relationship between staffing and workload is similar to restaurant shift scheduling: the lunch peak needs 5 servers, but 1 is enough just before closing at night. GDP gives no fixed headcount; instead it requires the company to assess for itself based on the "volume" and "scope" of activities. During an audit, inspectors compare your average daily order volume, warehouse area, proportion of special products and actual headcount to judge whether staffing is reasonable.

Key Notes

Deputising arrangements are particularly important: GDP explicitly requires deputising arrangements to be recorded in the job description. In practice, this means that when the warehouse manager is on a business trip or on leave, a clearly designated deputy must be in place, and that deputy must be competent enough to carry out critical tasks (such as release decisions and deviation handling). If a key person happens to be absent during an inspection with no clearly designated deputy, this in itself is an observation.

2.3 Designation of Responsibilities

Original Text

2.3 DESIGNATION OF RESPONSIBILITIES

2.3.1 The wholesale distributor must designate personnel responsible for GDP compliance. Relevant personnel should have appropriate competence and experience as well as knowledge of and training in GDP.

2.3.2 Wholesale distributors should nominate personnel for out of hours contact (e.g. emergencies and/or recall). Designated responsible person(s) may delegate duties but not responsibilities.

2.3.3 Written job descriptions for designated responsible person(s) should define their authority to take decisions with regard to their responsibilities. The wholesale distributor should give the designated responsible person(s) the defined authority, adequate resources and responsibility needed to fulfil their duties.

2.3.4 Designated responsible person(s) should carry out their duties in such a way as to ensure that the wholesale distributor can demonstrate GDP compliance and that public service obligations are met.

2.3.5 The responsibilities of the designated responsible person(s) include but are not limited to:

  1. ensuring that a quality management system is implemented and maintained;
  2. focusing on the management of authorised activities and the accuracy and quality of records;
  3. ensuring that initial and continuous training programmes are implemented and maintained;
  4. coordinating and promptly performing any recall operations for medicinal products;
  5. ensuring that relevant customer complaints are dealt with effectively;
  6. ensuring that suppliers and customers are approved;
  7. approving any subcontracted activities which may impact on GDP;
  8. ensuring that self-inspections are performed at appropriate regular intervals following a prearranged programme and necessary corrective measures are put in place;
  9. keeping appropriate records of any delegated duties;
  10. deciding on the final disposition of returned, rejected, recalled or falsified products;
  11. approving any returns to saleable stock;
  12. ensuring that any additional requirements imposed on certain products by national legislation are adhered to.

Tutorial Commentary

Key concept: the regulatory status of the Designated Responsible Person

In regulatory standing, the "Designated Responsible Person" (DRP) in GDP is equivalent to the "Qualified Person (QP)" in the GMP environment: the legal and responsibility core of the entire distribution quality system.

Key principle: duties may be delegated, but responsibilities may not.

Practical meaning: the DRP can assign a warehouse operator to carry out temperature recording, but when the temperature goes out of specification, the responsibility for deciding the final disposition of the product remains with the DRP. The DRP is not relieved of responsibility because the task has been delegated to someone else.

Analogy

The DRP's role is like that of the CEO of a hospital:

  • The CEO can entrust department heads with the day-to-day management of their departments (delegation of duties)
  • But when a medical incident occurs, the CEO remains ultimately accountable (responsibility cannot be transferred)
  • Must be contactable 24 hours a day (out of hours contact)
  • Needs adequate resources and authority to genuinely fulfil the role

Note the requirement of 2.3.3: the company must give the DRP "defined authority" and "adequate resources". If the DRP exists in name only, regulatory inspectors will regard this as a systemic deficiency.

Key Notes

"Out of Hours Contact": GDP explicitly requires the company to designate a person responsible for out-of-hours contact for emergencies and recalls. In practice, this means establishing an on-call rota, and including an emergency contact list (with mobile phone numbers) in an SOP and keeping it up to date. Inspectors will ask to see this list and confirm that it is current.

Returns to saleable stock is a high-risk step: Responsibility xi is particularly important. Before returned products re-enter saleable stock, they must be assessed and approved by the DRP, and the assessment should include confirmation of storage conditions, visual inspection, cold chain integrity and so on (see GDP Chapter 7 for details).

  2. When a batch of refrigerated product (2-8°C) is returned by a customer, what information does the DRP need to confirm before deciding whether it can be returned to saleable stock?
  3. In the CDMO context, what assessment aspects does "approving suppliers" (responsibility vi) usually include?
2.4 Training

Original Text

2.4 TRAINING

2.4.1 All personnel involved in wholesale distribution activities should be trained on the requirements of GDP. They should have the appropriate competence and experience prior to commencing their tasks.

2.4.2 Personnel should receive initial and continuing training relevant to their role, based on written procedures and in accordance with a written training programme. Designated responsible person(s) should also maintain their competence in GDP through regular training.

2.4.3 In addition, training should include aspects of product identification and avoidance of falsified medicines entering the supply chain.

2.4.4 Personnel dealing with any products which require more stringent handling conditions should receive specific training. Examples of such products include hazardous products, radioactive materials, products presenting special risks of abuse (including narcotic and psychotropic substances), and temperature-sensitive products.

2.4.5 A record of all training should be kept, and the effectiveness of training should be periodically assessed and documented.

Tutorial Commentary

Key concept: three layers of GDP training

GDP 2.4 establishes a three-layer training structure:

  • Foundation layer (applies to everyone): awareness of GDP requirements and identification of falsified medicines
  • Role layer (tailored to the position): initial training plus continuing training, carried out according to written SOPs and a written training programme
  • Special product layer (according to product characteristics): specific training for cold chain, narcotic and controlled drugs, radioactive materials and so on

Note the timing requirement of 2.4.1: competence and experience must be in place "prior to commencing their tasks". This means that training for new employees during the probationary period must be completed before they take on GDP-critical activities; they cannot learn on the job while performing those activities.

Analogy

Assessment of training effectiveness is like a student's examination:

  • The teacher has taught (training has been delivered) does not equal the student has learned (competence has been established)
  • GDP requires not only a "training attendance sheet" but also "assessment results": test scores, practical assessments, observation records and so on
  • If the assessment finds the training ineffective, the follow-up remedial actions must also be recorded

One of the questions inspectors ask most often: "How do you know the training is effective?" An attendance sheet alone cannot answer it.

Key Notes

Training in identifying falsified medicines is mandatory: 2.4.3 explicitly names training on the identification of falsified medicines. This is linked to the background of the EU Falsified Medicines Directive (FMD). In practice, warehouse operators must be able to recognise the characteristics of suspect products (abnormal packaging, serial numbers that cannot be verified, unknown origin and so on) and know how to initiate the abnormal-event handling procedure.

The DRP also needs continuing training: 2.4.2 explicitly requires the DRP to maintain GDP competence through regular training. This means the DRP cannot take GDP training once and then be exempt from training permanently. After regulatory updates and SOP revisions, the DRP also needs to be retrained, and records must be kept.

The importance of special product training in a CDMO setting: If a CDMO handles ambient, refrigerated (2-8°C) and frozen (-20°C or -80°C) products at the same time, operators in each temperature zone need cold chain training specific to that zone, and training records must be linked to the temperature requirements.

Practical Application

Scenario: Your company has added a distribution business for ultra-low-temperature (-80°C) frozen products.

  1. Under GDP 2.4, which training modules need to be planned for which positions?
  2. How would you design the training effectiveness assessment (what are the pass criteria)?
  3. If a warehouse operator fails the training assessment, how should the next steps be recorded and handled?
2.5 Hygiene

Original Text

2.5 HYGIENE

Appropriate procedures relating to personnel hygiene, relevant to the activities being carried out, should be established and observed. Such procedures should cover health, hygiene and clothing.

Tutorial Commentary

Key concept: hygiene requirements in the distribution environment

The hygiene requirements of GDP 2.5 are relatively concise, but they must be "appropriate" and "relevant to the activities" being performed. This means hygiene standards should be set according to the actual operating environment:

  • Health: management of health conditions, such as infectious diseases and skin conditions, that affect product integrity
  • Hygiene: basic requirements such as hand hygiene and personal cleanliness
  • Clothing: specification of work clothing, frequency of changing, and laundering control

Although the hygiene requirements of a distribution environment (warehouse) are less strict than those of a manufacturing cleanroom, contamination risk must still be prevented, especially when opening packaging or handling damaged goods.

Analogy

Hygiene management in a distribution warehouse is similar to staff management in a supermarket's fresh food section:

  • Employees with wounds or infectious diseases must report them and be temporarily reassigned (health management)
  • Hands must be washed before handling fresh food (hygiene procedures)
  • Wear the prescribed work clothing, and do not eat or drink in the work area (clothing and behaviour rules)

Medicinal products carry higher quality requirements than fresh food, so the documentation and enforcement discipline of hygiene procedures are correspondingly stricter.

Key Notes

"Procedures should be established and observed": These two verbs are equally important. Inspectors do not only check whether an SOP exists; they also verify through on-site observation, training records and non-conformance records whether the SOP is actually followed. From a regulatory standpoint, a hygiene SOP that nobody follows is worse than having no SOP, because it indicates a systemic failure of control.

Practical scope of hygiene SOPs: They should include the handling of emergencies (for example, the procedure when a warehouse operator is injured and bleeding during work, and the segregation and assessment of medicinal product packaging that may have come into contact with blood).

Practice Questions

  1. In a CDMO warehouse environment, what specific situations should a hygiene SOP cover at a minimum? List three.
  2. If a warehouse operator finds during work that they have touched a possibly contaminated surface, how does the existing hygiene procedure guide their next actions?

Chapter Summary

  • People are the core of GDP: Distribution quality is ultimately determined by people's competence and sense of responsibility. The regulation requires all personnel to be competent, and responsibilities must be clearly recorded
  • Documented organisational structure: The organisation chart plus job descriptions (including deputising arrangements) are the basic documentation requirements in a GDP inspection
  • The Designated Responsible Person (DRP) bears 12 core responsibilities; duties may be delegated but responsibilities may not, and a 24-hour emergency contact arrangement is required
  • Three-layer training structure: basic GDP knowledge (including identification of falsified medicines) → role-specific training → special product training (cold chain, controlled drugs and so on); training effectiveness must be assessed and recorded
  • Hygiene procedures cover three aspects: health, hygiene and clothing; they must be established and actually followed, and inspectors will verify this on site

3.1 — Principle

Original Text

3.1 PRINCIPLE

Wholesale distributors must have suitable and adequate premises, installations and equipment, so as to ensure proper storage and distribution of medicinal products. In particular, the premises should be clean, dry and maintained within acceptable temperature limits.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Three Basic Attributes of Premises

GDP 3.1 (Principle) sets three basic preconditions:

  • Clean: prevents cross-contamination and pests, and protects the integrity of product packaging
  • Dry: prevents moisture from affecting product stability (especially powder formulations and hygroscopic products)
  • Acceptable temperature limits: ensures products are kept under their labelled storage conditions; this is the most fundamental quality safeguard in medicinal product distribution

"Suitable and adequate" is a phrase that appears very often in GDP. It means the premises must satisfy two aspects at once: suitability in nature (technical specifications meet product requirements) and adequacy in quantity (space and capacity can support actual operational needs).

Analogy

A medicines warehouse is like a precise life-support system:

  • Temperature out of control = lack of oxygen (a direct threat to product shelf life and safety)
  • Humidity = too little or too much water (affects stability)
  • Lack of cleanliness = a source of infection (contamination risk)

None of the three conditions can be dropped. Even if the temperature is perfectly right, a leaking warehouse that dampens the packaging can still cause product quality problems and trigger a GDP non-compliance.

Key Notes

The function of the "Principle" in the GDP structure: The Principle at the start of each GDP chapter acts as that chapter's "statement of legislative purpose". When assessing a specific deficiency, inspectors refer back to the Principle to judge its seriousness. Understanding the core intent of 3.1 helps you make system design decisions that respect the spirit of the regulation.

3.3 — Temperature and Environment Control

Original Text

3.3 TEMPERATURE AND ENVIRONMENT CONTROL

3.3.1 Suitable equipment and procedures should be in place to check the environment where medicinal products are stored. Environmental factors to be considered include temperature, light, humidity and cleanliness of the premises.

3.3.2 An initial temperature mapping exercise should be carried out on the storage area before use, under representative conditions. Temperature monitoring equipment should be located according to the results of the mapping exercise, ensuring that monitoring devices are positioned in the areas that experience the extremes of fluctuations. The mapping exercise should be repeated for significant changes according to the results of a risk assessment exercise. For small premises of a few square meters which are at room temperature, an assessment of potential risks (e.g. heater / air-conditioner) should be conducted and temperature monitors placed accordingly.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Logic of Temperature Mapping

Temperature mapping (T-mapping) is one of the most important qualification activities in GDP warehouse management. The core logic is:

  1. Find the temperature extremes: Temperature is not evenly distributed across a warehouse; positions near doors, air-conditioning outlets, the floor and the ceiling can differ significantly
  2. Position monitors according to the results: Monitoring points must be placed where the limits are most likely to be exceeded, not where measurement is easiest
  3. Representative conditions: Mapping must be performed under conditions that simulate actual operations (for example summer, a fully loaded warehouse, doors opened frequently), not in an ideal empty-warehouse state

Triggers for re-mapping (significant changes): These normally include extensions, renovations, replacement of the air-conditioning system, changes to the maximum stock level, and upgrades of the monitoring system. Whether re-mapping is triggered should be based on a risk assessment, and the conclusion should be recorded.

Analogy

Temperature mapping is like looking for the hot and cold spots in an office:

  • Directly under the air-conditioning outlet = coldest area (product may be overcooled)
  • By a window in direct sunlight = hottest area (the upper limit may be exceeded)
  • The central aisle = relatively stable area

You would not put the thermometer in the most comfortable spot; you put it in the most extreme spot, so that you know whether the warehouse still complies under the worst conditions. Of the four monitored factors (temperature, light, humidity and cleanliness), temperature is the most critical, but the other three cannot be ignored for special products (for example light-sensitive injectables).

Key Notes

How inspectors read "representative conditions": Inspectors will review the description of the execution conditions in the temperature mapping report. If the mapping was done in winter, with an empty warehouse and doors and windows closed, while actual operations run at full shipping volume in the peak summer heat, inspectors may consider the results unrepresentative and ask for the study to be repeated or supplemented with worst-case testing.

Flexibility for small ambient warehouses: The last sentence of GDP 3.3.2 gives small ambient warehouses a practical alternative: full mapping is not mandatory, but a risk assessment must be performed and the location of monitoring points decided on its outcome. The assessment itself must be documented.

Procedures should be in place to ensure the integrity of medicinal products are maintained in the event of equipment failure.

3.4.5 Adequate records of repair, maintenance and calibration activities for key equipment should be made and the results should be retained. Key equipment would include for example cold stores, monitored intruder alarm and access control systems, refrigerators, thermo hygrometers, or other temperature and humidity recording devices, air handling units and any equipment used in conjunction with the onward supply chain.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Management Elements for GDP Key Equipment

The GDP 3.4 requirements for key equipment can be broken down into four dimensions:

  • Design/location: Equipment specifications and placement must suit the intended use
  • Maintenance: A planned (preventive) maintenance programme, including routine servicing and parts replacement
  • Cleaning: Cleaning standards should be documented, and cleaning must not become a source of contamination
  • Calibration: The calibration interval is determined on the basis of a risk and reliability assessment, and calibration must be traceable to national or international standards

3.4.5 lists specific examples of "key equipment". In a CDMO distribution environment in particular, a list should be established and each item managed individually.

Analogy

The management logic of the alarm system (3.4.3) is similar to a smoke detector at home:

  • Installed but never tested = you will not know when the battery is dead, and you discover the failure only in an emergency
  • Threshold set too sensitive = a false alarm every time the refrigerator is opened, and staff learn to ignore it
  • Threshold set too loose = the warning comes only after the product is already far out of limits, which is too late

GDP requires alarm limits to be "appropriately set" and "regularly tested". One of the most common inspection findings is that the alarm settings were never formally assessed, or that alarm test records are missing.

Key Notes

The regulatory meaning of calibration traceability: A calibration report must show the calibration chain: from your instrument, to the reference standard of the calibration laboratory, and on to the national metrology standard (for example, TAF accreditation of a laboratory recognised by BSMI). "Not knowing whether the calibration laboratory holds TAF accreditation" is a common finding in GDP inspections.

Equipment failure contingency procedure: 3.4.4 requires a procedure to ensure that product integrity is not compromised when equipment fails. In practice, the contingency procedure for cold chain equipment should include: (1) the failure notification process, (2) the steps for starting up backup equipment, (3) the decision process and time limits for transferring product, and (4) the procedure for recording and evaluating temperature deviations. This must be a written SOP prepared in advance, not something drafted ad hoc after a failure occurs.

Calibration intervals based on risk assessment: GDP 3.4.2 deliberately does not prescribe a fixed calibration frequency (such as once a year), and instead requires the company to decide on the basis of a "risk and reliability assessment". This means high-risk equipment (such as the only temperature sensor in a cold room) may need a shorter calibration interval, and the decision must be documented.

  2. How should the calibration interval for a cold room temperature recorder be determined through risk assessment? Which factors would shorten the calibration cycle?

3.5 — Computerised Systems

Original Text

3.5 COMPUTERISED SYSTEMS

3.5.1 Before a computerised system is brought into use, it should be demonstrated, through appropriate validation or verification studies, that the system is capable of achieving the desired results accurately, consistently and reproducibly.

3.5.2 A written, detailed description of the system should be available (including diagrams where appropriate). This should be kept up to date. The document should describe principles, objectives, security measures, system scope and main features, how the computerised system is used and the way it interacts with other systems.

3.5.3 Data should only be entered into the computerised system or amended by persons authorised to do so.

3.5.4 Data should be secured by physical or electronic means and protected against accidental or unauthorised modifications. Stored data should be checked periodically for accessibility. Data should be protected by backing up at regular intervals. Backup data should be retained for the period stated in national legislation but at least 5 years at a separate and secure location.

3.5.5 Procedures to be followed if the system fails or breaks down should be defined. This should include systems for the restoration of data.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Five Key Requirements for GDP Computerised Systems

GDP 3.5 sets five core requirements for computerised systems (including WMS, ERP and temperature monitoring software):

  1. Validation/verification before go-live: There must be documented evidence that the system achieves its intended function accurately, consistently and reproducibly
  2. System description: Written, detailed and kept up to date, covering security measures, system scope and interfaces
  3. Access control: Data may be entered or modified only by authorised persons
  4. Data protection and backup: Protection against accidental or unauthorised modification; regular backups; backup data kept for at least 5 years in a separate, secure location
  5. Failure contingency procedure: Operating procedures for system failure, including data recovery mechanisms

Analogy

The "off-site" requirement for backups (3.5.4) is similar to a bank's off-site recovery arrangement:

  • If the backup is kept in the same building as the main system = a fire destroys both at the same time
  • Separate location = a physically separate place (a different floor is not enough; it should be a different building or the cloud)
  • Secure location = a backup environment with access control that prevents unauthorised access

For a CDMO, the "5 years" retention requirement means the backup policy must be written into the information management SOP, and the accessibility of backup data must be verified regularly. A backup that cannot be restored is the same as no backup.

Key Notes

The difference between "validation" and "verification": GDP 3.5.1 mentions both terms, which reflects the pragmatic attitude of EU pharmaceutical law towards systems of different scale. Commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) software is usually subjected to "verification" rather than full "validation", with the focus on confirming that the configuration and use of the software meet the intended function. In-house developed or highly customised systems normally require full IQ/OQ/PQ validation.

Data integrity is at the heart of 21 CFR Part 11: The requirements of GDP 3.5.3 and 3.5.4 overlap heavily with the electronic records and electronic signatures requirements of 21 CFR Part 11 (in Taiwan, the relevant TFDA provisions). Access control, audit trails and records of data changes are the areas most often focused on in inspections.

Practical Application

  2. The electronic segregation function must meet the "equivalent security" requirement of 3.2.3. How would test cases for this be designed in the validation plan?
  3. Does cloud backup satisfy the "separate and secure location" requirement of 3.5.4? Which contract terms need to be confirmed?
3.6 — Qualification and Validation

Original Text

3.6 QUALIFICATION AND VALIDATION

3.6.1 Wholesale distributors should identify what key equipment qualification and/or key process validation is necessary to ensure correct installation and operation. The scope and extent of such qualification and/or validation activities (such as storage, pick and pack processes, transportation) should be determined using a documented risk assessment approach.

3.6.2 Equipment and processes should be respectively qualified and/or validated before commencing use and after any significant changes (e.g. repair or maintenance).

3.6.3 Validation and qualification reports should be prepared summarising the results obtained and commenting on any observed deviations. Deviations from established procedures should be documented and further actions decided to correct deviations and avoid their reoccurrence (corrective and preventive actions). The principles of CAPA should be applied where necessary. Evidence of satisfactory validation and acceptance of a process or piece of equipment should be produced and approved by appropriate personnel.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Scope of GDP Qualification/Validation

GDP 3.6 brings the qualification/validation concepts familiar from the GMP manufacturing environment into distribution. The scope includes:

  • Storage: Qualification of cold rooms, freezers and ambient warehouses (IQ/OQ/PQ)
  • Pick and pack: Ensuring the accuracy and consistency of the picking process
  • Transportation: Validation of cold chain transport (for example the temperature-holding capability of insulated shippers)

Key principle: The scope and depth of qualification/validation are determined by a documented risk assessment; not all equipment needs the same depth of qualification. The RP should lead this risk assessment decision.

Application of CAPA in GDP Reports

GDP 3.6.3 requires that a validation/qualification report must:

  • Record all deviations observed
  • Propose follow-up actions for the deviations (corrective and preventive actions, CAPA)
  • Be reviewed and approved for acceptance by "appropriate personnel"

In a GDP environment, "appropriate personnel" normally means the RP or their authorised delegate. This approval step is itself an auditable record point.

Analogy

Validation/qualification is like a safety inspection before a new car goes on the road:

  • Before commencing use: The PDI (pre-delivery inspection) before a new car leaves the factory confirms that all functions work properly
  • Re-qualification after significant changes: After major accident repairs the car must be inspected again; likewise, a cold room needs re-qualification after a major overhaul
  • Deviations do not mean failure: The inspection finds the braking distance slightly long, so it is adjusted and retested, and the CAPA loop is closed. GDP also accepts a validation conclusion of "deviations found but CAPA completed"; what matters is that the process is recorded honestly

Key Notes

"Re-qualification after repair" is a commonly overlooked item: GDP 3.6.2 explicitly lists "repair or maintenance" as one of the significant changes that trigger re-qualification. In practice, after the compressor or temperature controller of a cold room is replaced, you must assess whether OQ/PQ needs to be repeated, even if the equipment specification appears identical. This assessment must also be documented.

Letting risk assessment determine the scope is where GDP is more pragmatic than GMP: GDP does not require full IQ/OQ/PQ for all distribution equipment; it allows companies to decide through risk assessment which equipment needs qualification and to what depth. This gives a CDMO some flexibility, but it also demands that decisions be transparent and traceable.

Practice Questions

  1. A newly purchased thermal shipper is to be used for last-mile delivery of 2-8°C products. How does GDP 3.6 require it to be validated? Which test conditions (worst case) should the validation protocol include?
  2. The company's existing DQ/IQ/OQ/PQ document structure was built for GMP manufacturing equipment. What adjustments does this structure need for GDP warehouse qualification?
  3. If a validation report shows that one corner of the cold room reaches 9°C under worst-case conditions (exceeding the 8°C upper limit), does this count as a "validation failure"? How should it be handled?

Chapter Summary

  • Basic requirements for premises: Clean, dry and temperature-controlled are three non-negotiable conditions; "suitable and adequate" covers both quality suitability and sufficient capacity
  • Physical segregation is mandatory: Falsified, expired, recalled, rejected and unauthorised products must be physically segregated, and electronic segregation cannot replace this; it is one of the most rigid compliance requirements in GDP
  • Temperature mapping is the basis for selecting monitoring points: It must be performed under representative conditions, with monitors placed at the temperature extremes; after significant changes, assess whether re-mapping is needed
  • The four elements of equipment management: design/location + preventive maintenance + cleaning + calibration (traceable to national standards); alarm thresholds should be appropriately set and regularly tested
  • Computerised systems require validation before go-live, with a system description, access control and tamper-proof data protection; backup data must be kept for at least 5 years in a separate, secure location, and a failure contingency procedure must be in place
  • The scope of qualification and validation is determined by risk assessment and covers distribution processes such as storage, pick and pack, and transportation; after significant changes (including repair), assess whether re-qualification is needed; all deviations must be closed out through CAPA and approved by appropriate personnel

4.1 Principle & 4.2.1–4.2.4 Documentation Basics

Original Text

4.1 PRINCIPLE

Good documentation constitutes an essential part of the quality system. Written documentation should prevent errors from spoken communication and permits the tracking of relevant operations during the distribution of medicinal products. Records should be made at the time each operation is undertaken.

4.2.1 Documentation comprises all written procedures, instructions, contracts, records and data, in paper or in electronic form. Documentation should be readily available/retrievable.

4.2.2 With regard to the processing of personal data of employees, complainants or any other natural person, national legislation on the protection of individuals applies to the processing of personal data and to the free movement of such data.

4.2.3 Documentation should be sufficiently comprehensive with respect to the scope of the wholesale distributor's activities and in a language understood by personnel. It should be written in clear, unambiguous language and be free from errors.

4.2.4 Documentation should be approved, signed and dated by designated persons, as required. It should not be handwritten; although, where it is necessary, sufficient space should be provided for such entries.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Three Functions of Documentation

The GDP documentation system serves three core functions:

  • Prevention: Written instructions replace verbal ones, eliminating errors caused by miscommunication. Medicinal product distribution involves many links (supplier, warehouse, transport, customer), and every handover point is a potential communication risk.
  • Traceability: Every operation is recorded, so when a quality event occurs the whole distribution process can be "rewound" and reconstructed. This is the foundation of recall operations.
  • Accountability: Documents must be approved and signed by designated persons, so that every decision has a clearly assigned owner.

Analogy: The Airline Flight Log

Think of an airline flight log: every action on every flight must be recorded in real time (takeoff time, weather, abnormal events, fuel consumption). If entries are added afterwards, the details may already be distorted.

The GDP principle of contemporaneous recording (records should be made at the time each operation is undertaken) is exactly the same: entries cannot be written up later, because memory embellishes or blurs what really happened. This is also one of the issues auditors care about most during GMP inspections.

Key Point: Protection of Personal Data (4.2.2)

In warehouse operations at Taiwan CDMOs, this clause is often overlooked. Documents containing personal data include employee operation records (with names), complaint records (with complainant details) and supplier contact information.

Under Taiwan's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA), such data must be collected for a clear purpose, and the retention period should not exceed what is necessary. GDP specifically states that once the purpose of storage no longer exists, personal data should be "deleted or anonymised". Confirm the compliance measures with your legal and compliance department.

  2. Documents should "not be handwritten", yet "sufficient space should be provided" where entries are required. These two requirements seem contradictory; how would you balance them in practical document design? (Hint: which fields are usually filled in by hand?)
  3. 4.2.3 requires documents to use language "understood by personnel". In multilingual warehouse operations in Taiwan (local staff plus foreign workers), what does this mean for SOP writing?
4.2.5–4.2.8 Document Change Control & Version Management

Original Text

4.2.5 Any alteration made in the documentation should be signed and dated; the alteration should permit the reading of the original information. Where appropriate, the reason for the alteration should be recorded.

4.2.6 Documents should be retained for the period stated in national legislation but at least 5 years. Personal data should be deleted or anonymised as soon as their storage is no longer necessary for the purpose of distribution activities.

4.2.7 Each employee should have ready access to all necessary documentation for the tasks executed.

4.2.8 Attention should be paid to using valid and approved procedures. Documents should have unambiguous content; title, nature and purpose should be clearly stated. Documents should be reviewed regularly and kept up to date. Version control should be applied to procedures. After revision of a document a system should exist to prevent inadvertent use of the superseded version. Superseded or obsolete procedures should be removed from workstations and archived.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The "Single-Line Strike-Through" Principle for Document Amendments

The core requirement of 4.2.5 is that an amendment must not obscure the original information. The correct practice is to strike through the original entry with a single line, then sign and date next to it and, where necessary, state the reason.

Common non-compliant practices:

  • Covering the original entry with correction fluid: non-compliant, the original is no longer legible
  • Blacking out the original entry with a black marker: non-compliant, the original is no longer legible
  • Overwriting with double lines or scribbles: non-compliant, the original is no longer legible

Correct practice: strike through with a single line so the original remains legible, sign and date alongside, and for complex changes add an explanation of the reason.

Analogy: Notarised Amendments to Legal Documents

Imagine a legal contract: if you need to change an amount, you cannot black it out. You cross it out, write the correct figure beside it, and both parties initial the change to confirm it.

The logic for GDP document amendments is exactly the same: the "struck-through original" is like a "deleted but still legible old clause", and it proves that the integrity of the document has never been tampered with. This is especially important when responding to audits or legal disputes.

Key Point: The Six Elements of Version Control (4.2.8)

4.2.8 contains a complete version control cycle, and all six elements are indispensable:

  1. Clear title and purpose (Title & Purpose): staff know what a document is as soon as they pick it up
  2. Regular review (Regular Review): how often is a document reviewed? This must be defined in an SOP
  3. Version number management (Version Control): for example v1.0, v1.1, v2.0
  4. Preventing use of superseded versions (Prevent superseded use): a system mechanism is needed, not just reliance on people's memory
  5. Retrieval of old versions (Remove from workstations): no obsolete versions may remain on the floor
  6. Archiving of old versions (Archive superseded): kept for historical traceability, but clearly marked "superseded"

Practical Application: GDP-Compliant Design of an Electronic Document Management System (EDMS)

In warehouses at Taiwan CDMOs, many companies use electronic SOP systems (such as Veeva Vault, Master Control or in-house systems). According to 4.2.8, the system must provide:

  • When a new version is released, the old version is locked automatically (cannot be printed or accessed), so that floor staff can only see the effective version
  • A version history archive, so that auditors can review historical versions at any time
  • Compliance with 21 CFR Part 11 (electronic records/signatures), ensuring electronic signatures are legally valid
  • Role-based access, ensuring that, as 4.2.7 requires, each employee can access the documents needed for their tasks

4.2.9 Mandatory Transaction Record Fields

Original Text

4.2.9 Records must be kept either in the form of purchase/sales invoices, delivery slips, or on computer or any other form, for any transaction in medicinal products received or supplied. Records must include at least the following information:

  • date
  • name of the medicinal product
  • quantity received, supplied
  • name and address of the supplier, customer, or consignee, as appropriate
  • batch number, expiry date, as required by national legislation

Records are made contemporaneously and if handwritten, in clear, legible and indelible handwriting.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Minimum Field List for Transaction Records

4.2.9 clearly defines six required fields. They map directly to what recall operations need:

  • Date: establishes the timeline and confirms the distribution window of the affected batch
  • Name of medicinal product: identifies the affected product
  • Quantity: confirms the scale of distribution of the affected batch
  • Name and address of supplier/customer/consignee: defines the notification list for a recall
  • Batch number: precisely identifies the batches to be recalled
  • Expiry date: helps confirm the scope of the problem

If any one of these fields is missing, the recall will have blind spots, and it cannot be confirmed whether all affected product has been retrieved.

Analogy: Reverse Tracing in a Supermarket Barcode System

After the 2011 plasticizer incident in Taiwan, the food authority required all retail channels to remove every affected item from sale within 24 hours. This was possible because supermarket receiving systems recorded which lot arrived, when it was received and which store it was sold to.

GDP transaction records for medicinal products work in exactly the same way. The batch number is the key link: it is the "fingerprint" of a batch, letting the whole supply chain trace the product step by step from manufacturer to wholesaler to hospital, with a record available at every link.

Key Point: The Legal Implications of "Contemporaneously"

The last sentence of 4.2.9 is extremely important: records must be made contemporaneously, not written up afterwards.

In audits, documents that were back-filled can often be identified: ink colour, fluency of handwriting (handwriting under stress differs from normal) and metadata of electronic records (such as modification timestamps) are common ways to detect it.

Handwritten records have three further requirements: they must be clear, legible and indelible. Pencil or erasable ink pens are not acceptable; blue or black ink pens (indelible) must be used.

Practical Application: Electronic Record Design for a CDMO Warehouse

In goods receipt and dispatch at a sterile CDMO, the "transaction records" of 4.2.9 are usually generated automatically by the WMS (Warehouse Management System). Key design points:

  • The WMS should automatically record a timestamp when materials are scanned in or out, ensuring the "contemporaneously" requirement is met
  • Lot number and expiry date must be mandatory entries and cannot be left blank
  • Supplier addresses should be linked to the Approved Vendor List master data to avoid manual entry errors
  • Electronic records must meet the requirements of 21 CFR Part 11 (if exporting to the US market) or PIC/S Annex 11 (if aligning with EU regulations)

  1. 4.2.9 says "as required by national legislation", which means batch number and expiry date are not mandatory in every country. From a medicinal product safety perspective, in which situations do you consider these two fields absolutely necessary?
  2. If your company is designing a new WMS, which mandatory fields in the WMS correspond to the six required fields of 4.2.9? Are there fields that GDP does not require but you would recommend adding?

  • Documentation is the backbone of the quality system: written documents prevent verbal errors, trace all distribution operations and provide the basis for accountability (4.1).
  • Four approval elements for documents (4.2.4): approved, signed, dated, and performed by designated persons.
  • Amendment rule (4.2.5): strike through with a single line, keep the original legible, sign and date alongside, and state the reason where necessary. Correction fluid and blacking out are prohibited.
  • Retention period (4.2.6): per national legislation, but at least 5 years; personal data must be deleted or anonymised as soon as it is no longer necessary.
  • Version control (4.2.8): the system must prevent superseded versions from being used by mistake, obsolete versions must not remain on the floor, and old versions are archived.
  • Six elements of transaction records (4.2.9): date / product name / quantity / supplier or customer details / batch number / expiry date; records must be made contemporaneously.

5.1 Principle

Original Text

5.1 PRINCIPLE

All actions taken by wholesale distributors should ensure that the identity of the medicinal product is not lost and that the wholesale distribution of medicinal products is performed according to the information on the outer packaging. The wholesale distributor should use all means available to minimise the risk of falsified medicinal products entering the legal supply chain.

All medicinal products distributed in the intended market by a wholesale distributor must be appropriately authorised by the national authorities. All key operations described below should be fully described in the quality system in appropriate documentation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Two Missions of GDP Operations

5.1 Principle defines the two core missions of the whole of Chapter 5:

  • Identity preservation: At every step from manufacturer to final user, the identity of the product (name, strength, batch number) must not be lost or confused. This involves label control, shelf management, and picking accuracy.
  • Prevention of falsified products: PE 011-1 requires wholesalers to use "all means available" to prevent falsified medicines from entering the legal supply chain. This is not a recommendation but a legal obligation.

All the specific requirements in 5.2 to 5.9 are means of achieving these two missions.

Analogy: The "Chain of Trust" in the Medicines Supply Chain

The medicines supply chain is like a chain of trust. The manufacturer gives each batch its "legal identity" (marketing authorisation, batch number, CoA), and the wholesaler's duty is to make sure that this identity is not altered or replaced anywhere along the chain.

Falsified medicines are dangerous precisely because they "disguise themselves" as legitimate products and slip in through a weakness at some point in the supply chain. In essence, every GDP operational requirement serves to reinforce each link of this chain of trust.

Key Point: The Legal Status of "Information on the Outer Packaging"

"Distribute according to the information on the outer packaging" means that the wholesaler must not change, on its own judgement, any information relating to the product description. If the storage condition on the outer packaging is "2–8°C", the wholesaler must ensure that this temperature range is maintained throughout distribution, and may not claim an exemption on the grounds that "the distance is short and the time is brief".

This principle links directly to the storage management and cold chain management requirements in 5.5.

5.2 Supplier Qualification & 5.3 Customer Qualification | Supplier & Customer Qualification

Original Text

5.2 QUALIFICATION OF SUPPLIERS

5.2.1 Wholesale distributors must obtain their supplies of medicinal products only from persons who are themselves in possession of a wholesale distribution authorisation, or who are in possession of a manufacturing authorisation which covers the product in question.

5.2.2 Where medicinal products are obtained from another wholesale distributor the receiving wholesale distributor must verify that the supplier complies with the principles and guidelines of good distribution practices and that they hold a licence.

5.2.3 Appropriate qualification and approval of suppliers should be performed prior to procurement of any medicinal products. This should be controlled by a procedure and the results documented and periodically rechecked using a risk based approach.

5.2.4 When entering into a new contract with new suppliers the wholesale distributor should carry out 'due diligence' checks in order to assess the suitability, competence and reliability of the other party. Attention should be paid to:

  1. the reputation or reliability of the supplier;
  2. offers of medicinal products more likely to be falsified;
  3. large offers of medicinal products which are generally only available in limited quantities;
  4. diversity of products handled by supplier;
  5. and out-of-range prices.

5.3 QUALIFICATION OF CUSTOMERS

5.3.1 Wholesale distributors must ensure they supply medicinal products only to persons who are themselves in possession of a wholesale distribution authorisation or who are authorised or entitled to supply medicinal products to the public or otherwise authorised to procure medicinal products from a distributor.

5.3.2 Checks and periodic rechecks may include: requesting copies of customer's authorisations, verifying status on an authority website, requesting evidence of qualifications or entitlement according to national legislation.

5.3.3 Wholesale distributors should monitor their transactions and investigate any irregularity in the sales patterns of medicinal products at risk of diversion (e.g. narcotics, psychotropic substances). Unusual sales patterns that may constitute diversion or misuse of medicinal product should be investigated and reported to competent authorities where necessary.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Two Types of Legitimate Supplier

5.2.1 expressly limits "legitimate suppliers" to two types:

  • Holders of a Wholesale Distribution Authorisation: wholesalers recognised by the competent authority as lawfully entitled to conduct wholesale distribution of medicines. In Taiwan, this corresponds to a pharmaceutical firm licence (pharmaceutical wholesaler).
  • Holders of a Manufacturing Authorisation: a manufacturer may supply products within the scope of its authorisation directly, without also holding a wholesale authorisation. However, the scope of the authorisation must cover the specific product being purchased.

Important: Any other channel (for example individuals, unlicensed intermediaries, or online suppliers) does not meet the requirements of 5.2.1, even if the price is more competitive.

Analogy: Bank KYC (Know Your Customer)

When opening an account, a bank must perform KYC (Know Your Customer): verify the customer's identity and assess money-laundering risk. If a customer suddenly makes many small cash transactions, the system automatically flags them as "suspicious transactions" and reports them.

Supplier and customer qualification under GDP follows exactly the same logic:

  • 5.2 = the bank vetting "who may deposit money" (supplier qualification)
  • 5.3 = the bank vetting "who may withdraw money" (customer qualification)
  • 5.3.3 = the bank's suspicious transaction monitoring system (monitoring of unusual sales patterns)

Financial regulation and medicines regulation are strikingly similar in this logic: both aim to stop illegal activity from exploiting a legitimate system.

Key Point: The "Periodic Recheck" Cannot Be a One-Time Exercise

Both 5.2.3 and 5.3.2 mention "periodic recheck". This means that supplier and customer qualification is not a one-time activity; a mechanism for periodic, ongoing confirmation must be established.

The following situations should trigger an immediate recheck (rather than waiting for the periodic cycle):

  • The supplier's or customer's licence is about to expire
  • Notification is received from the competent authority (for example, a supplier's authorisation has been suspended)
  • Media reports of quality problems at the supplier
  • A customer shows the unusual purchasing patterns mentioned in 5.3.3

Practical Application: Supplier Qualification Management at a CDMO (AVL System)

In CDMO procurement, supplier qualification is usually managed systematically through an Approved Vendor List (AVL):

  • The AVL should hold a copy of each supplier's licence and its validity period, and the system should automatically remind staff to recheck 90 days before expiry
  • The due diligence records required by 5.2.4 should form a mandatory part of the new supplier assessment report
  • High-risk items (such as vaccine raw materials and rare excipients) should be rechecked annually; low-risk items can be rechecked every two years
  • Unusual purchase orders (value or quantity outside the historical range) should trigger an automatic review workflow, with confirmation by QA

5.4 Receipt & 5.5 Storage | Receipt & Storage

Original Text

5.4 RECEIPT OF MEDICINAL PRODUCTS

5.4.1 The purpose of the receiving function is to ensure that the arriving consignment is correct, that the medicinal products originate from approved suppliers and that they have not been visibly damaged during transport.

5.4.2 Medicinal products requiring special handling, storage or security measures should be prioritised and once appropriate checks have been conducted they should be immediately transferred to appropriate storage facilities.

5.4.3 Batches of medicinal products should not be transferred to saleable stock before assurance has been obtained in accordance with written procedures, that they are authorised for sale.

5.4.4 If a falsified product is suspected, the batch should be segregated and reported to competent authorities as required by national legislation.

5.5 STORAGE

5.5.1 Medicinal products and, if necessary, healthcare products should be stored separately from other products likely to alter them and should be protected from the harmful effects of light, temperature, moisture and other external factors. Particular attention should be paid to products requiring specific storage conditions.

5.5.2 Incoming containers of medicinal products should be cleaned, if necessary, before storage. Any activities performed on the incoming goods (e.g. fumigation) should not impact on the quality of the medicinal products.

5.5.3 Warehousing operations must ensure appropriate storage conditions are maintained and allow for appropriate security of stocks.

5.5.4 Stock should be rotated according to the first expiry, first out (FEFO) principle. Exceptions should be documented.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: Three Layers of Checks at Receipt (5.4.1)

The "purpose of receipt" in 5.4.1 has three aspects, corresponding to three different checking actions:

  • Correct: Check that the product name, strength, and quantity agree with the purchase order. This prevents "wrong deliveries" and quantity discrepancies.
  • Approved supplier: Confirm that the sender is indeed an approved supplier on the AVL and that valid supply documents (such as CoA and delivery note) accompany the goods.
  • Not visibly damaged: Check whether the packaging is intact and free of moisture damage, crushing, or damaged labels. If there is any doubt, the batch should be quarantined rather than put directly into stock.

Key Concept: FEFO vs FIFO: Why Expiry Date Comes First (5.5.4)

General warehouses commonly use FIFO (First In First Out), but GDP requires FEFO (First Expiry First Out).

The difference is this: the same product may exist in two batches, where batch A arrived first (first in) but expires later than batch B. Under FIFO, batch A would be issued first; under FEFO, batch B, which expires sooner, should go out first, so that product on the market always has sufficient remaining shelf life.

Exceptions must be documented: for example, when a customer specifically requests a batch, or batch B has a pending quality investigation. The reason for deviating from FEFO must be recorded in writing.

Analogy: Shelf Management of Fresh Milk in a Supermarket

When a supermarket restocks the refrigerator with fresh milk, the new stock goes to the back and the older stock with the nearer expiry date is pushed to the front. This is exactly the principle of FEFO. Customers take from the front and always get the product closest to expiry, so the milk is consumed within its shelf life and purchasing volumes become predictable.

A pharmaceutical warehouse works on the same logic: warehouse staff do not issue "newest first" but "closest to expiry first". This needs the support of a WMS (warehouse management system), which automatically recommends the batch with the nearest expiry date at picking, rather than leaving the decision to people.

Key Point: Mandatory Reporting Obligation for Suspected Falsified Products (5.4.4)

5.4.4 is one of the provisions of Chapter 5 with the greatest legal force. If a falsified product is suspected to have been received, there are two mandatory actions:

  1. Segregate immediately: Physically separate the suspect batch from normal stock and label it "Do not distribute" to prevent the falsified product from leaving the premises.
  2. Report to the competent authority: Report to the TFDA (Taiwan Food and Drug Administration) or the equivalent competent authority in accordance with national legislation.

Note: the provision does not say "if suspected, consider reporting"; it says the product "should be segregated and reported as required". Failure to report is itself a violation.

Key Point: The Engineering Rationale for "Not Directly on the Floor" (5.5.5)

Medicinal products must not be placed directly on the floor, for these reasons: the floor is a source of contamination (dust, cleaning agent residues, pests); a damp floor or abnormal temperatures near the floor may affect product quality; and forklift operations may crush the product.

Exception: "the package is designed to allow such storage", for example certain medical gas cylinders whose bases are designed with a supporting structure.

Practical Application: Receipt of Cold Chain Biologics

For a sterile CDMO in Taiwan, receipt of cold chain products (2–8°C or -20°C/-80°C) is a high-risk operation, corresponding to 5.4.2, which says that items requiring special handling should be prioritised:

  • Temperature record review: The carrier should provide the report from a temperature logger covering the whole journey. Any cold chain break (excursion) must be identified at receipt, and the product must not be put into stock.
  • Time limit from receipt to storage: The time from unloading to placing refrigerated/frozen goods in the cold room should be specified in an SOP (for example, no more than 15 minutes).
  • Quarantine put-away under 5.4.3: Even if the cold chain is intact, all received goods should first be placed in the "Quarantine" area, and moved to "Approved Stock" only after QA confirms that they may be released for sale.

  2. 5.5.6 says that products nearing expiry should be removed immediately from saleable stock, but "nearing" is not quantitatively defined. 5.6.1 Medicinal products intended for destruction should be appropriately identified, held separately and handled in accordance with a written procedure.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: Three Control Aspects of Destruction of Medicines (5.6)

Control of products for destruction has three layers:

  • Identification and segregation (5.6.1): Products to be destroyed must be clearly identified (for example, with a "For destruction" label) and physically segregated from normal stock. This prevents products intended for destruction from accidentally re-entering the supply chain (a common route for falsified product incidents).
  • Compliant destruction (5.6.2): Medicines (especially narcotics and cytotoxics) cannot be disposed of like ordinary waste. They must be handled by a qualified contractor in accordance with regulations (such as Taiwan's Waste Disposal Act and relevant TFDA rules).
  • Record keeping (5.6.3): Destruction records are an essential part of the audit trail. Auditors need to confirm that the destroyed batches were actually destroyed and not diverted to other uses.

Key Concept: Required Fields on Supply Documents (Delivery Notes) (5.8)

The field requirements for the delivery documents in 5.8 (delivery note / packing list) overlap heavily with the transaction records in 4.2.9, but add the following important fields:

  • Pharmaceutical dosage form: for example solution for injection, lyophilised powder for injection, or oral tablet. This prevents confusion between products of the same name in different dosage forms.
  • Actual physical storage premises: If the consignee's registered address differs from the actual storage address, the actual storage location must be stated, so that the product can be traced to a physical location.
  • Transport and storage conditions: That is, the conditions marked on the outer packaging, so that both the distributor and the recipient comply with them.

Analogy: The "Double Check" Logic of Picking

Imagine taking medicine from a medicine box in the dark. If two bottles look similar (for example 10 mg and 100 mg, a tenfold difference in dose), grabbing one at random could be fatal.

In practice, the requirement in 5.7 to ensure that the "correct product" is picked needs at least two checks:

  • First check: WMS scan verification (after the barcode/QR code is scanned, the system verifies product name and batch number)
  • Second check: Manual visual check (product name, strength, expiry date)

"Appropriate remaining shelf life" must also be confirmed at picking: whether the remaining shelf life meets the customer's minimum requirement (for example, a customer may require not less than 12 months remaining at delivery).

Key Point: Audit Risk in Destruction Records

The "retention of destruction records" in 5.6.3 is a frequent audit focus. The auditor's logic is: "If goods that came into stock have not all gone out or are not still in stock, where did they go?"

If destruction records are incomplete, auditors may question whether batches were illegally diverted or disposed of. Destruction records should therefore include: date of destruction, batch information, reason for destruction, quantity destroyed, personnel who carried it out, supervisor approval, and method of destruction (for example, the receipt/certificate from the contracted destruction company).

Practical Application: Export Distribution Document Management at a CDMO

A Taiwan CDMO usually has to meet the documentation requirements of both the TFDA and the importing country (such as the EU, US, or Japan). Recommendations for designing the delivery documents under 5.8:

  • Use a bilingual (Chinese and English) packing list format so that international customers can understand it
  • "Actual physical storage premises" is especially important where an intermediary distributor is involved: if Company A is the nominal customer but the goods are actually delivered to Hospital B, Hospital B's address must appear on the delivery note
  • The delivery note for cold chain products should attach a "Transport Validation Summary" confirming that the transport conditions meet the requirements
  • Electronic copies of all delivery documents should be archived in the WMS or ERP for later traceability queries

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Dual Authorisation Logic of Import and Export (5.9.1 & 5.9.2)

5.9 establishes two core principles for cross-border distribution:

  • National legislation takes priority (5.9.1): Import and export activities must comply with the legislation of both the exporting country and the importing country. This is not only a GDP requirement but also a general trade law obligation.
  • Two-way qualification check (5.9.2): Whether purchasing from abroad (import) or supplying abroad (export), you must confirm that the counterparty is an "authorised" operator in its own country. You cannot rely on domestic law alone.

Important supplement on free zones: Even medicines stored in a free zone must still meet the authorisation requirements of the "internal market" if they flow into it. Free zone status cannot be used as a basis for exemption from GDP.

Analogy: A Scenario from Taiwan Pharmaceutical Export Practice

A sterile CDMO in Taiwan manufactures a biological product under contract for a customer. The product has GMP clearance in Taiwan but has not obtained a marketing authorisation in Taiwan (it is for export only).

This is exactly the scenario addressed by 5.9.1, which requires preventing unapproved products from entering the internal market: this batch must be exported directly and must not circulate in the Taiwan market, even if there is demand in Taiwan (because there is no Taiwan marketing authorisation). The wholesaler is responsible for ensuring that the product is not "accidentally" introduced into the local market.

Key Point: Checklist for Verifying Import/Export Qualification Documents

Under 5.9.2, the following documents should be confirmed for cross-border transactions:

  • Exporter: the Taiwan wholesaler's GDP licence, export permit (if required), and TFDA approval documents
  • Importer: the importing country's wholesale licence or proof of importer status, and the product's Marketing Authorisation in the importing country
  • The product itself: confirm whether the product is approved for marketing in the importing country (if not, confirm whether a special exception applies, such as investigational medicines for clinical trials)

"Authorised or entitled" covers two situations: authorisation through a formal licence, or entitlement arising automatically under the law (for example, in some countries pharmacists may buy directly from manufacturers).

Practical Application: Compliance Strategy for Cross-Border Medicine Flows at a Taiwan CDMO

Common cross-border scenarios for a sterile CDMO in Taiwan:

  • Tolling (contract manufacturing and re-import): The active substance is made overseas, the intermediate is imported into Taiwan for filling, and the finished product is exported again. It must be confirmed that the API importer, the Taiwan CDMO, and the customer all hold the appropriate authorisations.
  • Multi-country marketing strategy: The same batch may supply both the US (FDA authorisation) and EU (EMA authorisation) markets. Delivery documents must clearly state the destination country and the corresponding basis of authorisation.
  • Export of investigational medicinal products (IMP export): Investigational Medicinal Products usually do not yet have a marketing authorisation but may be exported under a clinical trial authorisation. In this case, the interpretation of "authorised" in 5.9.2 must refer to the CTA (clinical trial authorisation) documents of the destination country.

6.1 Principle

Original Text

6.1 PRINCIPLE

All complaints, returns, suspected falsified medicinal products and recalls must be recorded and handled carefully according to written procedures. Records should be made available to the competent authorities. An assessment of returned medicinal products should be performed by designated personnel before any approval for resale. A consistent approach by all partners in the supply chain is required in order to be successful in the fight against falsified medicinal products.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The common foundation of the four event types: This section covers the four most sensitive event types in GDP. PIC/S PE 011-1 §6.1 sets three common foundations for them:

  • Written procedures: established in advance; events must not be handled ad hoc
  • Complete records: available for review by the competent authorities at any time
  • Designated personnel: returns must be assessed by specifically authorised persons, not decided by front-line warehouse staff on their own

Consistency across the supply chain: controlling falsified medicines requires a consistent response from the whole supply chain. Manufacturers, wholesalers and pharmacies must all follow the same logic, because a lapse at any one link can let a falsified product back into the market.

Analogy

Think of the supply chain as a river. A falsified medicine is like a pollutant: if there is a gap in the gates upstream (the manufacturer) or at any point midstream (the wholesalers), the contamination flows downstream to the patient. The "consistency requirement" in GDP §6.1 means every gate must be held to the same standard, and nobody can let something through on the grounds that "it is someone else's problem".

At warehouse level, this means your SOP is not only a way of protecting yourself but also part of the whole line of defence.

Key Notes

"Available to the competent authorities" does not mean "keep on file": It means records must be current, complete and indexed. If TFDA finds incomplete or untraceable records during an inspection, this is a major deficiency.

Authorisation documents for designated personnel: "Designated personnel" must have a documented basis for their authorisation (usually named in an SOP or quality agreement). Returns assessments cannot be performed by whoever happens to be free.

6.2 Complaints

Original Text

6.2.1 Complaints should be recorded with all the original details. A distinction should be made between complaints related to the quality of a medicinal product and those related to distribution. In the event of a complaint about the quality of a medicinal product and a potential product defect, the manufacturer and/or marketing authorisation holder (MAH) should be informed without delay. Any product distribution complaint should be thoroughly investigated to identify the origin of or reason for the complaint.

6.2.2 If a defect relating to a medicinal product is discovered or suspected, consideration should be given to whether other batches of the product should also be investigated.

6.2.3 A person should be appointed to handle complaints.

6.2.4 If necessary, appropriate follow-up actions (including CAPA) should be taken after investigation and evaluation of the complaint, including where required notification to the national competent authorities.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Classifying the complaint is the critical first step: GDP §6.2.1 requires a clear distinction between:

  • Quality complaint: concerns the product itself, such as abnormal appearance, insufficient potency or contamination. The manufacturer/MAH must be notified immediately, because a batch-wide quality problem may be involved.
  • Distribution complaint: concerns the logistics side, such as late delivery, damaged packaging (not a product problem) or temperature excursion records. The wholesaler investigates it itself.

CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Action): §6.2.4 requires CAPA to be implemented after investigation where necessary. This links complaint handling to the quality management system (QMS) and helps ensure the problem does not recur.

Extended investigation of other batches: The batch-extension principle in §6.2.2 is an important GDP way of thinking: a single complaint may be the tip of the iceberg, so you should systematically assess whether the problem is batch-related.

Analogy

Imagine you buy a bottle of drink with an off smell at a convenience store. If you complain to the store (= a distribution complaint), the store investigates whether refrigeration failed during delivery. But if you say "the drink itself is bad" (= a quality complaint), the store must notify the manufacturer immediately, because thousands of bottles from the same batch may be on the market.

The classification logic in GDP §6.2 is exactly the same: whoever owns the problem is responsible, but speed and scope differ.

Key Notes

"Without delay" does not mean after working hours: For a quality complaint involving a potential defect, the timeliness of notifying the MAH is critical. Delayed notification can allow more defective product to reach the market, and the competent authorities have strict expectations on this.

Designated complaint handler (§6.2.3): A specific person must be designated in writing; it cannot be "anyone can deal with it". In a CDMO setting this is usually a QA representative or customer service supervisor, with appropriate training records.

The importance of original details: "All the original details" means complaint records must not be rewritten or tidied up. The customer's original wording, timestamps, batch numbers and so on must be preserved in full to support later audits.

Practical Application

In a Taiwan CDMO warehouse scenario: a customer calls to report that the outer carton of the injectable they received is dented, but the inner packaging is intact and the product is undamaged.

Classification: This is a distribution complaint (damaged outer packaging is a logistics issue), so there is no need to notify the manufacturer immediately. Investigate whether the carton was crushed during packing, knocked in transit, or damaged during unloading at the customer site.

If the customer also says "the solution seems a bit yellow", the case escalates to a quality complaint: immediately place the batch concerned on hold and notify the MAH. The two issues must be logged and handled separately.

  1. A hospital pharmacist complains that the insulin injection they received expires two weeks earlier than expected. Is this a quality complaint or a distribution complaint? Why?
  2. After investigating a complaint you find the root cause was a picking error (FIFO not properly applied). How would you design the CAPA? Does the competent authority need to be notified?
6.3 Returned Medicinal Products

Original Text

6.3.1 Returned products must be handled according to a written, risk-based process taking into account the product concerned, any specific storage requirements and the time elapsed since the medicinal product was originally dispatched. Returns should be conducted in accordance with national legislation, and contractual arrangements between the parties. A record/list of returned goods must be maintained.

6.3.2 Medicinal products which have left the premises of the distributor should only be returned to saleable stock if all of the following are confirmed:

  • (i) the medicinal products are in their unopened and undamaged secondary packaging and are in good condition; have not expired and have not been recalled;
  • (ii) medicinal products returned from a customer not holding a wholesale distribution authorisation or from pharmacies authorised to supply medicinal products to the public should only be returned to saleable stock if they are returned within an acceptable time limit, for example 10 days;
  • (iii) it has been demonstrated by the customer that the medicinal products have been transported, stored and handled in compliance with the specific storage requirements;
  • (iv) they have been examined and assessed by a sufficiently trained and competent person authorised to do so; the distributor has reasonable evidence that the product was supplied to that customer (via copies of the original delivery note or by referencing invoice numbers/batch numbers, expiry date etc.), and that there is no reason to believe that the product has been falsified.

6.3.3 Moreover, for medicinal products requiring specific temperature storage conditions, returns to saleable stock can only be made if there is documented evidence that the product has been stored under the authorised storage conditions throughout the entire time. If any deviation has occurred a risk assessment has to be performed. The evidence should cover:

  • (i) delivery to customer;
  • (ii) examination of the product;
  • (iii) opening of the transport packaging;
  • (iv) return of the product to the packaging;
  • (v) collection and return to the distributor;
  • (vi) record of temperature readings during transportation;
  • (vii) return to the distribution site refrigerator.

6.3.4 Products returned to saleable stock should be placed such that the 'first expired first out' (FEFO) system operates effectively.

6.3.5 Stolen products that have been recovered cannot be returned to saleable stock and sold to customers.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The four gates for returning a product to saleable stock (§6.3.2): If any one is not met, the product must not go back to saleable stock and can only be segregated pending disposition.

  • Condition (i): intact, not expired and not recalled
  • Condition (ii): for returns from parties other than authorised wholesalers or pharmacies, the return must be handled within an acceptable time limit (for example 10 days)
  • Condition (iii): the customer must demonstrate that storage during their custody was compliant
  • Condition (iv): assessed by an authorised person, with proof of the original supply and no suspicion of falsification

The seven-point temperature evidence chain for cold-chain products (§6.3.3): This is the core of cold-chain returns. From shipment to return to the warehouse, written temperature records are needed at seven points, and any deviation at any point requires a risk assessment before a decision can be made on returning the product to stock.

FEFO (First Expired First Out): After a return is put back into stock, it cannot simply go into any location. It must be re-sequenced by expiry date so that the earliest-expiring product ships first and does not expire in stock.

Analogy

Think of a returned frozen fish as a returned cold-chain medicine. When a supermarket receives a frozen fish back from a customer, before putting it back on the shelf it must confirm:

  • The packaging is not broken (intact appearance)
  • The customer can prove the fish stayed at -18°C in their freezer the whole time (storage compliance)
  • It was bought this morning, not three weeks ago (time limit)
  • The fish was indeed bought from this supermarket (proof of original supply)

The logic for medicines is exactly the same, only with stricter standards. Summer temperatures in Taiwan reach 35°C, so the temperature integrity of cold-chain returns is a particularly high-risk item.

Key Notes

The burden of proof lies with the customer (§6.3.2.iii & 6.3.3): "Demonstrated by the customer" clearly states that the customer must provide proof of compliant storage; the wholesaler cannot simply assume the customer complied. If the customer cannot provide it, the return must not go back to stock, even if the product looks fine.

Products recovered after theft (§6.3.5) must never be returned to stock: This rule has no exceptions. The chain of custody of a stolen product has been broken, tampering or inappropriate storage conditions cannot be ruled out, and it can never return to saleable stock.

A risk assessment is not an approval: In §6.3.3, the risk assessment after a temperature deviation is a "decision tool", not a "release route". The outcome may well be "cannot be released, destroy".

Practical Application

Scenario: A hospital pharmacy returns a batch of biologics that require 2-8°C storage, 7 days after shipment. The customer provides continuous temperature records (logger data) from their refrigerator showing 3-6°C throughout. However, the pharmacy does not hold a wholesale dealer licence.

Decision flow:

  1. Confirm the outer packaging is intact, the product is not expired and not recalled ✓
  2. Is 7 days within the "acceptable time limit"? → To be defined in the company SOP (PE011 suggests, for example, 10 days) ✓
  3. The customer provided temperature logger data ✓ (this is one of the items of evidence required by §6.3.3)
  4. Confirm the original delivery note and invoice number match ✓
  5. The authorised person assesses the seven-point temperature evidence chain (including the temperature record of the return transport)
  6. Only after everything is confirmed may the product go back into the cold store, re-positioned according to FEFO

If the documentation for any one point is missing, the product goes back to the quarantine area to await a QA decision.

6.4 Falsified Medicinal Products

Original Text

6.4.1 The sale and distribution of a suspected falsified medicinal product should be suspended immediately.

6.4.2 Wholesale distributors must immediately inform the competent authority and the marketing authorisation holder (MAH) of any medicinal products they identify as falsified or suspect to be falsified and act on the instructions as specified by the competent authority. A procedure should be in place to this effect. It should be recorded with all the original details and investigated.

6.4.3 Any falsified medicinal products found in the supply chain should immediately be physically segregated and stored in a dedicated area away from all other medicinal products and be appropriately labelled. All relevant activities in relation to such products should be documented and records retained.

6.4.4 Upon confirmation as a falsified medicinal product, a formal decision should be taken on removal from the market, ensuring that it does not re-enter the supply chain, including retention of any samples necessary for public health, regulatory, or legal needs and arrangements for its disposal. All related decisions should be appropriately documented.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Four steps in responding to falsified medicinal products:

  1. Immediately suspend sale/distribution (§6.4.1): "Suspected" is enough to trigger this; there is no need to wait for confirmation
  2. Immediately notify TFDA and the MAH (§6.4.2): both must be notified at the same time, supported by a written procedure (SOP)
  3. Physical segregation + labelling (§6.4.3): a dedicated area, clearly labelled, with all related activities recorded
  4. After confirmation, formal withdrawal + retained samples + destruction arrangements (§6.4.4): ensure the falsified product does not return to the supply chain, and keep the necessary samples for regulatory or judicial needs

Marketing Authorisation Holder (MAH): The MAH is the legal entity ultimately responsible for the quality and safety of the medicinal product. A wholesaler that finds a suspected falsified product must notify it, because the MAH is best placed to authenticate the product.

Analogy

Think of a falsified medicine as an explosive device suddenly found in the warehouse: you do not wait for a bomb disposal expert to confirm before evacuating. On "suspicion" you act immediately (stop sales), cordon off the area (dedicated segregation space) and notify the police and the authorities (TFDA + MAH). After confirmation, the competent authority directs what happens next.

Time is critical: every extra day a falsified medicine stays on the market adds risk. Speed takes priority over a perfect procedure.

Key Notes

"Suspected" triggers action without waiting for confirmation: §6.4.1 uses "suspected", not "confirmed". This is an important GDP design choice: better to wrongly hold a good batch than to let a falsified product circulate for one more hour.

No disposal on your own; wait for the competent authority's instructions: §6.4.2 explicitly requires you to "act on the instructions as specified by the competent authority". A wholesaler cannot decide on its own to destroy falsified product and must wait for TFDA's instructions, because the samples may be needed for judicial use.

Labelling must be clear: "Appropriately labelled" in practice means a clear segregation label (for example QUARANTINE - SUSPECTED FALSIFIED), so that warehouse staff do not pick or issue the product by mistake.

Practical Application

Scenario: During routine goods-in inspection, a warehouse operator notices abnormal label print quality on the vials of an injectable batch (blurred fonts, batch number format different from usual) and suspects falsification.

Immediate action list (per §6.4):

  1. Do not accept the consignment, or if it has already been put into stock, immediately stop any sales/picking orders
  2. Move the suspect product to a dedicated quarantine area (separate from the general returns quarantine area too) and apply a clear suspected-falsified quarantine label
  3. Immediately notify the QA manager, who activates the SOP
  4. The QA manager notifies both TFDA (Taiwan Food and Drug Administration) and the MAH (marketing authorisation holder)
  5. Record every action: who found it, when, which batch, a description of the situation on site, and photographs
  6. After TFDA gives its instructions, proceed as instructed (retained samples, return, destruction, etc.)

Practice Questions

  1. In a consignment you received, 5% of the quantity (10 units in total) has abnormal labels and the other 95% looks normal. Should you segregate only those 10 units or the whole batch? Why?
  2. The supplier phones to say "that batch is a normal variation caused by the printer changing paper stock, not a falsified product". Can you release the quarantine on that basis? What is the correct approach under §6.4?
6.5 Medicinal Product Recalls

Original Text

6.5.1 There should be documentation and procedures in place to ensure traceability of products received and distributed, to facilitate product recall.

6.5.2 In the event of a product recall, all customers to whom the product has been distributed shall be informed with the appropriate degree of urgency and clear actionable instructions.

6.5.3 The national regulatory authority should be informed of all product recalls. If the product is exported, the overseas counterparts and/or regulatory authorities must be informed of the recall as required by national legislation.

6.5.4 The effectiveness of the arrangements for product recall should be evaluated regularly (at least annually).

6.5.5 Recall operations should be capable of being initiated promptly and at any time.

6.5.6 The distributor must follow the instructions of a recall message, which should be approved, if required, by the competent authorities.

6.5.7 Any recall operation should be recorded at the time it is carried out. Records should be made readily available to the competent authorities.

6.5.8 The distribution records should be readily accessible to the person(s) responsible for the recall, and should contain sufficient information on distributors and directly supplied customers (with addresses, phone and/or fax numbers inside and outside working hours, batch numbers as required by national legislation and quantities delivered), including those for exported products and medicinal product samples.

6.5.9 The progress of the recall process should be recorded for a final report including reconciliation of the recalled product.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three systemic requirements for recalls:

  • Traceability: §6.5.1 and §6.5.8 require distribution records detailed enough to trace which batch went to which customers, including contact telephone numbers outside working hours
  • Able to start at any time (§6.5.5): recall capability cannot be limited to Monday-to-Friday office hours. If a recall notice arrives at 3 a.m., you must still be able to start the operation
  • Annual effectiveness evaluation (§6.5.4): carry out a mock recall or exercise at least once a year to verify the effectiveness of the recall procedure

Final recall report and reconciliation: The reconciliation required by §6.5.9 means that "quantity sent out vs. quantity recovered" must be documented, with reasons given for any difference. Complete quantity reconciliation is a core indicator the competent authority uses to assess the effectiveness of a recall.

Analogy

Think of a recall as a military mobilisation order: in peacetime (§6.5.1 traceability) you must already hold a complete roster of personnel (customer data); when the order comes (the recall notice), you must reach everyone in the shortest possible time (§6.5.2 clear, actionable instructions); and when it ends you need a roll-call report (§6.5.9 final report and reconciliation).

The "annual exercise" in §6.5.4 is the peacetime mobilisation drill. It makes sure that when it is really needed, the recall does not fail because the procedure is unfamiliar or the contact data are out of date.

Key Notes

"Out-of-hours contact numbers" are a hard requirement (§6.5.8): Distribution records must include customers' telephone numbers "inside and outside working hours". If the customer list holds only office numbers, you cannot reach anyone when a recall happens late at night, and this is an audit deficiency.

Recall notification obligations for exported products (§6.5.3): If a product has been exported, overseas counterparts and local competent authorities must be notified in accordance with national legislation. A Taiwan CDMO with export business must set out this process clearly in its SOP.

Approval of recall messages (§6.5.6): The wholesaler must act "in accordance with the recall notice" and cannot modify the recall scope or instructions on its own. If the competent authority requires prior approval of the recall notice, approval must be obtained before it is sent out.

Real-time recording (§6.5.7): "Recorded at the time it is carried out" means recall records cannot be written up after the fact. They must be made contemporaneously, with timestamps.

Practical Application

Designing a mock recall exercise (for a Taiwan CDMO warehouse):

At least once a year, select a batch that has already been shipped and simulate the following steps:

  1. Traceability test: starting from the batch number, produce the list of all customers and quantities shipped within 2 hours
  2. Contactability test: if it happened outside working hours, could you reach all direct customers within 4 hours?
  3. Stock reconciliation: the quantity not yet shipped or already returned to stock + the quantity customers confirm as recovered should equal the original quantity shipped
  4. Documentation: record the whole exercise in real time and, at the end, produce an exercise report documenting the problems found and improvement actions

If the exercise finds outdated customer contact data, batch tracing that exceeds the time limit, or an incomplete customer list, each of these is a deficiency requiring CAPA.

Practice Questions

  1. Your distribution records show that 1000 units of a batch were shipped. After the recall, customers returned 820 units and 50 units were found in stock. 130 units remain unaccounted for. Under the reconciliation requirement in §6.5.9, how should your final report handle the 130-unit difference?
  2. On a Saturday at dawn you receive a Class II recall notice from the MAH, requiring all customers to be notified within 24 hours. Should your company SOP cover recall initiation outside working hours? What is the reasoning under §6.5.5?
  3. Why does §6.5.4 require recall effectiveness to be evaluated "at least annually", rather than only after a recall has occurred?

Section Summary

PIC/S PE 011-1 Chapter 6 is the most operationally practical chapter of GDP, covering management requirements for four high-risk event types:

  • 6.1 Principle: all four event types require written procedures, complete records and designated personnel, and every link of the supply chain must respond consistently
  • 6.2 Complaints: distinguish quality complaints (notify the MAH immediately) from distribution complaints (investigate yourself); a designated complaint handler is required; implement CAPA after investigation where needed
  • 6.3 Returns: all four conditions must be met before return to stock; cold-chain products need the seven-point temperature evidence chain; products recovered after theft must never be returned to stock; position returned stock according to FEFO
  • 6.4 Falsified medicines: suspicion alone means stop sales → immediately notify TFDA and the MAH → physically segregate and label → after confirmation, withdraw and destroy as directed by the competent authority, keeping the necessary samples
  • 6.5 Recalls: traceability is the foundation of a successful recall; be able to start at any time; notification obligations extend overseas; exercise and evaluate at least annually; the final report must include quantity reconciliation

In a Taiwan CDMO setting, managing these four event types directly affects customer trust, TFDA inspection outcomes and legal liability. Clear SOPs, complete distribution records (including customers' out-of-hours contact details) and an annual mock recall exercise are the minimum baseline for compliance.

PIC/S PE 011-1 (2014): Guide to Good Distribution Practice for Medicinal Products | p20

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the regulatory framework for GDP outsourced activities: outsourcing does not transfer accountability, and the Contract Giver remains ultimately responsible
  • Distinguish the responsibilities and obligations of the Contract Giver and the Contract Acceptor
  • Master the audit requirements before and during outsourcing, including the logic for designing a risk-based audit frequency

7.1 Principle

Original Text

Any activity covered by the GDP Guide that is outsourced should be correctly defined, agreed and controlled in order to avoid misunderstandings which could affect the integrity of the product. There must be a written Contract between the Contract Giver and the Contract Acceptor which clearly establishes the duties of each party.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Control Elements of Outsourced Activities

This section sets out three necessary conditions for compliant outsourcing:

  • Correctly Defined: The scope of the outsourced activity must be described clearly, leaving no gray areas. For example, "cold chain transport" is not specific enough; define it as "2–8°C temperature-controlled transport from the delivery point at Site A to the warehouse of Customer B, including temperature records and handover confirmation".
  • Agreed: There must be a written contract. This is mandatory wording (must), not a recommendation (should). A verbal agreement is not accepted in a GDP inspection.
  • Controlled: Outsourcing does not end when the contract is signed. There must be ongoing oversight mechanisms (audits, KPI monitoring, deviation reporting).

Key logic: outsourcing is the "delegated performance" of responsibility, not the "transfer" of responsibility.

Analogy

Imagine you entrust a valuable artwork to a moving company. You are the contract giver and the moving company is the contract acceptor. You cannot say, "I handed it over to them, so if it breaks it has nothing to do with me." Legally, you are responsible for confirming that the moving company has adequate equipment and training, and that the contract clearly specifies packaging requirements, temperature and humidity limits, insurance liability and so on. GDP outsourcing management follows the same logic. Medicines, which tolerate failure even less than artwork, demand an even more rigorous approach to outsourcing.

Key Note

Regulatory implication of "Must" vs. "Should": This section says "There must be a written Contract". In GDP/GMP documents, must means an absolute requirement, while should means a strong recommendation with some flexibility. A written contract is an absolute prerequisite for GDP outsourcing, and the absence of one is a major deficiency.

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO has engaged a logistics company to distribute refrigerated medicines, but the two parties have only a verbal agreement. How would an inspector classify this situation in a GDP inspection?
  2. "Avoid misunderstandings that may result in a product of unsatisfactory quality": which three contract clauses are the most prone to misunderstanding and the most critical?
7.2 Contract Giver

Original Text

7.2.1 The Contract Giver is responsible for the activities contracted out.

7.2.2 The Contract Giver is responsible for assessing the competence of the Contract Acceptor to successfully carry out the work required and for ensuring by means of the contract and through audits that the principles and guidelines of GDP are followed. An audit of the Contract Acceptor should be performed before commencement of, and whenever there has been a change to, the outsourced activities. The requirement for audit and frequency should be defined based on risk depending on the nature of the outsourced activities. Audits should be permitted at any time.

7.2.3 The Contract Giver should provide the Contract Acceptor with all the information necessary to carry out the contracted operations in accordance with the specific product requirements and any other relevant requirements.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Layers of Responsibility for the Contract Giver

7.2.1 Ultimate responsibility cannot be outsourced: Even when the activity is performed by the Contract Acceptor, the Contract Giver remains ultimately responsible. This is the core logic of GDP outsourcing management. For a CDMO, if you engage a third-party warehouse to store a customer's medicines and a temperature deviation occurs there, you as the Contract Giver are still answerable to the customer.

7.2.2 Three points in time for audits:

  • Before commencement: A qualification audit to confirm the Contract Acceptor is capable
  • Whenever there has been a change: For example, the Contract Acceptor relocates, changes equipment or has personnel changes
  • Periodic audits (risk-based frequency): The frequency is defined according to the risk of the activity; it is not a fixed once a year

7.2.3 Provision of sufficient information: The Contract Giver is obliged to provide the Contract Acceptor with sufficient product information, including temperature requirements, special handling precautions and product hazards. If problems arise from insufficient information, the Contract Giver cannot escape blame.

Analogy

Imagine you hire a nanny to look after your child. You (the contract giver) cannot say, "I hired a nanny, so if something happens to the child it has nothing to do with me." You are responsible for: (1) confirming the nanny is qualified (pre-engagement audit); (2) telling the nanny about the child's allergies and routines (providing sufficient information); (3) coming home unannounced from time to time to check on things (audits at any time). The logic of GDP outsourcing management is exactly the same; only the thing being protected changes from a child to a medicine.

Key Note

Designing a risk-based audit frequency: The document says the frequency should be "based on risk", which means you need a supplier risk scoring system. High-risk Contract Acceptors (such as cold chain logistics or a warehouse handling narcotics) should be audited more often than low-risk ones (such as an office cleaning service). It is advisable to define the risk categories and the corresponding audit frequencies explicitly in the Supplier Qualification SOP (for example: high risk = on-site audit every year, medium risk = every two years, low risk = self-assessment questionnaire).

"Audits should be permitted at any time": This sentence should be written into the contract terms. The Contract Acceptor must agree in the contract to accept unannounced audits by the Contract Giver; otherwise the contract itself does not meet GDP requirements.

Practical Application

Your CDMO is evaluating a new cold chain logistics provider (2–8°C) to undertake domestic distribution of customers' medicines. Under the requirements of 7.2, you need to complete the following before formally outsourcing:

  1. Supplier qualification questionnaire: Confirm the provider has GDP-compliant equipment, SOPs and personnel training records
  2. On-site audit: Focus on the temperature control equipment and calibration records of the refrigerated vehicles, driver training and the deviation handling SOP
  3. Contract drafting: It must include a clause permitting unannounced audits, a time limit for reporting temperature deviations (24 hours is recommended) and confidentiality obligations
  4. Transfer of product technical information: Provide the medicines' storage requirements, emergency contacts and a list of customer delivery addresses

Practice Questions

  1. Your contracted logistics provider is expanding its business and moves its warehouse from Taipei to Taoyuan. Does this count as "a change"? Do you need to re-audit after the move?
  2. If the cold chain logistics provider you outsource to holds GDP certification, are you exempt from your audit obligation? Why or why not?
  3. For a sterile injectable product, what specific documents or data should the "all necessary information" provided by the Contract Giver include?
7.3 Contract Acceptor

Original Text

7.3.1 The Contract Acceptor is responsible for the activities covered by GDP and delegated by the Contract Giver.

7.3.2 The Contract Acceptor should have adequate premises and equipment, procedures, knowledge and experience, and competent personnel to carry out the work ordered by the Contract Giver.

7.3.3 The Contract Acceptor should not pass to a third party any of the work entrusted to him under the contract without the Contract Giver's prior evaluation and approval of the arrangements and an audit of the third party by the Contract Giver or the Contract Acceptor. Arrangements made between the Contract Acceptor and any third party should ensure that the wholesale distribution information is made available in the same way as between the original Contract Giver and Contract Acceptor.

7.3.4 The Contract Acceptor should refrain from any activity which may adversely affect the quality of the product(s) handled for the Contract Giver.

7.3.5 The Contract Acceptor must forward any information that can influence the quality of the product(s) to the Contract Giver in accordance with the requirement of the contract.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Five Obligations of the Contract Acceptor

7.3.1 Responsibility for performance: The Contract Acceptor is responsible for the GDP activities it performs. This exists alongside the Contract Giver's ultimate responsibility and does not replace it. The responsibilities of each party must be clearly distinguished in the contract.

7.3.2 Capability requirements (four elements):

  • Premises and equipment: suitable storage conditions and transport equipment
  • Procedures: well-established SOPs
  • Knowledge and experience: understanding of the special requirements of medicine distribution
  • Competent personnel: trained, with records

7.3.3 Unauthorized sub-contracting is prohibited: The Contract Acceptor must not sub-contract work to a third party on its own initiative. It must first obtain the Contract Giver's prior approval, and the Contract Giver (or the Contract Acceptor) must audit the third party. The obligation to pass on information (wholesale distribution information) must extend through the entire outsourcing chain.

7.3.5 Obligation to report information (must): The Contract Acceptor must proactively report to the Contract Giver any information that may affect product quality, including temperature deviations, handling damage and suspected falsified medicines.

Analogy

The Contract Acceptor is like a sub-contractor on a construction project. If the developer (contract giver) hands the waterproofing of a building to a waterproofing company (contract acceptor), that company cannot pass the work on to an unknown small firm without telling the developer (unauthorized sub-contracting). If cracks are found in a wall during construction (information affecting quality), the waterproofing company is obliged to report them to the developer immediately, rather than judging for itself or concealing them. In GDP outsourcing, "information symmetry" is the foundation of quality management.

Key Note

Information flow requirements across the sub-contracting chain: 7.3.3 specifically stresses that if the Contract Acceptor sub-contracts to a third party, "wholesale distribution information must be made available in the same way". This means the completeness of batch tracing, temperature records and handover documentation must be consistent across the entire sub-contracting chain. In practice, it is advisable to require the Contract Acceptor in the contract to disclose complete information on the sub-contracting chain, and to establish an approval procedure in the supplier management SOP.

Proactive obligations vs. passive execution of the Contract Acceptor: Read 7.3.4 and 7.3.5 together: the Contract Acceptor does not merely "passively carry out instructions"; it must also "proactively avoid any activity that may adversely affect quality" and "proactively report problems". These are the Contract Acceptor's active compliance obligations.

Practical Application

Scenario: Your CDMO is the Contract Acceptor, providing finished product warehousing for an international pharmaceutical company (the Contract Giver). One day, an air conditioning failure in your warehouse causes a batch of 2–8°C product to rise to 12°C within 30 minutes.

Under the requirements of 7.3, your obligations are:

  1. Immediately start the temperature deviation SOP: Record the time of the deviation, the maximum temperature and the affected batches
  2. Immediately notify the Contract Giver (7.3.5): Do not wait until the investigation is complete. "Information that may affect product quality" must be reported promptly
  3. Do not dispose of the product on your own: Until the Contract Giver makes a quality assessment decision, do not release, destroy or continue to use the affected batches
  4. Complete the CAPA: Investigate the root cause and propose corrective measures, and submit them to the Contract Giver for review

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO, as Contract Acceptor, is busy and privately engages a partner warehouse to store some of the Contract Giver's medicines. Which GDP requirement does this violate? What measures can the Contract Giver take?
  2. How should the contract specify the time limit and format of "information reporting" so that 7.3.5 can be implemented effectively in practice?
  3. If the way of working required by the Contract Giver (such as the packaging method) could actually be detrimental to product quality, how should the Contract Acceptor handle this under 7.3.4?

Section Summary

  • Outsourcing does not remove accountability: The Contract Giver remains ultimately responsible for all outsourced GDP activities, and a written contract is an absolute requirement (must)
  • Three audit occasions: Before outsourcing, when there is a significant change, and periodically at a risk-defined frequency, with unannounced audits always permitted
  • Obligation of information symmetry: The Contract Giver must provide sufficient product information; the Contract Acceptor must proactively report any information that may affect quality
  • Sub-contracting control: The Contract Acceptor must not sub-contract without the Contract Giver's prior approval (including an audit); the obligation to pass on distribution information along the sub-contracting chain must be equivalent to that of the original contracting chain
  • Proactive obligations of the Contract Acceptor: Beyond passively carrying out instructions, the Contract Acceptor has an active obligation to avoid any activity that may adversely affect product quality
  • The CDMO's dual role: A CDMO may be both a Contract Giver (outsourcing logistics) and a Contract Acceptor (accepting outsourced manufacturing from pharmaceutical companies), so compliance obligations must be examined from both perspectives

Chapter 8: Self-Inspections

p21

PIC/S PE 011-1 (2014): Guide to Good Distribution Practice for Medicinal Products | p21

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the role of self-inspection in GDP compliance: it is a proactive monitoring mechanism, not a reactive response to inspections
  • Master the design elements of a self-inspection programme: scope of coverage, time frame, and the flexibility to divide the programme into parts
  • Understand the qualification requirements for those performing self-inspections: being impartial and being competent are both essential
  • Clarify the complementary role of external expert audits: valuable, but not a substitute for self-inspection
  • Build a complete chain of records, reports and CAPA follow-up, from identifying a finding to verifying the improvement
8.1 Principle

Original Text

Self-inspections should be conducted in order to monitor implementation and compliance with GDP principles and to propose necessary corrective measures.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Dual Purpose of Self-Inspection

This section concisely sets out two functions of self-inspection:

  • Monitor implementation: Confirm that SOPs are actually followed, not merely "present" in the document management system. It is the mechanism that verifies the company does what it says.
  • Propose necessary corrective measures: Self-inspection is not only about finding problems; it is the engine that drives improvement. A self-inspection without follow-up CAPA is meaningless.

In essence, self-inspection is a company's own "preventive audit": the aim is to find and resolve problems before the regulatory authority does.

Analogy

Self-inspection is like a "pre-trip vehicle safety check". You do not wait until the car breaks down to check it; you regularly confirm that the brakes, tyres, lights and fuel level are all in order. GDP self-inspection follows the same logic: rather than waiting for the regulator to find problems, you check regularly yourself to make sure each distribution control is working. Fixing problems immediately (CAPA) is what keeps the car (the company) safe on the road.

Key Note

Self-inspection vs. external audit: Self-inspection (performed internally) is a mandatory GDP requirement; external audits (customer audits, third-party inspections) are external quality assurance tools. Their purposes differ: self-inspection is your own oversight of yourself, while an external audit is an assessment of you by stakeholders. External audits help uncover "blind spots", but GDP explicitly states that they cannot replace the internal self-inspection programme.

Practice Questions

  1. If a company undergoes three customer audits a year, does it still need to carry out GDP self-inspections? Why?
  2. If a self-inspection identifies deficiencies but no corrective action is taken, what consequences might this have in a GDP inspection?
8.2 Self-Inspections

Original Text

8.2.1 A self-inspection programme should be implemented covering all aspects of GDP and compliance with the regulations, guidelines and procedures within a defined time frame. Self-inspections may be divided into several individual self-inspections of limited scope.

8.2.2 Self-inspections should be conducted in an impartial and detailed way by designated competent company personnel. Audits by independent external experts may also be useful but may not be used as a substitute for self-inspection.

8.2.3 All self-inspections should be recorded. Reports should contain all the observations made during the inspection. A copy of the report should be provided to the management and other relevant persons. In the event that irregularities and/or deficiencies are observed, their cause should be determined and the corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) should be documented and followed up.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Four Design Elements of a Self-Inspection Programme

8.2.1 Programme design:

  • Full coverage (all aspects of GDP): The programme must cover all GDP chapters, including quality management, personnel, documentation, storage, transportation and outsourced activities
  • Defined time frame: The cycle for completing the programme must be clearly specified (for example, one full round per year)
  • Divided into limited scope: The programme may be run in parts by topic or department; it does not all have to be completed at once. For example, storage in Q1, transportation in Q2, documentation in Q3 and outsourced activities in Q4

8.2.2 Qualifications of those performing self-inspections: "Impartial" means inspectors should not inspect the operations they are responsible for; "competent" means they have a sound understanding of GDP requirements. Cross-auditing is a common practice.

8.2.3 Complete records and the CAPA chain: All observations (both positive findings and deficiencies) must be recorded. Management must receive a copy of the report, so that senior leadership is informed and supports the improvements. CAPA must be documented and tracked continuously; problems must not simply be dropped once found.

Analogy

A self-inspection programme is like an "annual health check programme". You do not need to do every test in one day; you schedule them by quarter (blood tests, imaging, eye and dental checks each get their own slot). "Impartial" is like seeing a different doctor rather than diagnosing yourself. "Management receives the report" is like the results going not only to you but also to your family doctor for the file. "CAPA follow-up" is like a doctor telling you your cholesterol is high and asking you to come back in three months to confirm it has improved, rather than just saying "I know" and leaving it there.

Key Note

Recording "all observations": 8.2.3 requires the report to include "all observations", not only deficiencies but also confirmation of good practices. This complete record matters during an inspection: inspectors will want to see your self-inspection reports, and a report that says only "everything is fine" with no deficiencies found will actually raise suspicion (no system is perfect). Appropriately disclosing minor issues together with their CAPA demonstrates effective self-monitoring.

External audits cannot replace the internal programme: Even if your customers audit you every quarter, GDP still requires you to have your own self-inspection programme performed by company personnel. External audits offer the advantage of an "outside perspective", while internal self-inspection offers "deep knowledge of the system"; the two complement rather than replace each other.

Practice Questions

  1. Your warehouse supervisor considers "self-inspection" to be his daily walk-through of the warehouse. Does this meet the GDP self-inspection requirement? What is the difference?
  2. The last self-inspection found that 10% of temperature data loggers were not calibrated on time. A CAPA plan was set, but a follow-up inspection three months later shows the problem persists. What escalation actions should be taken next?
  3. If the self-inspection report is requested during a later TFDA inspection, what format and content would you want it to have in order to demonstrate GDP compliance most effectively?

Practical Application

Your CDMO needs to establish a GDP self-inspection programme for its wholesale distribution business. The following is an annual programme framework that meets the requirements of 8.2:

Within 10 working days after each inspection: submit the report to the General Manager; within 30 days: CAPA plan approved; within 90 days: CAPA completed and effectiveness verified.

Section Summary

  • Purpose of self-inspection: To proactively monitor GDP implementation and drive improvement; it is the company's preventive audit of its own operations
  • Three elements of programme design: Full coverage of all aspects of GDP / a defined completion cycle (time frame) / division into several limited-scope inspections run in parts
  • Qualifications of inspectors: Inspections must be performed by personnel designated within the company who are "impartial and competent"; cross-auditing is a practical way to ensure impartiality
  • Role of external audits: They add value but cannot replace the internal self-inspection programme
  • Records and the CAPA chain: All observations (including positive ones) must be recorded → report submitted to management → deficiencies must have CAPA → CAPA must be tracked and verified
  • CDMO application: Design the self-inspection programme as a quarterly rotation through the GDP topics, designate personnel from departments not directly responsible to cross-audit, and set fixed timelines for reporting and CAPA

9.1 Principle

Original Text

9.1.1 It is the responsibility of the supplying wholesale distributor to protect medicinal products against breakage, adulteration, theft and to ensure that temperature conditions are maintained within acceptable limits during transport.

9.1.2 Regardless of the mode of transport, it should be possible to demonstrate that the medicines have not been exposed to conditions that may compromise their quality and integrity. A risk-based approach should be utilised when planning transportation.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Four Protection Aspects of Transport Responsibility

The four protection obligations in 9.1.1:

  • Breakage: appropriate cushioning packaging, handling restrictions, fragile-goods labelling
  • Adulteration: intact box sealing, no co-loading of medicinal products with non-pharmaceutical goods, clean vehicles
  • Theft: secure storage and tracking are needed, especially for narcotics and high-value biologics
  • Temperature: maintaining an acceptable temperature range throughout transport

The "demonstrate" requirement in 9.1.2: It is not enough to do it; you must be able to prove you did it. Temperature records, GPS tracking and handover confirmation documents are all tools for "demonstrating".

Risk-based approach: A transport plan is not one-size-fits-all. Different products (ambient vs. refrigerated vs. frozen), different routes (urban delivery vs. cross-border export) and different seasons (Taiwan's hot summer vs. winter) carry different levels of risk, so the controls needed also differ.

Analogy

Imagine you are sending a fresh cream cake (a refrigerated medicine) by courier. You (the supplying distributor) are responsible for making sure the cake arrives intact and cold; you cannot just say, "I handed it to the courier, the rest is not my concern." A "risk-based approach" is like choosing a delivery method by considering the distance, the weather, whether the recipient has a refrigerator, and how delicate (valuable) the cake is. Based on these factors, would you choose next-day refrigerated courier, ordinary ambient delivery, or drive it there yourself?

Key Note

Where the responsibility of the "supplying wholesale distributor" begins: Note that responsibility lies with the supplying wholesale distributor, not the receiving side. For a Taiwan CDMO, once the product leaves the warehouse, you remain the primary responsible party for the entire transport until it is delivered to the customer (even if you have contracted a third-party logistics provider). This is consistent with the outsourcing responsibility logic in Chapter 7.

9.2 Transportation

Original Text

9.2.1 The required storage conditions for medicinal products should be maintained during transportation within the defined limits as described on the outer packaging and/or relevant packaging information.

9.2.2 If a deviation such as temperature excursion or product damage has occurred during transportation, this should be reported to the distributor and recipient of the affected medicinal products. A procedure should also be in place for investigating and handling temperature excursions.

9.2.3 It is the responsibility of the wholesale distributor to ensure that vehicles and equipment used to distribute, store or handle medicinal products are suitable for their use and appropriately equipped to prevent exposure of the products to conditions that could affect their quality and packaging integrity.

9.2.4 There should be written procedures in place for the operation and maintenance of all vehicles and equipment involved in the distribution process, including cleaning and safety precautions.

9.2.5 Risk assessment of delivery routes should be used to determine where temperature controls are required. Equipment used for temperature monitoring during transport within vehicles and/or containers, should be maintained and calibrated at regular intervals.

9.2.6 Dedicated vehicles and equipment should be used, where possible, when handling medicinal products. Where non-dedicated vehicles and equipment are used procedures should be in place to ensure that the quality and integrity of the medicinal product will not be compromised.

9.2.7 Deliveries should be made to the address stated on the delivery note and into the care or the premises of the consignee. Medicinal products should not be left on alternative premises.

9.2.8 For emergency deliveries outside normal business hours, persons should be designated and written procedures should be available.

9.2.9 Where transportation is performed by a third party, the contract in place should encompass the requirements of Chapter 7. Transportation providers should be made aware by the wholesale distributor of the relevant transport conditions applicable to the consignment. Where the transportation route includes unloading and reloading or transit storage at a transportation hub, particular attention should be paid to temperature monitoring, cleanliness and the security of any intermediate storage facilities.

9.2.10 Provision should be made to minimise the duration of temporary storage while awaiting the next stage of the transportation route.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Ten Aspects of Transport Management

Extension of storage conditions (9.2.1): The storage conditions stated on the outer packaging (such as "2–8°C" or "15–25°C") remain valid throughout transport. This requirement seems basic but is often overlooked in practice: loading and unloading, the door being opened at traffic lights, and non-temperature-controlled handling on the last mile can all cause an excursion.

Responsibility for vehicles and equipment (9.2.3-9.2.4): The distributor must confirm that the vehicles and equipment used are "suitable for their purpose" and have written procedures for operation, maintenance and cleaning. This covers both owned and rented vehicles.

Route risk assessment (9.2.5): Not every route needs the same degree of temperature control. Variables in the risk assessment include distance, climate, season, product sensitivity and traffic conditions (congestion on Taiwan's expressways can raise the temperature in the cargo compartment).

Dedicated vs. non-dedicated equipment (9.2.6): If non-dedicated vehicles are used (for example, also carrying non-pharmaceutical goods), there must be written procedures ensuring product quality is not affected, such as segregation requirements, cleaning verification and procedures to exclude cross-contamination.

Analogy

9.2.7 requires that the product "must be delivered to the address stated on the delivery note and placed into the hands of the recipient, and not left at alternative premises". This is like fresh-food delivery from an online shop: frozen food cannot simply be left on your doorstep (it would melt in summer); someone must confirm receipt, or it must be left in a designated refrigerated receiving box. Medicine delivery is stricter: any handover not made to the designated place or person is a GDP failure, because the storage conditions at the "alternative location" cannot be confirmed.

Key Note

Two-way notification of temperature excursions (9.2.2): When a temperature excursion occurs, two parties must be notified: (1) the distributor responsible for the delivery; and (2) the recipient of the affected products. Why both? The recipient (such as a hospital pharmacy) needs to decide whether the affected batch can be used, while the distributor needs to investigate the root cause.

Written procedures for emergency deliveries (9.2.8): Emergencies (for example, a critically ill patient urgently needing medicine, with delivery late at night) operate outside normal procedures and are therefore more error-prone. There must be a list of designated on-call personnel and written procedures to ensure GDP compliance in emergencies as well.

Special risks of transit (9.2.9): Exported international shipments often have to be transferred by air or sea at transit hubs (such as Hong Kong or Singapore), and the period of interim storage in a warehouse is the most vulnerable point for temperature control. The transport contract must clearly specify the temperature control and security requirements for transit facilities.

Minimizing waiting time (9.2.10): The longer a product is held at a transit point, the higher the risk. Time buffers must therefore be considered when planning transport, to avoid goods waiting for long periods in a non-temperature-controlled environment.

Practical Application

Scenario: Your CDMO needs to export a batch of 2–8°C sterile injectables from its Hsinchu plant in Taiwan to Japan. The route is: plant → Taoyuan Airport (refrigerated truck, about 1.5 hours) → airport cold store (awaiting customs clearance, about 4 hours) → aircraft hold (flight of about 3 hours) → Narita Airport transit (about 2 hours) → customer warehouse (refrigerated truck, about 2 hours).

Under 9.2, you need to confirm:

  • 9.2.1: Is the temperature maintained at 2–8°C at every stage? Which stages carry the highest risk? (Answer: waiting at the airport for customs clearance, and the transit warehouse)
  • 9.2.5: Where are the temperature data loggers placed? How is the whole journey covered (including the aircraft hold)?
  • 9.2.9: Does the contract with the air freight company include the Chapter 7 requirements? Do the cold storage facilities used during airport customs waiting comply with GDP?
  • 9.2.10: Has the flight schedule minimized the waiting time at the airport? If a delay exceeds the insulation holding time, what is the contingency plan?

  1. Your logistics provider uses the same truck to deliver both medicines and food supplements (non-medicinal products). Under 9.2.6, what documents or procedures should you require from the provider?
  2. On delivery, you find that nobody is receiving goods at the customer's warehouse today. The driver cannot leave the medicines at the door; what is the correct procedure, and under which GDP requirement?
  3. A hospital in Taiwan urgently needs an emergency medicine from your plant in the middle of the night. What elements should your emergency delivery SOP include so that it complies with GDP outside working hours?
9.3 Containers, Packaging and Labelling

Original Text

9.3.1 Medicinal products should be transported in containers that have no adverse effect on the quality of the products, and that offer adequate protection from external influences, including contamination.

9.3.2 Selection of a container and packaging should be based on the storage and transportation requirements of the medicinal products; the space required for the amount of medicines; the anticipated external temperature extremes; the estimated maximum time for transportation including transit storage at customs; the qualification status of the packaging and the validation status of the shipping containers.

9.3.3 Containers should bear labels providing sufficient information on handling and storage requirements and precautions to ensure that the products are properly handled and secured at all times. The containers should enable identification of the contents of the containers and the source.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Six Factors in Container Selection (9.3.2)

9.3.2 lists six evaluation dimensions for container selection. This is a rare "explicit list" in GDP and is worth mastering item by item:

  1. Storage and transport requirements: the product's own temperature, light and humidity requirements (as stated on the outer packaging)
  2. Quantity of medicines: the space required affects the choice of container size
  3. Expected external temperature extremes: Taiwan's maximum summer temperature (up to 38°C) is a key input for designing the insulation system
  4. Maximum transit time (including customs waiting): this determines the design duration (protection period) of the insulated box and coolants
  5. Qualification status of the packaging: Has the packaging material been qualified?
  6. Validation status of the transport container: Has the insulated box or temperature-controlled container been validated to confirm it maintains the target temperature under specified conditions?

Analogy

Choosing a container for medicine transport is like designing a backpack for a polar expedition. You cannot take an ordinary city backpack to Antarctica; you choose the material, insulation layers and waterproofing based on the lowest temperature at the destination (external temperature extremes), the number of days (maximum transit time) and the list of items to carry (quantity and requirements of the medicines). And before departure, the backpack must be tested in a laboratory (verification/qualification) to confirm that it keeps the inside warm under simulated Antarctic conditions (validation).

Key Note

The subtle difference between qualification and validation: 9.3.2 distinguishes the "qualification status" of packaging from the "validation status" of transport containers. In GDP/GMP terminology:

  • Qualification: confirming that equipment or a system is correctly designed, correctly installed and operates correctly under static conditions (DQ/IQ/OQ)
  • Validation: confirming that the intended results are consistently achieved under actual conditions of use (PQ)

Validation of an insulated box normally includes a temperature distribution test under worst-case conditions (highest summer ambient temperature, longest expected transit time), confirming that all points inside the box stay within 2–8°C (or another target range). This report may be requested during customer audits and regulatory inspections.

The "identification function" of labelling (9.3.3): Container labels must satisfy all of the following: (1) handling instructions (how to handle); (2) identification of contents (what's inside); (3) identification of origin (where it's from). If the outside of a container only has a "fragile" mark but no batch number or manufacturer name, it does not meet the requirements of 9.3.3.

Practical Application

Your CDMO is to design an insulated box system for the export of a 2–8°C biologic. Based on the six factors in 9.3.2, what should you record in the specification?

  • External temperature extremes: maximum summer ambient temperature at Taoyuan Airport of 38°C (measured data or historical data from the weather bureau)
  • Maximum transit time: the longest scenario from the plant to the customer's warehouse in Japan, including delays (plant 1.5 h + airport wait 8 h + flight 4 h + transit 3 h + delivery 2 h = a design buffer of 18.5 hours)
  • Validation report: measured data showing that the insulated box maintains 2–8°C for more than 24 hours at an ambient temperature of 38°C
  • Seasonal configurations: the summer configuration (less coolant) and the winter configuration (to prevent freezing) must each be validated separately

Practice Questions

  1. Your customer asks you to use an insulated box that they supply. If they cannot provide a validation report for this box, how should you respond to remain compliant with GDP?
  2. Does a shipping box labelled only "FRAGILE" and the recipient's address meet the labelling requirements of 9.3.3? What is missing?
  3. If the summer and winter insulated box configurations (coolant quantity/arrangement) differ, how should they be managed so that warehouse staff do not mistakenly use the summer configuration in winter?
9.4 Products Requiring Controlled Conditions

Original Text

9.4.1 In relation to deliveries containing medicinal products requiring special conditions such as narcotics or psychotropic substances, the wholesale distributor should maintain a safe and secure supply chain for these products in accordance with requirements laid down in national legislation. There should be additional control systems in place for delivery of these products. There should be a protocol to address the occurrence of any theft.

9.4.2 Medicinal products comprising highly active and radioactive materials should be transported in safe, dedicated and secure containers and vehicles. The relevant safety measures should be in accordance with international agreements and national legislation.

9.4.3 For temperature-sensitive products, qualified equipment (e.g. thermal packaging, temperature-controlled containers or temperature controlled vehicles) should be used to ensure correct transport conditions are maintained between the manufacturer, wholesale distributor and customer.

9.4.4 If temperature-controlled vehicles are used, the temperature monitoring equipment used during transport should be maintained and calibrated at regular intervals. Temperature mapping under representative conditions should be carried out and should take into account seasonal variations, if applicable.

9.4.5 If requested, customers should be provided with information to demonstrate that products have complied with the temperature storage conditions.

9.4.6 If cool packs are used in insulated boxes, they need to be located such that the product does not come in direct contact with the cool pack. Staff must be trained on the procedures for assembly of the insulated boxes (seasonal configurations) and on the reuse of cool packs.

9.4.7 There should be a system in place to control the reuse of cool packs to ensure that incompletely cooled packs are not used in error. There should be adequate physical segregation between frozen and chilled ice packs.

9.4.8 The process for delivery of sensitive products and control of seasonal temperature variations should be described in a written procedure.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Tiers of Specially Controlled Products

9.4 provides tiered requirements for three categories of special products:

  1. Narcotic and psychotropic substances (9.4.1): In addition to GDP requirements, the extra controls of national legislation must be followed (in Taiwan, the Controlled Drugs Act); an anti-theft response plan is required
  2. Highly active and radioactive materials (9.4.2): dedicated, safe and secure containers and vehicles must be used; compliance with international agreements (such as the IAEA regulations for the transport of radioactive material) is required
  3. Temperature-sensitive products (9.4.3-9.4.8): the most heavily specified category and the scenario CDMOs handling sterile products meet most often; the focus is on qualified equipment, temperature mapping and coolant management

The importance of qualified equipment (9.4.3): Qualified equipment must be used. This means that before routine use, temperature-controlled vehicles or insulated containers need to complete a qualification study confirming that they maintain the target temperature range under specified conditions.

Analogy

Coolant management (9.4.6-9.4.7) has an intuitive analogy: imagine keeping seafood on ice. If the ice is not fully frozen (an incompletely cooled pack) when used, the cooling effect is greatly reduced and the seafood may spoil before arrival. 9.4.6 says "products must not be in direct contact with coolants", just as you would not let fish lie directly against ice that is not cold enough; on the one hand the ice may not be cold enough, and on the other hand an "overcooled" coolant may freeze the product (a serious problem for 2–8°C products). Training on seasonal configurations is like telling staff that the way to pack a refrigerator differs between summer and winter.

Key Note

Seasonal requirements for temperature mapping (9.4.4): Temperature mapping (a temperature distribution study) of temperature-controlled vehicles must take "seasonal variations" into account. Ambient temperature differences between summer and winter in Taiwan can exceed 20°C, and the temperature distribution inside the cargo compartment can be quite different in different seasons. Compliant mapping should include at least one study in summer (July-September, the highest-temperature scenario) and one in winter (January-February, the lowest-temperature scenario), or cover the full-year temperature range by simulating the worst case.

Obligation to provide temperature records to customers (9.4.5): "If requested" means the customer has the right to ask you for the temperature compliance records of the transport. This means you must retain complete temperature records, and the record format must be clearly explainable to the customer. It is advisable to proactively attach a brief temperature compliance confirmation to the standard delivery documents and to specify the retention period clearly in the contract.

The risk of "incompletely cooled" coolants (9.4.7): This is the detail most easily overlooked in practice. If reused coolant has not been completely re-frozen, it may look solid but have a melted core, with cooling capacity far below the standard. A system (such as colour coding, weight checks or a defined minimum freezing time after return) must be established to prevent this.

Core Concept: Precise Definitions of Supply Chain Roles

The boundary of Wholesale Distribution: The definition contains five verbs (procuring, holding, supplying, importing, exporting) but explicitly excludes "supplying to the public". This boundary determines whether you need to hold a wholesale distribution authorisation.

Analogy

It is like the difference between a wholesale market (selling to retailers) and a convenience store (selling to consumers): food wholesalers fall under wholesale regulations, while convenience stores fall under retail regulations. Wholesale Distribution is the control mechanism for the "wholesale end" of the medicinal product supply chain.

The Taiwan counterpart of Competent Authority: The Competent Authority in Taiwan is the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration (TFDA) under the Ministry of Health and Welfare. When PE 011-1 refers to requirements or authorisations of the "competent authority", in the Taiwan context this means the relevant TFDA rules. Understanding this correspondence helps you translate GDP requirements accurately into Taiwan regulatory compliance obligations.

Core Concept: The Dual-Track Definition of Falsified Medicinal Products

PE 011-1 provides two definitions of "falsified medicinal product", reflecting its global applicability:

Within the EEA (stricter and broader): It covers false representations in three aspects (identity, source and history), broken down into ingredients, excipients, strength, manufacturer, country of origin, MAH (Marketing Authorisation Holder), distribution records and so on. This is the essence of the EU Falsified Medicines Directive 2011/62/EU.

Outside the EEA (WHO definition, more concise): It focuses on "deliberately and fraudulently mislabelled with respect to identity and/or source", emphasising intent (deliberately and fraudulently).

Taiwan's position: Taiwan is outside the EEA, so in theory the WHO definition applies. In actual audits and SOP writing, however, we recommend using the stricter EEA three-aspect definition as the internal standard, because it is more comprehensive and consistent with the compliance requirements of major customers (European and US pharmaceutical companies).

Analogy

The spirit of Due Diligence is like doing your homework before lending money to a friend. In the GDP outsourcing context, due diligence is the supplier capability assessment (audit) in 7.2.2: you cannot sign a contract just because the other party looks honest. You must investigate their qualifications, facilities and track record, and only then decide whether and how to outsource and how to design the controls. The "Standard of care" protects you at the legal level: if you carried out due diligence to a reasonable standard, there is room to reduce your legal liability even when something goes wrong.

Key Note

A Manufacturing Licence includes the distribution authorisation: The words "and distribute" in the parentheses of the definition are very important: the manufacturing authorisation itself includes authorisation to distribute the medicinal products it covers. This echoes the core point of the Introduction to this guide: a manufacturer that holds a manufacturing authorisation and distributes its own products must comply with GDP (because the manufacturing authorisation already includes distribution, the distribution activities fall within the scope of the authorisation and GDP obligations follow).

Practical significance of a Free Zone: The definition says a free zone/bonded warehouse is "part of the customs territory but separated from other parts". For a Taiwan CDMO, if a customer's warehouse is located in an export processing zone, storage and distribution of products after entry must still comply with GDP; you cannot assume GDP does not apply just because it is a "special customs area".

Practical Application

Your CDMO warehouse receives a returned batch. The packaging looks intact, but the supplier cannot provide complete distribution chain documents (invoices and batch tracking records are both incomplete). Under the definition of falsified medicinal product:

  • The "history" aspect of the EEA definition includes "records and documents relating to the distribution channels used", so missing distribution documents may themselves be one of the features of a "falsified medicinal product"
  • Under GDP, this batch should be quarantined immediately and labelled "Suspected Falsified", the competent authority notified, and the batch must not be released
  • This scenario shows why GDP requires complete chain of custody documents: the absence of documents is itself a risk signal

Official Definitions

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Position of GDP within the Quality Assurance System

GDP is part of QA: The definition states clearly that GDP is "that part of quality assurance", covering the whole supply chain from the manufacturer to the pharmacy/person authorised to supply to the public. This definition establishes the boundaries of GDP:

  • Starting point: Site of manufacturer (the manufacturer's release point)
  • End point: The pharmacy or the person authorised to supply to the public
  • Everything in between: All wholesale distribution activities

GMP governs the manufacturing process, while GDP governs the whole flow from after manufacture to before retail; together they protect the integrity of the medicinal product from raw material to the patient.

The precise distinction between Qualification and Validation:

  • Qualification: Applies only to "equipment": confirming that the equipment works correctly and leads to the expected results
  • Validation: Broader, covering "procedure, process, equipment, material, activity and system"

In the GDP context, "qualification" of an insulated shipper confirms the equipment itself, while "validation" of the whole packing-distribution-delivery process confirms that the whole system achieves its objective under actual operating conditions.

Analogy

The four steps of Quality Risk Management (Assessment, Control, Communication, Review) can be explained with a "typhoon response" analogy: the weather bureau assesses the typhoon's path and intensity (assessment), the government decides whether to close offices and schools and distribute supplies (control), the media broadcast the information to citizens (communication), and after the typhoon the disaster prevention measures are evaluated for effectiveness (review). QRM applies this systematic thinking to medicinal product quality risks so that every decision has a basis rather than relying on intuition.

Key Note

The brevity of the Qualification definition vs. the detailed GMP definition: The PE 011-1 definition of Qualification is very brief (it mentions only equipment), whereas GMP documents (such as PIC/S Annex 15) define qualification in more detail (the DQ, IQ, OQ and PQ stages). In practice, the GDP concept of qualification should be carried out with the detailed GMP methodology rather than relying only on this short definition. This is also why GMP and GDP knowledge must be mastered together.

The "all aspects" of a Quality System: The definition says a Quality System is "the sum of all aspects", which means no single isolated SOP or procedure equals the Quality System. It is an integrated system comprising the organisational structure, responsibilities, procedures, training, monitoring, improvement mechanisms and so on. In a GDP inspection, the inspector evaluates the whole system, not individual SOPs.

Practical Application

Your CDMO is to set up a GDP-compliant quality system. Based on "Quality System = the sum of all aspects", your GDP quality system should include at least:

  • Organisation and responsibilities: Definition of the responsibilities of the Responsible Person
  • Document management: Control procedures for SOPs and records
  • Storage management: Temperature control, FIFO, quarantine area management
  • Transport management: Vehicle qualification, temperature monitoring, deviation handling
  • Outsourced activities management: Supplier qualification assessment, contract management, periodic audits
  • Self-inspection: Annual plan, records, CAPA tracking
  • Deviations and CAPA: The complete flow of detection, reporting, investigation, improvement and verification
  • Complaints and falsified medicines reporting: Identification, quarantine and notification procedures

Official Definitions

Temperature Range Summary

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Precise Definitions of Temperature Terms and Compliance Implications

"In a Refrigerator" = +2 to +8°C (exact boundaries): This is the official GDP definition of the refrigerated temperature range. Note that the upper limit is +8°C, not +10°C. When designing storage temperature alarms, the alarm upper limit must take this boundary into account. A common recommendation is to set the upper alarm limit at 7.5°C (leaving a 0.5°C buffer) so that the temperature does not exceed the 8°C specification limit before action is taken after the alarm is triggered.

The special definition of "Ambient": Ambient is not a fixed temperature value but "the storage temperature required for non-refrigerated medicinal products", which is determined by the specification of each individual product. It may be 15-25°C (equivalent to room temperature) or 15-30°C (a looser room temperature requirement). Ambient and Room Temperature cannot simply be equated.

Practical use of the "Cold or Cool" range: +8 to +15°C is rare in practice, but some herbal preparations or special dosage forms may require it. When designing warehouse zones, if you have such products you need a separate "cool store" between the cold room and the room temperature store.

Analogy

Temperature terms are like the temperature language of wine storage. When a sommelier says "this white wine is best served at 8-12°C", it corresponds to the GDP "Cold or Cool" range, and "red wine at room temperature of 18-22°C" corresponds to the GDP "Room Temperature". If you took "room temperature" to mean a Taiwan summer 32°C (the room temperature without air conditioning), the red wine would be spoiled long ago. The GDP definition of room temperature is 15-25°C, and the "natural room temperature" of an uncontrolled warehouse in Taiwan usually exceeds this range in summer, so Taiwan ambient-temperature warehouses must have air conditioning to maintain the GDP-defined "Room Temperature".

Key Note

The challenge of summer warehouse temperature control in Taiwan: GDP defines Room Temperature as 15-25°C. In the Taoyuan-Hsinchu-Miaoli area, summer highs in Taiwan can reach 38°C, and without sufficient air conditioning the indoor temperature can easily exceed the 25°C upper limit. For warehouses storing "room temperature" products, summer air conditioning capacity calculation and temperature control validation are the key to compliance. Since 2024, there have been several recorded TFDA inspection findings caused by inadequate summer temperature control.

Consistency of temperature definitions across documents: If your SOP says "refrigerated", confirm that it means +2 to +8°C (the PE 011-1 definition) and not someone's own idea of "about 4°C is fine". Consistent term definitions are the foundation of GDP compliance and a key tool for avoiding misunderstandings (as described in PE 011-1 Chapter 7).

Section Summary

  • Supply chain roles: Contract Giver, Contract Acceptor and Wholesale Distributor each have a clear regulatory definition that directly determines the allocation of responsibility
  • Boundary of wholesale distribution: It covers five activities (procuring, holding, supplying, importing and exporting) but excludes direct supply to the public; the distinction between Transport and Holding depends on whether the storage period is "justified"
  • Dual-track definition of falsified medicinal products: Within the EEA a three-aspect definition (identity, source, history) applies; outside the EEA the WHO two-element definition (deliberate false labelling of identity and/or source) applies; Taiwan CDMOs are advised to adopt the stricter EEA version
  • Qualification vs. Validation: Qualification applies to equipment, while Validation is broader (procedure, process, equipment, material, activity, system); in practice both must be carried out together with the GMP DQ/IQ/OQ/PQ framework
  • Exact temperature values: Deep frozen <−15°C / refrigerator +2 to +8°C / cold or cool +8 to +15°C / room temperature +15 to +25°C / Ambient = the individually required temperature for non-refrigerated products
  • Application for Taiwan CDMOs: Term definitions are the language foundation of SOPs and contracts; when defining alarm parameters, designing cold chain systems and drafting outsourcing contracts, cite the official PE 011-1 terms first to ensure consistent communication across departments and organisations
