Section 1: Scope

PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) — Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products | p2

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the scope of PIC/S Annex 1 (2022), including which product types and manufacturing processes it covers
  • Grasp why quality risk management (QRM) applies across the whole annex, and how
  • Understand how the principles of this annex can be extended to certain non-sterile products (such as creams, ointments and low bioburden biological intermediates)
  • Understand the documentation obligations a manufacturer must meet when it chooses to apply sterile manufacturing principles to non-sterile products
1 Scope

Original Text

The manufacture of sterile products covers a wide range of sterile product types (active substance, excipient, primary packaging material and finished dosage form), packed sizes (single unit to multiple units), processes (from highly automated systems to manual processes) and technologies (e.g. biotechnology, classical small molecule manufacturing systems and closed systems). This Annex provides general guidance that should be used in the design and control of facilities, equipment, systems and procedures used for the manufacture of all sterile products applying the principles of Quality Risk Management (QRM), to ensure that microbial, particulate and endotoxin/pyrogen contamination is prevented in the final product.

QRM applies to this document in its entirety and will not, normally, be referred to in specific paragraphs. Where specific limits or frequencies or ranges are specified, these should be considered as a minimum requirement. They are stated due to historical regulatory experience of issues that have been identified and have impacted the safety of patients.

"The intent of the Annex is to provide guidance for the manufacture of sterile products. However, some of the principles and guidance, such as contamination control strategy, design of premises, cleanroom classification, qualification, validation, monitoring and personnel gowning, may be used to support the manufacture of other products that are not intended to be sterile..."

Where a manufacturer elects to apply guidance herein to non-sterile products, the manufacturer should clearly document which principles have been applied and acknowledge that compliance with those principles should be demonstrated.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The scope of sterile products: This section makes clear that "sterile products" covers a very wide range. It is not limited to the finished dosage form (such as an injectable) but also includes active substances, excipients and primary packaging materials. This means that even a CDMO that only performs sterile filling may be indirectly affected by Annex 1 through its active substance suppliers.

Quality Risk Management (QRM): QRM is the underlying logic of the whole document. Annex 1 does not repeat QRM in every paragraph; instead it assumes that every decision must be grounded in risk management thinking. In practice, an inspector will ask "How did you assess the risk?" rather than simply checking that you performed a particular action.

Minimum requirement: The text states specifically that all numerical limits (such as particle count limits and environmental monitoring frequencies) are minimum requirements, drawn from many years of regulatory experience. Companies can, and should, set stricter standards on the basis of their QRM analysis.

Analogy

Imagine a set of "road safety regulations". They govern every vehicle on the road (cars, trucks and motorcycles alike), yet the same safe-driving principles (keeping to the correct side, giving way to pedestrians) can also be followed voluntarily by a cyclist, provided the cyclist records which principles they have chosen to follow.

PIC/S Annex 1 works the same way. The rules are designed for sterile products, but manufacturers of non-sterile products such as creams and ointments are welcome to borrow these principles to raise quality, on the condition that they document in writing which parts they have chosen to apply.

Key Notes

Conditions and risks of extended application: For manufacturers that choose to apply Annex 1 principles to non-sterile products, the documentation obligation is mandatory. During an inspection, claiming to follow Annex 1 without complete supporting documentation can actually lead to an inspection finding.

The "invisible presence" of QRM: Although QRM is not spelled out in every paragraph, it sits behind every inspection question. When facing an FDA or PIC/S inspection, the answers to "Why did you set this limit?" and "What do you do if it is exceeded?" all need to be supported by QRM documentation.

Practical Application

A CDMO produces both injectables (sterile) and ophthalmic ointments (non-sterile). Management wants the ophthalmic ointment process to also reference the Annex 1 cleanroom classification and environmental monitoring requirements, to strengthen customer confidence.

Under this section, that is permitted, but the CDMO must: (1) document in writing the specific principles it has chosen to apply; (2) confirm that it actually meets the requirements; and (3) state the scope of application clearly in its PQS documents. Without this documentation, an inspector could conclude that the company "claimed compliance but did not implement it".

Practice Questions

  1. PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) states that QRM applies throughout the whole document. What does this mean in day-to-day manufacturing management? Does it mean every operating SOP must be accompanied by a risk assessment document?
  2. If a manufacturer voluntarily applies Annex 1 principles to low bioburden biological intermediates, which documents is an inspector most likely to ask to review?
  3. How does the "minimum limit" required by the regulation differ from a manufacturer's own "action limit"? If your action limit is stricter than the regulatory requirement, what is the impact on an inspection?

Section Summary

  • PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) applies to the manufacture of all sterile products, covering active substances, excipients, primary packaging materials and finished dosage forms, and includes technologies ranging from highly automated to manual operations.
  • Quality risk management (QRM) is the implicit underlying framework of the whole of Annex 1: all decisions, limit setting and deviation investigations must be supported by QRM thinking.
  • All numerical limits in the document are "minimum requirements" derived from historical regulatory experience; manufacturers may set stricter internal standards but must not go below the regulatory requirements.
  • Annex 1 principles may be voluntarily extended to certain non-sterile products (such as creams and ointments), but the manufacturer must clearly document the principles applied and confirm that they are met.

PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) — Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products | p2–p5

Learning Objectives

  • Master the seven key principles of sterile product manufacturing (2.1–2.7) and understand their systematic logic and order of priority
  • Understand in depth the definition, constituent elements and establishment of the Contamination Control Strategy (CCS)
  • Understand the primacy of QRM in overall manufacturing management: the mindset of "design first, then monitor and verify"
  • Know the 16 elements of a CCS and how to establish and maintain a CCS in practice in a CDMO environment
  • Understand why "monitoring or testing alone cannot assure sterility": sterility assurance comes from the design of the whole system
2.1 - Key Areas for Sterile Manufacturing

Original Text

2.1 The manufacture of sterile products is subject to special requirements in order to minimize risks of microbial, particulate and endotoxin/pyrogen contamination. The following key areas should be considered:

  • i. Facility, equipment and process should be appropriately designed, qualified and/or validated and where applicable, subjected to ongoing verification according to the relevant sections of the Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) guide. The use of appropriate technologies (e.g. Restricted Access Barriers Systems (RABS), isolators, robotic systems, rapid/alternative methods and continuous monitoring systems) should be considered to increase the protection of the product from potential extraneous sources of endotoxin/pyrogen, particulate and microbial contamination such as personnel, materials and the surrounding environment, and assist in the rapid detection of potential contaminants in the environment and the product.
  • ii. Personnel should have adequate qualifications and experience, training and behaviour with a specific focus on the principles involved in the protection of sterile product during the manufacturing, packaging and distribution processes.
  • iii. Processes and monitoring systems for sterile product manufacture should be designed, commissioned, qualified, monitored and regularly reviewed by personnel with appropriate process, engineering and microbiological knowledge.
  • iv. Raw materials and packaging materials should be adequately controlled and tested to ensure that level of bioburden and endotoxin/pyrogen are suitable for use.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three Contamination Types:

  • Microbial Contamination: Living microorganisms such as bacteria, moulds and yeasts pose a direct infection risk
  • Particulate Contamination: Visible or sub-visible particles may cause venous embolism or tissue damage
  • Endotoxin/Pyrogen Contamination: Fragments of the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria that still trigger a fever response even after the cells have died; this is the type of contamination that is hardest to eliminate completely in sterile manufacturing

Barrier Technology First: RABS (restricted access barrier systems) and isolators represent the technical trend in modern sterile manufacturing. Annex 1 (2022) explicitly expects manufacturers to "consider" these technologies, which indirectly signals that the traditional open cleanroom design is no longer regarded by regulators as the best option.

Analogy

The four key considerations in sterile manufacturing are like running a top-class operating theatre:

  • Hardware design (facilities, equipment) = the theatre's positive-pressure air conditioning, laminar airflow and smooth floors that harbour no microbes
  • Personnel = the surgeon's scrub-in procedure and the way surgical gowns are worn
  • Process monitoring = the intra-operative vital-signs monitors
  • Control of materials = sterilisation qualification of the surgical instruments and biological indicators

If any one of them is missing, the patient can still be infected even when the other three are fully in place. Sterile manufacturing is the same: only systematic, holistic control can deliver sterility assurance.

Key Notes

Regulatory implications of isolator vs RABS: Annex 1 (2022) recommends isolators and RABS (restricted access barrier systems) more actively than the previous version. For a CDMO whose existing facility still uses traditional open filling in Grade A, the CCS should explain why the existing design still provides adequate contamination protection and attach the supporting risk assessment documents.

Special emphasis on personnel behaviour: Note the word "behaviour". This is not only a training issue; it also concerns the culture and habits of personnel in the cleanroom. Regulators increasingly focus on the contamination risk caused by "behavioural deviations".

Practice Questions

  1. Why is endotoxin/pyrogen contamination harder to control than microbial contamination? Can terminal sterilisation eliminate the pyrogen problem?
  2. A new aseptic filling line is evaluating RABS versus an isolator. From the Annex 1 perspective, what are the main differences between the two?
2.2 - QRM and Design Priority

Original Text

2.2 Processes, equipment, facilities and manufacturing activities should be managed in accordance with QRM principles to provide a proactive means of identifying, scientifically evaluating and controlling potential risks to quality. Where alternative approaches are used, these should be supported by appropriate rationale, risk assessment and mitigation, and should meet the intent of this Annex.

In the first instance, QRM priorities should include appropriate design of the facility, equipment and processes, followed by the implementation of well-designed procedures, and finally application of monitoring systems as the element that demonstrates that the design and procedures have been correctly implemented and continue to perform in line with expectations.

"Monitoring or testing alone does not give assurance of sterility."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The three-level order of priority for sterility assurance:

1. Design - the most important
↓
2. Procedures
↓
3. Monitoring - verifies that the design is effective

This order of priority overturns the intuition of many manufacturers. Here "monitoring" is the last layer: it only confirms that the first two layers are working, and is not the main means of contamination protection. If a manufacturer relies on large volumes of environmental monitoring data to "prove" the environment is clean, rather than "preventing" contamination through good design, this is not accepted under the philosophy of Annex 1.

Analogy

It is like building a waterproof house:

  • Design first: choose the right roof pitch, waterproofing materials and drainage design (this is what actually keeps the house waterproof)
  • Procedures: SOPs for regular maintenance, checking joints and replacing aged materials
  • Monitoring: place hygrometers in the house; if they show dry conditions over the long term, both the design and the procedures are working

If a house's waterproofing strategy is "put in lots of hygrometers and deal with it urgently whenever it gets damp", that is putting the cart before the horse. The logic of sterility assurance is exactly the same.

Key Notes

"Monitoring cannot assure sterility" is one of the most important philosophical statements in Annex 1. Its regulatory meaning is this: even if all environmental monitoring results are acceptable, if the process design itself is flawed (for example, inadequate Grade A protection or inappropriate personnel flows), the product cannot be claimed to be "safe and sterile".

This is also why PDA TR22 (environmental monitoring) stresses that "monitoring is a verification tool, not a control tool".

Practical Application

While preparing for a routine inspection, a CDMO is asked by the inspector to review the historical environmental monitoring (EM) trends. The company presents 12 consecutive months of EM records with no excursions and claims this proves that the aseptic environment is well controlled.

Following the logic of Annex 1, a reasonable follow-up question from the inspector would be: "Are these passing results due to a well-designed system, or because you only fill under conditions that pass?" In other words, good design documentation and validation reports are the real sterility assurance; EM is only supporting evidence.

2.3 - Contamination Control Strategy (CCS): The Core Clause

Original Text

2.3 A Contamination Control Strategy (CCS) should be implemented across the facility in order to define all critical control points and assess the effectiveness of all the controls (design, procedural, technical and organisational) and monitoring measures employed to manage risks to medicinal product quality and safety. The combined strategy of the CCS should establish robust assurance of contamination prevention. The CCS should be actively reviewed and, where appropriate, updated and should drive continual improvement of the manufacturing and control methods. Its effectiveness should form part of the periodic management review. Where existing control systems are in place and are appropriately managed, these may not require replacement but should be referenced in the CCS and the associated interactions between systems should be understood.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - In-Depth Analysis of the CCS

What is a Contamination Control Strategy (CCS)?

A CCS is a "living", integrated document that systematically describes how a manufacturer identifies all potential sources of contamination and how each control measure (design, procedural, technical and organisational) works together to ensure product quality and patient safety.

A CCS covers four categories of control measures:

  • Design Controls: cleanroom classification, airflow design, HVAC systems, choice of RABS/isolator
  • Procedural Controls: gowning procedures, cleaning and disinfection SOPs, rules of personnel behaviour
  • Technical Controls: automated systems, closed systems, filter design
  • Organisational Controls: training programmes, supplier qualification, change management

"Part of the periodic management review": This is a new requirement. A CCS is not just a document; its effectiveness must be reviewed periodically at management level, similar to the annual product quality review (APR) expected under ICH Q10.

Analogy - CCS = a Hospital Infection Control Plan

A CCS (contamination control strategy) is like a hospital's infection control plan.

A good hospital does not simply say "we have a hand-washing rule". It has a complete plan covering: ward design (negative-pressure isolation rooms), personnel management (hand-washing procedures, mask rules), environmental cleaning and disinfection (frequency, choice of chemicals), supply chain control (purchasing specifications for sterile consumables), a monitoring system (tracking of hospital-acquired infection rates), and the investigation and CAPA process when a cluster of infections occurs.

The same is true of a pharmaceutical plant's CCS: it is the "master plan" covering all contamination control activities in the plant, making sure every part is coordinated and mutually supportive and keeps improving.

Key Notes

The CCS is one of the most important new concepts in Annex 1 (2022). The previous Annex 1 (2008 version) did not explicitly require a CCS. The 2022 revision upgraded the CCS from a recommended concept to a regulatory requirement, which is one of the biggest changes in this revision.

Existing systems do not all need to be replaced: The text states clearly that where existing control systems are in place and well managed, they "do not need to be replaced", but they must be referenced in the CCS and the interactions between the systems must be understood. This is favourable wording for established manufacturers, but it also requires them to be able to explain clearly how the individual control measures complement one another.

The regulatory obligation of "continual improvement": The CCS must drive continual improvement, not merely maintain the status quo. This means the periodic review meetings of the CCS must produce improvement actions as an output.

2.4-2.5 - Contamination Sources & CCS Elements: The 16 Elements of a CCS

Original Text

2.4 Contamination control and steps taken to minimize the risk of contamination from microbial, endotoxin/pyrogen and particle sources includes a series of interrelated events and measures. These are typically assessed, controlled and monitored individually but their collective effectiveness should be considered together.

2.5 The development of the CCS requires detailed technical and process knowledge. Potential sources of contamination are attributable to microbial and cellular debris (e.g. pyrogen, endotoxin) as well as particulate (e.g. glass and other visible and sub-visible particles).

Elements to be considered within a CCS should include (but are not limited to):

  1. design of both the plant and processes including the associated documentation;
  2. premises and equipment;
  3. personnel;
  4. utilities;
  5. raw material controls — including in-process controls;
  6. product containers and closures;
  7. vendor approval — such as key component suppliers, sterilisation of components and single use systems (SUS), and critical service providers;
  8. management of outsourced activities and availability/transfer of critical information between parties, e.g. contract sterilisation services;
  9. process risk management;
  10. process validation;
  11. validation of sterilisation processes;
  12. preventative maintenance — maintaining equipment, utilities and premises (planned and unplanned maintenance) to a standard that will ensure there is no additional risk of contamination;
  13. cleaning and disinfection;
  14. monitoring systems - including an assessment of the feasibility of the introduction of scientifically sound, alternative methods that optimize the detection of environmental contamination;
  15. prevention mechanisms — trend analysis, detailed investigation, root cause determination, corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) and the need for comprehensive investigational tools;
  16. continuous improvement based on information derived from the above.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - Interpreting the 16 Elements of a CCS

The concept of "collective effectiveness":

Each control measure can be assessed individually, but real contamination protection comes from the combined effect of all measures working together as a system. It is like a chain: every link may be strong on its own, but if one link is weak, the strength of the whole chain equals that of its weakest link.

Structural analysis of the 16 elements (grouped by category):

  • Hardware and design (1-4): plant design, facilities and equipment, personnel, utilities
  • Material control (5-8): raw material control, container closure systems, supplier qualification, management of outsourced activities
  • Process quality (9-11): process risk management, process validation, sterilisation validation
  • Maintenance and cleaning (12-13): preventive maintenance, cleaning and disinfection
  • Monitoring and improvement (14-16): monitoring systems, preventive mechanisms (CAPA), continual improvement

Note that supplier qualification (vii) and management of outsourced activities (viii) are listed explicitly as CCS elements for the first time. This matters especially for a CDMO: the quality control of your single-use systems (SUS) supplier is also part of your CCS.

Analogy

Imagine the 16 elements of a CCS as a restaurant's food safety management system (HACCP):

  • Kitchen design (hardware), ingredient purchasing specifications (raw material control), supplier audits (supplier qualification), cooking temperature control (process validation), a cleaning and disinfection plan, and staff hygiene training: only all of these together make up complete food safety assurance.
  • Spot-testing ingredients alone (the equivalent of "monitoring") is not enough, and neither is having HACCP documents that are not put into practice. The same applies to a CCS: it must be systematic, living and executable.

Key Notes

Alternative methods: Element xiv specifically mentions evaluating "scientifically justified alternative monitoring methods". This is an implicit encouragement of rapid microbiological methods (RMM). The traditional 7-14 day culture-media methods increasingly struggle to meet the need for real-time deviation response, and RMM is the future trend.

CAPA as a core component of the CCS: Element xv lists CAPA (corrective and preventive action) as one of the preventive mechanisms of the CCS. This means the effectiveness of the CAPA system should be assessed as part of the CCS review: merely completing the CAPA records is not enough; it must be verified that the root cause has actually been resolved.

Practical Application

A CDMO switches from traditional glass vial filling to single-use systems (SUS). Which elements need particular attention when updating the CCS?

  • Element vii (supplier qualification): evaluate the SUS supplier's sterilisation process qualification and biocompatibility documentation
  • Element xi (sterilisation validation): SUS are usually pre-sterilised by the supplier, so the applicability of the supplier's sterilisation validation data must be transferred and confirmed
  • Element viii (outsourced activities): the supplier's Quality Agreement must clearly define the boundaries of responsibility
  • Element xii (preventive maintenance): SUS do not need traditional CIP/SIP, but the batch set-up verification procedure needs to be updated

Practice Questions

  1. Given the concept of "collective effectiveness" in the CCS elements, how should the scope of the follow-up investigation be defined when an inspection finds that a single control measure is inadequate (for example, an environmental monitoring excursion)?
  2. If a plant's preventive maintenance (PM) programme is found during a CCS review to have only a 70% completion rate, how does this affect the overall CCS effectiveness assessment?
2.6-2.7 - CCS Lifecycle & Sterility Assurance: The Final Principles

Original Text

2.6 The CCS should consider all aspects of contamination control with ongoing and periodic review resulting in updates within the pharmaceutical quality system as appropriate. Changes to the systems in place should be assessed for any impact on the CCS before and after implementation.

2.7 The manufacturer should take all steps and precautions necessary to assure the sterility of the products manufactured within its facilities. Sole reliance for sterility or other quality aspects should not be placed on any terminal process or finished product test.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Lifecycle management of the CCS:

Clause 2.6 establishes that the CCS is not a static document but a "living document" that evolves continually as the plant operates. The key requirements include:

  • Assessment before a change: any process change (such as an equipment replacement or an SOP revision) must be assessed for its impact on the CCS before implementation
  • Confirmation after a change: after implementation, it must also be confirmed that the CCS is still effective
  • Periodic review: the overall effectiveness of the CCS is evaluated periodically (usually annually) as an input to the PQS management review

The philosophical meaning of 2.7: sterility assurance comes from the whole system:

"Sterility must not rely on terminal processing or finished product testing alone" is the core statement of the whole of Annex 1. The statistical limitations of the sterility test are well known: it is impossible to perform destructive testing on an entire batch of finished product, and the representativeness of sampling is limited. Sterility assurance must therefore be built on the reliability of the entire manufacturing system.

Analogy

"Sterility cannot be assured by finished product testing alone" is like the fact that food safety cannot be ensured only by a final food safety spot check.

A lunch-box factory cannot possibly run microbial tests on every single lunch box (it would be destructive and extremely costly). Real food safety assurance comes from the whole system: raw material control, a clean production environment, health management of the cooks, temperature control and so on. The "safety" of each lunch box is "manufactured in", not "tested out".

The same is true of the sterility of injectables: sterility is "built in" through a complete manufacturing system and is not "tested out" by the sterility test at the end of the batch.

Key Notes

The link between change control and the CCS: Clause 2.6 requires the CCS impact to be assessed both before and after a change, which means the plant's change control procedure (Change Control SOP) must include a "CCS impact assessment" step. This was not explicitly required in the previous Annex 1 and is an important addition in the 2022 version.

Statistical limitations of the sterility test: The sample size of a typical sterility test is very small (usually 20 units), so statistically it cannot guarantee that the whole batch is sterile. Even if the sterility test passes, a batch should still not be released if there was a known contamination event during the process (such as an environmental monitoring excursion). This is exactly the spirit that 2.7 conveys.

Practical Application

A CDMO plans to change the model of HEPA filter in its Grade A filling area (from a different manufacturer, but with the same specification). Under the requirements of 2.6, which assessment steps are necessary?

  1. Before the change: assess the impact of the new HEPA filter model on the "design controls" element of the CCS; confirm supplier qualification; update the relevant sections of the CCS
  2. During implementation: perform the full post-installation integrity test of the HEPA filter (DOP/PAO test) and re-confirm the cleanroom classification
  3. After implementation: confirm that environmental monitoring data remain within the acceptable range; update the CCS document to reflect the current state

Practice Questions

  1. If an environmental monitoring excursion (EM excursion) occurs during the filling of a batch, but the subsequent sterility test result is negative (pass), can the batch be released? Based on the spirit of 2.7, what is the argument for your decision?
  2. How can the periodic CCS review mechanism be designed so that it is effective and not merely a formality? What input data should be included in the annual CCS effectiveness assessment?

Section Summary

  • The core principles of sterile manufacturing cover four areas, facility/equipment design, personnel management, process monitoring and control of materials, and none of them can be omitted
  • The QRM order of priority is: design > procedures > monitoring. Monitoring only verifies that the first two are effective and is not the main means of contamination protection
  • The Contamination Control Strategy (CCS) is the most important new core concept in Annex 1 (2022); it requires manufacturers to establish an integrated contamination control plan covering 16 elements and to keep it maintained and updated
  • The CCS is a "living document": its impact must be assessed before and after every system change, and it must be an input to the periodic management review
  • Sterility assurance cannot rely on terminal sterilisation or finished product sterility testing alone: it is the combined expression of the reliability of the entire manufacturing system, and must be "built in" through complete system design rather than "tested out"

3.1 — PQS Specific Requirements for Sterile Manufacturing

Original Text

3.1 The manufacture of sterile products is a complex activity that requires specific controls and measures to ensure the quality of products manufactured. Accordingly, the manufacturer's PQS should encompass and address the specific requirements of sterile product manufacture and ensure that all activities are effectively controlled so that the risk of microbial, particulate and endotoxin/pyrogen contamination is minimized in sterile products. In addition to the PQS requirements detailed in Chapter 1 of the GMP Guide (Part I — Basic Requirements for Medicinal Products), the PQS for sterile product manufacture should also ensure that:

  • i. An effective risk management system is integrated into all areas of the product life cycle with the aim to minimize microbial contamination and to ensure the quality of sterile products manufactured.
  • ii. The manufacturer has sufficient knowledge and expertise in relation to the products manufactured and the equipment, engineering and manufacturing methods employed that have an impact on product quality.
  • iii. Root cause analysis of procedural, process or equipment failure is performed in such a way that the risk to product is correctly identified and understood so that suitable corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) are implemented.
  • iv. Risk management is applied in the development and maintenance of the CCS, to identify, assess, reduce/eliminate (where applicable) and control contamination risks. Risk management should be documented and should include the rationale for decisions taken in relation to risk reduction and acceptance of residual risk.
  • v. Senior management should effectively oversee the state of control throughout the facility and product lifecycle. Risk management outcome should be reviewed regularly as part of the on-going quality management, during change, in the event of a significant emerging problem, and during the periodic product quality review.
  • vi. Processes associated with the finishing, storage and transport of sterile products should not compromise the sterile product. Aspects that should be considered include: container integrity, risks of contamination and avoidance of degradation by ensuring that products are stored and maintained in accordance with the registered storage conditions.
  • vii. Persons responsible for the certification/release of sterile products have appropriate access to manufacturing and quality information and possess adequate knowledge and experience in the manufacture of sterile products and the associated critical quality attributes. This is in order to allow such persons to determine if the sterile products have been manufactured in accordance with the registered specifications and approved process and are of the required quality.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The role of the PQS in sterile manufacturing is like an airline's Safety Management System (SMS):

  • General PQS (Chapter 1) = the basic flight safety rules every airline must follow
  • Sterile-manufacturing-specific PQS (Section 3) = the extra safety protocols required for long-haul ocean crossings or special missions (such as medical transport)

Key analogy points:

  • Requirement ii (knowledge and expertise) = flying a long-haul aircraft requires an additional type rating; a basic pilot license is not enough
  • Requirement v (Senior Management) = the airline CEO cannot focus only on financial statements and must receive regular reports from the safety committee
  • Requirement vii (release personnel) = the captain's sign-off before departure is not just a ticked box but a professional decision based on sound technical judgment

Key Notes

Why requirement v (Senior Management oversight) matters:

Annex 1 (2022) explicitly writes the oversight responsibilities of Senior Management into the regulation. This is not a formality: inspectors sometimes ask about the content and frequency of management review and the level of management involvement. If management review records show that senior leaders merely attended as a formality, without substantive discussion of risk management outcomes, this may be cited as a PQS deficiency.

Requirement iv (rationale for accepting residual risk): The regulation allows a certain level of "residual risk" to be accepted, but there must be a documented rationale explaining why that residual risk is considered acceptable. "We have always done it this way" or "we have never had a problem before" is not a valid rationale. The rationale must be based on scientific evaluation and historical data.

Requirement vi (downstream processes) is an easily overlooked blind spot: Many manufacturers focus on the aseptic filling process, but Annex 1 explicitly requires that packaging, storage and transport must not compromise sterility either. Container Closure Integrity (CCI) testing and cold-chain transport controls both fall within this scope.

Practical Application

A CDMO is preparing for an initial EU GMP inspection. The inspector is paying particular attention to compliance with Section 3. The following is the list of documents most likely to be reviewed:

  • Requirement i: The quality risk management procedure (QRM SOP) and records of its application to sterile processes (FMEA or risk assessment reports)

3.2 — Non-Conformities and Batch Release Investigation

Original Text

3.2 All non-conformities, such as sterility test failures, environmental monitoring excursions or deviations from established procedures should be adequately investigated before certification/release of the batch. The investigation should determine the potential impact upon process and product quality and whether any other processes or batches are potentially impacted. The reason for including or excluding a product or batch from the scope of the investigation should be clearly justified and recorded.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three specific types of non-conformity:

  • Sterility test failure: The most serious outcome. It usually triggers a major investigation and batch rejection/destruction, unless a systematic sterility testing laboratory error can be confirmed
  • Environmental monitoring (EM) excursion: Particle counts or microbial monitoring results exceeding the action limit. Note: an action limit is not a specification, so an excursion does not necessarily mean the batch automatically fails, but it must be investigated
  • Deviation from procedures: Operators not following the SOP, such as omitted gowning steps or process parameters out of range

Four core elements of the investigation:

  1. Assessment of the potential impact on the process and product quality
  2. Whether other processes or batches are affected (batch extension assessment)
  3. A clear statement of which batches are included in or excluded from the scope of the investigation, with the reasons
  4. Batches can only be released after the investigation is complete

Analogy

The requirement to investigate before batch release is like the investigation principles of an air accident investigation body (such as the NTSB):

After a minor incident, an aircraft cannot be considered cleared simply because the visible damage has been repaired and it is able to fly. The investigation must establish: (1) the root cause; (2) whether other aircraft of the same type are affected; (3) a clear statement of which aircraft are affected and which are not, with the supporting rationale. Until the investigation is complete, all affected aircraft are grounded.

The same logic applies to deviation investigations for sterile batches: a batch must not be released until the investigation is complete, and the investigation conclusion must clearly define which batches are within the scope of the investigation, with a documented rationale.

Key Notes

"Clearly documenting the rationale for excluding batches" is a frequent finding:

A common problem in practice: when a deviation occurs, the site concludes after investigation that "only Batch A is affected, and Batches B and C are not", but the investigation report does not clearly explain why B and C were excluded. Annex 1 explicitly requires that the reasons for exclusion be "clearly explained and documented".

Typical handling flow for an environmental monitoring excursion:

  1. Immediate notification: notify QA and production management during the same shift as the excursion
  2. Enhanced sampling: perform intensified monitoring around the excursion location
  3. Deviation investigation: open a deviation report and investigate the root cause (identify the contamination source)
  4. Batch impact assessment: assess whether batches filled during the excursion period are affected
  5. CAPA: define corrective actions (such as increased disinfection frequency or review of personnel behavior)
  6. Batch release decision: made by qualified release personnel based on the complete investigation conclusions

Special challenge for CDMOs: If a deviation affects batches for several clients at the same time, each client's Quality Agreement may set different requirements for notification obligations and depth of investigation, which must be managed carefully.

4.1–4.4 Cleanroom Fundamentals and Grade Overview: General Premises & Grade Overview

Original Text

4.1 The manufacture of sterile products should be carried out in appropriate cleanrooms, entry to which should be through change rooms that act as airlocks for personnel and airlocks for equipment and materials. Cleanrooms and change rooms should be maintained to an appropriate cleanliness standard and supplied with air which has passed through filters of an appropriate efficiency. Controls and monitoring should be scientifically justified and should effectively evaluate the state of environmental conditions of cleanrooms, airlocks and pass-through hatches.

4.2 The various operations of component preparation, product preparation and filling should be carried out with appropriate technical and operational separation measures within the cleanroom or facility to prevent mix up and contamination.

4.3 Restricted Access Barrier Systems (RABS) or isolators are beneficial in assuring required conditions and minimizing microbial contamination associated with direct human interventions in the critical zone. Their use should be considered in the CCS. Any alternative approaches to the use of RABS or isolators should be justified.

4.4 For the manufacture of sterile products there are four grades of cleanroom/zone:

Grade A The critical zone for high-risk operations (e.g. aseptic processing line, filling zone, stopper bowl, open primary packaging or for making aseptic connections under the protection of first air). Normally, such conditions are provided by a localised airflow protection, such as unidirectional airflow workstations within RABS or isolators. The maintenance of unidirectional airflow should be demonstrated and qualified across the whole of the grade A area. Direct intervention into the grade A area by operators should be minimized by premises, equipment, process and procedural design.
Grade B For aseptic preparation and filling, this is the background cleanroom for grade A (where it is not an isolator). Air pressure differences should be continuously monitored. Cleanrooms of lower grade than grade B can be considered where isolator technology is used (see paragraph 4.20).
Grade C and D These are cleanrooms used for carrying out less critical stages in the manufacture of aseptically filled sterile products or as a background for isolators. They can also be used for the preparation/filling of terminally sterilised products.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Four Cleanroom Grades

Grade A: The highest grade, the critical zone where product is directly exposed. Filling areas, dispensing areas and exposed primary packaging components all fall into this category. Unidirectional airflow (UDAF) must be maintained, and a RABS or isolator must provide barrier protection.

Grade B: The background environment for Grade A (where an isolator is not used). It is the supporting environment surrounding the Grade A cleanroom. Pressure differentials must be continuously monitored.

Grade C: A supporting environment for less critical aseptic operations, for example compounding rooms and solution preparation before filtration. It can also serve as the background environment for a closed isolator.

Grade D: The lowest grade, used for preliminary preparation or as the filling background for terminally sterilised products. It can also serve as the minimum background for a closed isolator.

Analogy: Layers of Protection in an Operating Theatre

Imagine the design of a top-tier operating theatre:

  • Grade A = the sterile field where the scalpel meets the wound (the strictest area, completely isolated)
  • Grade B = the operating room itself (the space where staff work in sterile gowns)
  • Grade C = the scrub room and anteroom (where preparation is completed)
  • Grade D = the theatre corridor (where support materials are temporarily held)

Each step inward requires changing garments, disinfecting and passing through an airlock. This is exactly the design logic of Annex 1.

Key Point: Strategic Choice Between RABS and Isolator

The significance of clause 4.3 is this: using a RABS or isolator is no longer just an option but a technical measure that must be considered in the CCS. If you choose not to use one, the CCS must clearly state the alternative approach and its justification.

The spirit of the 2022 revision of Annex 1 is to replace human control with engineering control, and RABS and isolators embody this principle. Any technology that reduces direct human intervention in the Grade A area is the direction regulators encourage.

Practical Application: CDMO Facility Planning Strategy

For a CDMO, facility grade design directly affects whether a client's product can be accepted. When taking on high-risk biological products (such as live virus vaccines), a negative pressure isolator may be needed to protect both the product and the operators. Choosing an isolator over a RABS requires higher upfront investment, but it allows the background environment to drop from Grade B to Grade D (or even Grade C), which greatly reduces HVAC operating costs and improves competitiveness in TCO (total cost of ownership).

4.5–4.11 Physical Design and Material Transfer: Physical Design & Material Transfer

Original Text

4.5 In cleanrooms and critical zones, all exposed surfaces should be smooth, impervious and unbroken in order to minimize the shedding or accumulation of particles or micro-organisms.

4.6 To reduce accumulation of dust and to facilitate cleaning there should be no recesses that are difficult to clean effectively, therefore projecting ledges, shelves, cupboards and equipment should be kept to a minimum. Doors should be designed to avoid recesses that cannot be cleaned. Sliding doors may be undesirable for this reason.

4.7 Materials used in cleanrooms, both in the construction of the room and for items used within the room, should be selected to minimize generation of particles and to permit the repeated application of cleaning, disinfectant and sporicidal agents where used.

4.8 Ceilings should be designed and sealed to prevent contamination from the space above them.

4.9 Sinks and drains should be prohibited in the grade A and grade B areas. In other cleanrooms, air breaks should be fitted between the machine or sink and the drains. Floor drains in lower grade cleanrooms should be fitted with traps or water seals designed to prevent back flow and should be regularly cleaned, disinfected and maintained.

4.10 The transfer of equipment and materials into and out of the cleanrooms and critical zones is one of the greatest potential sources of contamination. Any activities with the potential to compromise the cleanliness of cleanrooms or the critical zone should be assessed and if they cannot be eliminated, appropriate controls should be implemented.

4.11 The transfer of materials, equipment, and components into the grade A or B areas should be carried out via a unidirectional process. Where possible, items should be sterilised and passed into these areas through double-ended sterilisers (e.g. through a double-door autoclave or depyrogenation oven/tunnel) sealed into the wall. Where sterilisation upon transfer is not possible, a procedure which achieves the same objective of not introducing contamination should be validated and implemented, (e.g. using an effective transfer disinfection process, rapid transfer systems for isolators or, for gaseous or liquid materials, a bacteria-retentive filter). The removal of items from the grade A and B areas should be carried out via a separate unidirectional process. If this is not possible, time-based separation of movement (incoming/exiting material) by procedure should be considered.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Five Principles of Physical Design

  • Smooth and seamless (4.5): Surfaces must have no cracks or pores; otherwise microorganisms can remain and multiply in the crevices even after cleaning.
  • No dead spots (4.6): Every protruding structure (ledges, shelves) is a cleaning dead spot, so cleanroom design should be minimalist. The tracks of sliding doors are also a source of contamination, so they are not recommended.
  • Chemical resistance (4.7): Cleaning agents, disinfectants and sporicides are used repeatedly, so materials must withstand these strong chemicals without degrading or shedding particles.
  • Ceiling sealing (4.8): The space above a suspended ceiling usually contains piping and fibres; if it is not properly sealed, dust can fall into the cleanroom through gaps in the ceiling.
  • No drains (4.9): Sinks and drains are prohibited in Grade A/B areas, because they are breeding grounds for microorganisms (especially Gram-negative bacteria).

Key Concepts: Three Ways to Transfer Materials

1. Double-ended steriliser: For example a double-door autoclave or a depyrogenation tunnel oven built into the wall. Materials are loaded from the Grade C side and, after sterilisation, unloaded from the Grade B side. Transfer and sterilisation happen in one step, which makes this the ideal method.

2. Rapid Transfer Port (RTP): Also known as an Alpha-Beta rapid transfer port, designed specifically for isolators. The alpha port is mounted on the isolator wall and the beta port on the container. When the two are docked they form a sterile sealed interface, so the container can be opened into the isolator without being exposed to the outside environment.

3. Time-based separation: Where a unidirectional flow cannot be designed, procedures stipulate that material entry and material removal must not take place at the same time; separating them in time reduces the risk of cross-contamination.

Key Point: The Essence of the Unidirectional Flow Principle

Clause 4.11 stresses a "unidirectional process": materials may only move in one direction, and entry and removal must use different paths or different times. This concerns not only unidirectional airflow but also unidirectional material flow.

Think about this: why must waste (such as used swabs and environmental monitoring samples) also be removed "via a separate unidirectional process"? Because the surfaces of these items may already be contaminated, and if they share a route with incoming materials there is a risk of carrying contamination in.

Analogy: Airport Security

Imagine airport security:

  • Passengers (materials) go from the general area to the boarding gate (Grade A) and must pass a security check (disinfection/sterilisation)
  • Luggage (equipment) enters through the security channel of the X-ray machine (the double-ended steriliser)
  • Passengers who have already boarded (used materials/waste) leave through a different exit and cannot pass back through the security area
  • The rapid transfer port (RTP) is like the VIP channel: cleverly designed so that specific items can enter seamlessly without disturbing overall security

4.12–4.17 Airlock Design, Pressure Cascades and Monitoring: Airlock Design, Pressure Cascades & Monitoring

Original Text

4.12 Airlocks should be designed and used to provide physical separation and to minimize microbial and particle contamination of the different areas and should be present for material and personnel moving between different grades. Wherever possible, airlocks used for personnel movement should be separated from those used for material movement. Airlocks should be flushed effectively with filtered air to ensure that the grade of the cleanroom is maintained. The final stage of the airlock should, in the "at rest" state, be of the same cleanliness grade (viable and total particle) as the cleanroom into which it leads. Where the CCS indicates that the risk of contamination is high, separate change rooms for entering and leaving production areas should be used. Airlocks should be designed as follows:

  • i. Personnel airlocks: Areas of increasing cleanliness used for entry of personnel (e.g. from the grade D area to the grade C area to the grade B area). In general hand washing facilities should be provided only in the first stage of the changing room and not be present in changing rooms directly accessing the grade B area.
  • ii. Material airlocks: used for materials and equipment transfer. Only materials and equipment that have been included on an approved list and assessed during validation of the transfer process, should be transferred into the grade A or grade B areas via an airlock or pass-through hatches. Equipment and materials (intended for use in the grade A area) should be protected when transiting through the grade B area. Pass-through hatches should be designed to protect the higher-grade environment, for example by effective flushing with an active filtered air supply. The movement of material or equipment from lower grade or unclassified area to higher grade clean areas should be subject to cleaning and disinfection commensurate with the risk and in line with the CCS.

4.13 For pass-through hatches and airlocks, the entry and exit doors should not be opened simultaneously. For airlocks leading to the grade A and grade B areas, an interlocking system should be used. For airlocks leading to grade C and D areas, a visual and/or audible warning system should be operated as a minimum.

4.14 Cleanrooms should be supplied with a filtered air supply that maintains a positive pressure and/or an airflow relative to the background environment of a lower grade under all operational conditions. Adjacent rooms of different grades should have an air pressure difference of a minimum of 10 Pascals (guidance value). The recommendations regarding air supplies and pressures may need to be modified where it is necessary to contain certain materials (e.g. pathogenic, highly toxic or radioactive products). Decontamination of facilities and treatment of air leaving a clean area may be necessary for some operations.

4.15 Airflow patterns within cleanrooms and zones should be visualised to demonstrate that there is no ingress from lower grade to higher grade areas. Where unidirectional airflow is required, visualisation studies should be performed. Video recordings of the airflow patterns should be retained. The outcome of the air visualisation studies should be documented and considered when establishing the facility's environmental monitoring programme. Airflow pattern studies should be performed both at rest and in operation (e.g. simulating operator interventions).

4.16 Indicators of air pressure differences should be fitted between cleanrooms. Air pressure differences identified as critical should be continuously monitored and recorded. A warning system should be in place to instantly indicate and warn operators of any failure in the air supply or reduction of air pressure differences. The warning signal should not be overridden without assessment. Where alarm delays are set, these should be assessed and justified within the CCS.

4.17 Facilities should be designed to permit observation of production activities from outside the grade A and B areas (e.g. through the provision of windows or remote cameras with a full view of the area and processes to allow observation and supervision without entry).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Airlock Design Principles

An airlock is the transition space between cleanrooms of different grades. Its core design idea is "buffering": before entering a higher-grade area, personnel or materials change garments and are disinfected in an intermediate space, and the environment is allowed to recover on its own.

Key requirement: The final stage of an airlock (the side connecting to the higher-grade cleanroom) must meet, in the at rest state, the same cleanliness grade as the cleanroom it connects to. In other words, the last changing room before Grade B must meet the Grade B standard at rest.

Personnel airlocks vs material airlocks: The two should be separate wherever possible. Hand-washing facilities should be provided only at the first (lowest-grade) entrance of the changing rooms; there should be no sink in the Grade B changing room (again echoing clause 4.9).

≥ 10 Pa between adjacent rooms of different grades (guidance value)

Key Point: Strict Requirements for Alarm Systems

Clause 4.16 specifically stresses that pressure differential alarms must not be overridden without assessment. This matters greatly in practice: operators cannot cancel an alarm and carry on working because they are "in a hurry" or pressed a button by mistake.

Alarm delay settings must also be justified in the CCS. For example, a brief drop in pressure differential when a door is opened is normal, so a 30-second delay is reasonable, but without written justification it is not compliant.

Clause 4.17 (viewing windows/remote cameras) rests on this logic: supervisors and QA should be able to oversee production activities without entering Grade A/B, reducing the contamination risk that comes with personnel entry. This reflects replacing human intervention with engineering.

Analogy: A Pressure Cascade Is Like a Waterfall

Imagine a series of pools stepping down from high to low (a waterfall): water (air) always flows from higher to lower.

  • Grade A is the highest pool; water (clean air) is continuously fed in from above (the HEPA filters) and overflows outward into Grade B
  • The water level in Grade B is higher than in Grade C, and Grade C is higher than Grade D
  • Any external contaminant (dirty water) can only flow from a higher level to a lower one and can never climb upward
  • If the water level in one pool drops abnormally (a pressure differential alarm), it indicates a possible leak or HVAC problem that must be dealt with immediately

Practical Application: Pressure Design for Special Products

For facilities handling highly toxic or radioactive drugs (such as ADCs, i.e. antibody-drug conjugates, and radiopharmaceuticals), clause 4.14 allows a negative pressure design. The protection direction is reversed here: outside air flows inward, preventing hazardous substances from escaping.

In a CDMO, if the same facility produces both ordinary sterile products (requiring positive pressure) and highly toxic products (requiring negative pressure), independent HVAC systems and physical separation are usually needed. This is a major facility design challenge and one of the bases for CDMO differentiated pricing.

4.18–4.22 Barrier Technologies: Barrier Technologies: Isolators & RABS

Original Text

Barrier Technologies

4.18 Isolators or RABS, which are different technologies, and the associated processes, should be designed to provide protection through separation of the grade A environment from the environment of the surrounding room. The hazards introduced from entry or removal of items during processing should be minimized and supported by high capability transfer technologies or validated systems that robustly prevent contamination.

4.19 The design of the technology and processes used should ensure appropriate conditions are maintained in the critical zone to protect the exposed product during operations.

i. Isolators:

  • a. Open isolators: should ensure grade A conditions with first air protection in the critical zone and unidirectional airflow that sweeps over and away from exposed products during processing.
  • b. Closed isolators: should ensure grade A conditions with adequate protection for exposed products. Airflow may not be fully unidirectional in closed isolators where simple operations are conducted. However, any turbulent airflow should not increase risk of contamination. Where processing lines are included, grade A conditions should be ensured with first air protection and unidirectional airflow.
  • c. Negative pressure isolators: should only be used when containment of the product is considered essential (e.g. radiopharmaceutical products) and specialized risk control measures should be applied to ensure the critical zone is not compromised.

ii. RABS:

The design of RABS should ensure grade A conditions with unidirectional airflow and first air protection in the critical zone. A positive airflow from the critical zone to the supporting background environment should be maintained.

4.20 The background environment for isolators or RABS should ensure the risk of transfer of contamination is minimized.

i. Isolators:

  • a. The background environment for open isolators should generally correspond to a minimum of grade C. The background for closed isolators should correspond to a minimum of grade D. The decision on the background classification should be based on risk assessment and justified in the CCS.
  • b. Key considerations when performing the risk assessment for the CCS of an isolator should include: the bio-decontamination programme, the extent of automation, the impact of glove manipulations that may potentially compromise 'first air' protection, the impact of potential loss of barrier/glove integrity, transfer mechanisms used, and activities such as set-up or maintenance that may require the doors to be opened prior to the final bio-decontamination of the isolator.
  • c. Airflow pattern studies should be performed at the interfaces of open isolators to demonstrate the absence of air ingress.

ii. RABS:

The background environment for RABS used for aseptic processing should correspond to a minimum of grade B and airflow pattern studies should be performed to demonstrate the absence of air ingress during interventions, including door openings if applicable.

4.21 The materials used for glove systems (for both isolators and RABS) should be demonstrated to have appropriate mechanical and chemical resistance. The frequency of glove replacement should be defined within the CCS.

i. Isolators: Leak testing of the glove system should be performed using a methodology demonstrated to be suitable for the task and criticality. The testing should be performed at defined intervals — generally at a minimum frequency of the beginning and end of each batch or campaign. Glove integrity monitoring should include a visual inspection associated with each use and following any manipulation that may affect the integrity of the system.

ii. RABS: For RABS, gloves used in the grade A area should be sterilised before installation and sterilised or effectively bio-decontaminated by a validated method prior to each manufacturing campaign. If exposed to the background environment during operation, disinfection using an approved methodology following each exposure should be completed. Gloves should be visually examined with each use, and integrity testing should be performed at periodic intervals.

4.22 Decontamination methods (cleaning and bio-decontamination, and where applicable inactivation for biological materials) should be appropriately defined and controlled. The cleaning process prior to the bio-decontamination step is essential.

i. For isolators: The bio-decontamination process of the interior should be automated, validated and controlled within defined cycle parameters and should include a sporicidal agent in a suitable form (e.g. gaseous or vaporized form). Gloves should be appropriately extended with fingers separated to ensure contact with the agent.

ii. For RABS: The sporicidal disinfection should include the routine application of a sporicidal agent using a method that has been validated and demonstrated to robustly include all areas of the interior surfaces.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Open vs Closed Isolators

Open isolator: Has air inlets/outlets and a controlled exchange of air with the surroundings. The background must be at least Grade C. A filter is required at the outlet to prevent contaminants from entering.

Closed isolator: Fully sealed, with no continuous air exchange. The background need only be Grade D. Often used for small batches or discontinuous operations (such as radiopharmaceutical preparation).

Negative pressure isolator: Used only where "containment" is needed, such as radiopharmaceuticals or highly toxic substances. The inside is at negative pressure relative to the outside, preventing hazardous substances from escaping.

Analogy: Space Station vs Cleanroom

Isolator = space station: Completely cut off from the outside world, with a full life-support system (bio-decontamination). Astronauts (operators) interact with the interior through special interfaces (glove ports/RTPs), and a complete "airlock procedure" is needed before entry.

RABS = surgical zone inside a high-specification cleanroom: Operations are carried out in a strictly controlled background environment (Grade B) through rigid barriers and glove ports, but the barrier is not fully sealed; it relies on positive pressure airflow pushing outward from Grade A to maintain protection. When a door is opened, operations must stop and the system must be re-verified.

Key Point: The Importance of Glove Integrity Testing

Clause 4.21 sets strict requirements for glove systems because gloves are the weakest link in barrier technology. They must simultaneously have:

  • Mechanical strength (resistance to tearing)
  • Chemical compatibility (not attacked by VHP or disinfectants)
  • Sterility (isolator gloves must withstand bio-decontamination cycles)

Leak testing of isolator gloves must be performed at the start and end of each batch (the minimum frequency), with a visual inspection after each use. This is because even if the pre-batch test is passed, minor damage can still occur during operation through improper handling.

Key Concepts: Qualification vs Environmental Monitoring

Clause 4.24 stresses that two concepts must be clearly distinguished:

Environmental monitoring (EM): Routine monitoring during production to confirm that the environment continues to meet requirements. EM results are used for trend analysis and cannot replace qualification.

In short, qualification asks: "Can this room achieve Grade B?" EM asks: "Was Grade B maintained during today's production?"

The special nature of Grade A: The particle limits at rest and in operation are the same (0.5 µm = 3,520/m³), which means Grade A must maintain its at rest cleanliness even while personnel are working. This is precisely why UDAF and RABS/isolator barriers exist.

The marked difference in Grade B: At rest it is 3,520 (the same as Grade A), but in operation up to 352,000 is allowed, a 100-fold difference! This shows that once personnel enter Grade B, even in cleanroom garments they still bring in a large number of particles.

"Not predetermined" for Grade D: The in operation particle limit is not predetermined; the manufacturer sets it based on risk assessment. This gives the site flexibility but also requires sufficient supporting data.

  • Containment leak test (ix): Particularly applicable to isolators; tests the sealing integrity of the whole enclosure

  • Requalification after change = re-inspection after a major repair: if a HEPA filter is replaced, filter integrity must be retested

Original Text

4.33 The disinfection of cleanrooms is particularly important. They should be cleaned and disinfected thoroughly in accordance with a written programme. Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Three Levels of Disinfection

The disinfection requirements of Annex 1 fall into three progressive levels:

Level 1: Cleaning: First physically remove dirt, organic matter and disinfectant residues from surfaces. If this step is inadequate, the disinfectant cannot effectively reach the microorganisms on the surface (organic matter "protects" bacteria).

Level 2: Disinfection: Use chemical agents to kill or inactivate microorganisms. Disinfectants with different mechanisms of action must be rotated to prevent resistance from developing.

Level 3: Sporicide: Periodically use agents that can kill spore-forming organisms (such as sodium hypochlorite or hydrogen peroxide). Ordinary disinfectants are ineffective against bacterial spores, which is why Annex 1 explicitly requires the periodic use of sporicidal agents.

Key Point: The Need to Rotate Disinfectants

Clause 4.33 requires the use of "more than one type of disinfectant", for these reasons:

  • Different disinfectants have different mechanisms of action (oxidation, delipidation, penetration and so on), and rotating them prevents microorganisms from developing resistance
  • Long-term use of a single disinfectant creates selection pressure on the flora in the environment, eventually producing "resistant organisms" that withstand the existing disinfection programme
  • Microbial trend analysis of environmental monitoring (EM trending) is the key tool for detecting a decline in disinfection effectiveness: if the same species is detected persistently, the disinfection procedure may have gaps

Key Point: Disinfectants for Grade A/B Must Be Sterile

The core logic of clause 4.35: you cannot use a "dirty" disinfectant to disinfect the cleanest areas. If the disinfectant itself is microbially contaminated, applying it will instead carry contamination into the Grade A/B area.

This means:

  • Disinfectants used in Grade A/B must be filtered (0.22 µm) or otherwise sterilised
  • They must be dispensed under aseptic conditions and used within their expiry period
  • "Ready-made" disinfectants from vendors need a Certificate of Analysis and a completed supplier qualification

Analogy: Disinfection as Multiple Locks

Imagine protecting an important document:

  • Cleaning = first making sure no clutter on the desk gets in the way, so that the lock can be fitted
  • Disinfection = the first lock (kills common bacteria and fungi)
  • Sporicide = the second lock (kills the most stubborn spore-forming bacteria)
  • VHP fumigation (4.36) = setting a new security code on the whole safe (used for comprehensive bio-decontamination of an isolator or an entire cleanroom)

Each lock answers a different threat; only with all of them in place can "sterility assurance" be ensured.

Practical Application: Practical Challenges in Validating Disinfection Programmes

Clause 4.34 requires disinfection programmes to be validated. In a CDMO environment, this means:

  • Validation must be performed on "representative surface materials" (stainless steel, epoxy flooring, PVC soft walls and so on)
  • "In-use expiry" must be supported by data: how long does a diluted disinfectant remain effective?
  • If the CDMO adopts a new surface coating or building material (for example during a facility renovation), the disinfection programme may need to be revalidated
  • Monitoring for microbial resistance is a long-term part of managing a disinfection programme and requires a historical database

Section Review: Section 4 Summary

  • Grade classification: The four grades A/B/C/D form the environmental framework for sterile manufacturing. Grade A must maintain the same particle standard (3,520/m³ at 0.5 µm) both at rest and in operation, which is precisely why UDAF and barrier technology are necessary.
  • Barrier technologies: The use of isolators and RABS (restricted access barrier systems) must be considered in the CCS. An isolator provides higher sterility assurance but requires bio-decontamination validation; a RABS must operate in a Grade B background.
  • Airlocks and pressure differentials: Airlocks must be used unidirectionally, and Grade A/B airlocks need an interlocking system. The minimum pressure differential between adjacent rooms of different grades is 10 Pa; pressure differentials must be continuously monitored, and alarms must not be overridden without assessment.
  • Airflow visualisation: The air velocity for UDAF systems is 0.36–0.54 m/s (guidance value), and airflow visualisation studies must be performed both at rest and in operation, with video records retained.
  • Material transfer: Materials entering or leaving Grade A/B must follow a unidirectional process, with double-ended sterilisers or RTPs preferred; where sterilisation is not possible, a validated disinfection procedure must be used instead.
  • Requalification frequency: Grade A/B must be requalified at most every 6 months; Grade C/D at most every 12 months. This includes particle classification, filter integrity, airflow volume, pressure differential and air velocity (five minimum requirements in all).
  • Disinfection requirements: Cleaning comes before disinfection; disinfectants with different mechanisms must be rotated; sporicidal agents must be used periodically; disinfectants used in Grade A/B must be sterile; disinfection programmes must be validated.

Key Concepts

5.8–5.9 Conveyor Restrictions & Particle Counter Qualification

Original Text

5.8 A conveyor belt should not pass through a partition between a grade A or B area and a processing area of lower air cleanliness, unless the belt itself is continually sterilised (e.g. in a sterilising tunnel).

5.9 Particle counters, including sampling tubing, should be qualified. The manufacturer's recommended specifications should be considered for tube diameter and bend radii. Tube length should typically be no longer than 1m unless justified and the number of bends should be minimized. Portable particle counters with a short length of sample tubing should be used for classification purposes. Isokinetic sampling heads should be used in unidirectional airflow systems. They should be oriented appropriately and positioned as close as possible to the critical location to ensure that samples are representative.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Restriction on conveyors crossing grades: a conveyor is a "moving carrier of contamination": particles and microorganisms carried on the belt from a lower grade area would go directly into grade A/B. The only exception is where the belt itself is continuously sterilised (for example by passing through a hot-air sterilising tunnel), destroying the contamination before it crosses the dividing wall.

Isokinetic Sampling Head: in a unidirectional airflow (UDAF) system, the sampling head must face into the airflow, and the intake velocity must equal the ambient air velocity, to ensure the sample is representative. If the head is oriented wrongly or the velocity does not match, the count result can be seriously distorted.

Analogy

A particle counter sampling tube is like the "tubing of a stethoscope": if the tube is too long or has too many bends, the sound (the particle signal) is attenuated or distorted in transmission. PIC/S limits tube length to no more than 1 metre and requires bends to be minimised, precisely so that what you "hear" is the true signal and not a false negative caused by transmission loss.

Key Notes

Particle counter qualification is the foundation for the reliability of the environmental monitoring program. Common compliance issues:

  • Sampling tubing not included in the qualification scope (only the counter itself is qualified)
  • Isokinetic sampling heads not used in UDAF areas
  • Tubing length exceeding the specified limit when portable counters are used for classification
  • Insufficient calibration frequency or incomplete records for counters

Practical Application

At the CDMO facility design stage, if a client asks for a grade A filling area to be connected to a grade C conveyor discharge area, engineers must resolve the conveyor grade-crossing problem during design. Feasible options include: (1) installing a hot-air sterilising tunnel where the conveyor passes through the wall; (2) switching to an RTP (rapid transfer port) for batch transfer; (3) re-laying out the line so that filled product is initially handled inside a grade A isolator before being transferred out. Each option has different cost and validation requirements.

6.1–6.6 General Utilities Principles

Original Text

6.1 The nature and extent of controls applied to utility systems should be commensurate with the risk to product quality associated with the utility. The impact should be determined via a risk assessment and documented as part of the CCS.

6.2 In general, higher risk utilities are those that:

  • i. directly contact product e.g. water for washing and rinsing, gases and steam for sterilisation,
  • ii. contact materials that will ultimately become part of the product,
  • iii. contact surfaces that come into contact with the product,
  • iv. otherwise directly impact the product.

6.3 Utilities should be designed, installed, qualified, operated, maintained and monitored in a manner to ensure that the utility system functions as expected.

6.4 Results for critical parameters and critical quality attributes of high risk utilities should be subject to regular trend analysis to ensure that system capabilities remain appropriate.

6.5 Records of utility system installation should be maintained throughout the system's life-cycle. Such records should include current drawings and schematic diagrams, construction material lists and system specifications. Typically, important information includes attributes such as:

  • i. pipeline flow direction, slopes, diameter and length,
  • ii. tank and vessel details,
  • iii. valves, filters, drains, sampling and user points,

6.6 Pipes, ducts and other utilities should not be present in cleanrooms. If unavoidable, then they should be installed so that they do not create recesses, unsealed openings and surfaces which are difficult to clean. Installation should allow cleaning and disinfection of outer surface of the pipes.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Utilities risk classification: PIC/S applies a risk-commensurate principle: the intensity of control is proportional to the risk to product quality. High-risk utilities (those in direct contact with the product or with product-contact surfaces) require full qualification/validation, monitoring and trend analysis; low-risk utilities (such as electrical supply for building lighting) are controlled less stringently.

Part of the CCS (contamination control strategy): The risk assessment of utilities must be integrated into the CCS. This means utilities management does not stand alone: it is a component of the site-wide sterility assurance system and requires cross-functional collaboration (Engineering, QC, Production) to maintain.

Analogy

Utilities are like the "life-support systems" of a hospital: the oxygen supply line (going directly into the patient's lungs, high risk) versus the corridor lighting circuit (low risk). A hospital applies strict material certification, pressure monitoring and backup systems to the oxygen line, while the lighting circuit needs only routine maintenance. The PIC/S risk classification of utilities follows the same logic: the intensity of control is driven by risk.

Key Notes

Compliance points for piping installed in cleanrooms (6.6):

  • Ideally, piping should not enter the cleanroom at all, because pipe joints, flanges and insulation are all potential points where dust or liquid can accumulate
  • If unavoidable, the outer surface of the piping must be cleanable and disinfectable (no dead spots, crevices or recesses)
  • Common deficiency: poor sealing where piping passes through walls, creating an air short-circuit path that undermines the cleanroom pressure cascade

  2. If trend analysis of utilities shows that WFI conductivity has been rising slowly over the past three months (but is still within specification), what action should be taken?
6.7–6.15 Water Systems: WFI & Monitoring

Original Text

6.7 Water treatment plant and distribution systems should be designed, constructed, installed, commissioned, qualified, monitored and maintained to prevent microbiological contamination and to ensure a reliable source of water of an appropriate quality. Measures should be taken to minimize the risk of presence of particulates, microbial contamination/proliferation and endotoxin/pyrogen (e.g. sloping of piping to provide complete drainage and the avoidance of dead legs). Where filters are included in the system, special attention should be given to their monitoring and maintenance. Water produced should comply with the current monograph of the relevant Pharmacopeia.

6.8 Water systems should be qualified and validated to maintain the appropriate levels of physical, chemical and microbial control, taking the effect of seasonal variation into account.

6.9 Water flow should remain turbulent through the pipes in water distribution systems to minimize the risk of microbial adhesion, and subsequent biofilm formation. The flow rate should be established during qualification and be routinely monitored.

6.10 Water for injections (WFI) should be produced from water meeting specifications that have been defined during the qualification process, stored and distributed in a manner which minimizes the risk of microbial growth (e.g. by constant circulation at a temperature above 70°C). WFI should be produced by distillation or by a purification process that is equivalent to distillation. This may include reverse osmosis coupled with other appropriate techniques such as electrodeionization (EDI), ultrafiltration or nanofiltration.

6.11 Where WFI storage tanks are equipped with hydrophobic bacteria retentive vent filters, the filters should not be a source of contamination and the integrity of the filter tested before installation and after use. Controls should be in place to prevent condensation formation on the filter (e.g. by heating).

6.12 To minimize the risk of biofilm formation, sterilisation, disinfection or regeneration of water systems should be carried out according to a predetermined schedule and as a remedial action following out-of-limit or specification results. Disinfection of a water system with chemicals should be followed by a validated rinsing/flushing procedure. Water should be tested after disinfection/regeneration. Chemical testing results should be approved before the water system is returned to use and microbiological/endotoxin results verified to be within specification and approved before batches manufactured using water from the system are considered for certification/release.

6.13 Regular ongoing chemical and microbial monitoring of water systems should be performed to ensure that the water continues to meet compendial expectations. Alert levels should be based on the initial qualification data and thereafter periodically reassessed on data obtained during subsequent re-qualifications, routine monitoring, and investigations. Sampling programmes should reflect the requirements of the CCS and should include all outlets and points of use, at a specified interval, to ensure that representative water samples are obtained for analysis on a regular basis. Sample plans should be based on the qualification data, should consider the potential worst case sampling locations and should ensure that at least one representative sample is included every day of the water that is used for manufacturing processes.

6.14 Alert level excursions should be documented and reviewed, and include an investigation to determine whether the excursion is a single (isolated) event or if results are indicative of an adverse trend or system deterioration. Each action limit excursion should be investigated to determine the probable root causes and any potential impact on the quality of products and manufacturing processes as a result of the use of the water.

6.15 WFI systems should include continuous monitoring systems such as Total Organic Carbon (TOC) and conductivity, as these may give a better indication of overall system performance than discrete sampling. Sensor locations should be based on risk.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Water for Injection (WFI): The highest grade of pharmaceutical water, used in the manufacture of injectable products, which must meet pharmacopoeial standards (endotoxin, microbiological and chemical purity). WFI is the "lifeline" of sterile injectable manufacture: loss of quality control directly affects the safety of every batch.

Purified Water (PW): Pharmaceutical water used for non-injectable products, with a lower standard than WFI. In sterile manufacturing lines WFI is the preferred choice, and PW may be used only for low-risk steps (such as equipment pre-cleaning).

Dead legs: Dead-end sections of piping where the flow velocity is close to zero, which are a breeding ground for biofilm. WHO and the pharmacopoeias recommend that the dead leg length should not exceed 6 times the pipe diameter (the 6D rule).

Total Organic Carbon (TOC): An indicator of the organic content of the water, which indirectly reflects the risk of microbial contamination and the cleanliness of the system. Conductivity reflects the inorganic ion content. Continuous monitoring of both gives earlier warning of system deterioration than intermittent sampling.

Analogy

A WFI system is like a "hospital IV fluid production system": the saline given intravenously to a patient must be made from the purest water source, circulated in a fully closed loop, and kept at high temperature (above 70°C) to suppress bacterial growth. Once water quality deteriorates (biofilm, endotoxin), it is as if the IV fluid has been contaminated, and every patient receiving it is at risk. This is why WFI system monitoring requirements are so strict: they protect the life and safety of the end patient.

Key Temperature and Flow Requirements for WFI Systems

WFI storage and circulation temperature:
  Hot loop: > 70°C  ← suppresses microbial growth
  Cold loop: < 4°C  ← alternative (requires validation)

Turbulent flow:
  Reynolds number Re > 4000 (turbulent)
  Re = ρvD/μ
  where ρ = density, v = flow velocity, D = pipe diameter, μ = viscosity

Dead leg limit:
  Dead leg length ≤ 6 × pipe diameter (6D rule)
  Dead legs longer than 6D require special cleaning/disinfection validation

Monitoring frequency (minimum requirement):
  At least one representative sampling point per manufacturing day
  Critical points of use: higher frequency

Key Notes

Management of hydrophobic vent filters (6.11):

  • The vent filter on a WFI storage tank is a potential contamination entry point: microorganisms in the ambient air may pass through a failed filter into the tank
  • The filter must be integrity tested before installation and after use (bubble point or diffusive flow test)
  • There must be a mechanism to prevent condensation (such as an electrically heated jacket): condensate can block the hydrophobic membrane, causing the filter to fail, and also creates conditions for microbial growth
  • This is a frequently overlooked compliance risk point in WFI systems

Difference between alert levels and action limits (6.14): An alert level excursion only requires an investigation into whether it is the start of a trend; an action limit excursion requires investigation of the root cause, assessment of the impact on batches already manufactured, and corrective action, and the system may not continue to be used until it has returned to normal.

Practical Application

In CDMO facility design, WFI system design decisions directly affect long-term compliance cost: a hot loop system (>70°C) and a cold loop system (<4°C) each have advantages and disadvantages. A hot loop effectively suppresses biofilm but consumes more energy; a cold loop consumes less energy but requires more rigorous microbial monitoring. Most sterile injectable facilities choose the hot loop as the first choice to reduce the validation burden of microbial control. In addition, in CDMO operations the WFI system capacity must take account of the demands of simultaneous multi-product manufacture, to avoid manufacturing delays caused by insufficient capacity.

  1. Once biofilm has formed in a WFI system, why is it difficult to remove with ordinary disinfection? What fundamental system design measures should be taken to prevent it?
  2. If TOC remains high (above the action limit) after chemical sanitisation of a WFI system, describe all the steps that must be completed before production is resumed.
  3. How do seasonal changes affect WFI system quality? Give one specific example.
6.16–6.17 Pure Steam as Direct Sterilising Agent

Original Text

6.16 Feed water to a pure steam (clean steam) generator should be appropriately purified. Pure steam generators should be designed, qualified and operated in a manner to ensure that the quality of steam produced meets defined chemical and endotoxin levels.

6.17 Steam used as a direct sterilising agent should be of suitable quality and should not contain additives at a level which could cause contamination of product or equipment. For a generator supplying pure steam used for the direct sterilisation of materials or product-contact surfaces (e.g. porous / hard-good autoclave loads), steam condensate should meet the current monograph for WFI of the relevant Pharmacopeia (microbial testing is not mandatory for steam condensate). A suitable sampling schedule should be in place to ensure that representative pure steam is obtained for analysis on a regular basis. Other aspects of the quality of pure steam used for sterilisation should be assessed periodically against validated parameters. These parameters should include the following (unless otherwise justified): non-condensable gases, dryness value (dryness fraction) and superheat.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Pure Steam (Clean Steam): Steam generated from high-purity feed water (usually PW or WFI grade) and free of additives, used for sterilisation of product or of equipment inner surfaces in direct contact. Its condensate must meet the WFI standard (although microbiological testing is not mandatory).

Three key quality parameters:

  • Non-condensable gases: Such as air and nitrogen. They form "air pockets" in the corners of the sterilisation chamber, hindering steam penetration and leading to local cold spots and sterilisation failure.
  • Dryness (dryness value/fraction): The ratio of pure steam to liquid water in the steam. Too much liquid water (wet steam) dilutes the sterilising effect and may carry in particles or chemical contaminants.
  • Superheat: The degree to which the steam temperature exceeds the saturation temperature. If it is too high, the steam becomes dry and the sterilising effect decreases; only suitable saturated steam can efficiently transfer latent heat for sterilisation.

Analogy

Pure steam sterilisation is like "pressure cooker cooking": the pot must be filled with saturated steam to reach a uniformly high temperature. If there is air (non-condensable gas) in the pot, the temperature in some corners will not reach the level required for sterilisation; if the steam is too "wet" (too much liquid water), heat transfer efficiency drops; if the steam is too "dry" (superheated), it behaves like hot air rather than steam and the sterilising effect is greatly reduced. Pure steam quality control is about ensuring ideal conditions inside the pot.

Key Notes

Pure steam condensate must meet the WFI standard, but microbiological testing is not mandatory. This is a common exam and audit question. The reason is that the high temperature of the steam itself has a sterilising effect, and the microbial risk of the condensate comes mainly from environmental contamination after condensation, not from the steam itself. However, the chemical attributes (TOC, conductivity, endotoxin) must still meet the WFI pharmacopoeial standard.

Practice Questions

  1. A biological indicator (BI) test in an autoclave has failed, and you suspect cold spots caused by excessive non-condensable gases. How would you design an investigation plan to confirm this hypothesis?
  2. The feed water for pure steam must be "suitably purified". In your facility, should this feed water be WFI, PW or municipal water? What is the basis for your choice?
6.18–6.22 Compressed Gases, Vacuum & HVAC/Cooling Systems

Original Text

6.18 Gases that come in direct contact with the product/primary container surfaces should be of appropriate chemical, particulate and microbial quality. All relevant parameters, including oil and water content, should be specified, taking into account the use and type of the gas, the design of the gas generation system and, where applicable, comply with the current monograph of the relevant Pharmacopeia or the product quality requirement.

6.19 Gases used in aseptic processes should be filtered through a sterilising grade filter (with a nominal pore size of a maximum of 0.22 µm) at the point of use. Where the filter is used on a batch basis (e.g. for filtration of gas used for overlay of aseptically filled products) or as product vessel vent filter, then the filter should be integrity tested and the results reviewed as part of the batch certification/release process. Any transfer pipework or tubing that is located after the final sterilising grade filter should be sterilised. When gases are used in the process, microbial monitoring of the gas should be performed periodically at the point of use.

6.20 Where backflow from vacuum or pressure systems poses a potential risk to the product, there should be mechanism(s) to prevent backflow when the vacuum or pressure system is shut off.

6.21 Major items of equipment associated with hydraulic, heating and cooling systems should, where possible, be located outside the filling room. There should be appropriate controls to contain any spillage and/or cross contamination associated with the system fluids.

6.22 Any leaks from these systems that would present a risk to the product should be detectable (e.g. an indication system for leakage).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Sterilising grade filter (0.22 µm): Used to filter gases to achieve sterile quality. In aseptic filling processes, gases used for steps such as nitrogen overlay and vessel venting must pass through a 0.22 µm filter, and the filter must be integrity tested at the end of the batch. As with liquid filters, the record of a passed integrity test is a required document for batch release.

Backflow prevention: When a vacuum line or pressure system is shut down, without a check valve or other backflow prevention mechanism, material in the line may flow back into the product container and cause contamination. This is especially dangerous in liquid filling steps.

Analogy

Compressed gases in sterile processes are like the "anaesthetic gas lines in an operating theatre": they must be the purest medical-grade gas, with filtration assurance at each point of use, and the piping materials and connections must be specially certified. If the gas contains oil (compressor oil), water (condensate) or particles, direct entry into an open container will contaminate the product. This is why gas quality specifications must include limits for oil and water content.

Key Notes

Gas filter integrity testing is part of the batch release documentation (6.19): This point is often overlooked. In both of the following cases, the filter must be integrity tested and the result must be part of the batch record:

  • Gas filters used on a batch basis (such as the nitrogen overlay filter during filling)
  • Vent filters on product containers

Sterilisation requirement for piping downstream of the final sterilising filter (6.19): Any transfer line downstream of the final sterilising filter must also be sterilised. Even though the gas has been filtered to sterility, if it flows through unsterilised piping it can still carry away microbial contaminants from the piping. This concept that the line after the outlet must also be sterilised applies equally to liquid filtration systems.

Practical Application

At CDMO facility acceptance, utilities qualification/validation documents are often among the most easily overlooked. It is recommended to establish the following checklist before the facility goes into operation: WFI system validation report (including seasonal validation), pure steam quality test report (non-condensable gases, dryness, superheat), compressed air/nitrogen quality specifications and monitoring records, and initial integrity test records for each critical filter. These documents are often the first focus of a GMP inspection.

Practice Questions

  1. The integrity test of the 0.22 µm nitrogen overlay filter failed after filling was completed. What steps must you take? How should this batch be dispositioned?
  2. Coolant has leaked onto the floor of the filling area from a cooling system. What level of deviation is this? What is your immediate action procedure?

Section Summary

  • The intensity of utilities control must be commensurate with the risk to product quality. High-risk utilities (in direct contact with the product or with product-contact surfaces) require full qualification/validation and trend analysis, and must be included in the CCS.
  • WFI (Water for Injection) systems must be stored with a hot loop (>70°C) or an equivalent low-temperature measure, avoiding dead legs, maintaining turbulent flow and preventing biofilm; the hydrophobic vent filter on the WFI tank must be integrity tested and protected against condensation. WFI systems must be sampled at least once per manufacturing day, with continuous monitoring of TOC and conductivity.
  • Pure steam condensate must meet the WFI chemical standard; the three key quality parameters (non-condensable gases, dryness, superheat) must be confirmed periodically.
  • Gases used in sterile processes must pass through a 0.22 µm sterilising filter, and filters used on a batch basis must be integrity tested and included in the batch release documentation; vacuum/pressure systems need backflow prevention; cooling/hydraulic systems should be located outside the filling room, and leaks must be detectable.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand that personnel are the primary source of contamination in aseptic processing, and grasp the minimum-personnel principle and its importance for sterility assurance
  • Know how gowning requirements differ by cleanroom grade (grade A/B vs C vs D), and the procedure and frequency of aseptic gowning qualification
  • Understand the rules on personnel health, exclusion criteria (shedding organisms) and access to restricted areas
  • Grasp the rules on behaviour in the cleanroom, garment management (laundry validation, maximum number of uses) and viable microbial monitoring of personnel

Core Principle of This Section

Personnel are the greatest contamination threat in aseptic processing. Even with the most advanced isolator technology, people still affect sterility assurance indirectly through their operations, interventions and design decisions. In RABS (restricted access barrier systems) and open filling operations, personnel are a direct source of contamination risk. Every requirement in Section 7 follows from this core principle: how to minimise the risk of contamination from personnel through training, gowning, health control and behavioural rules.

7.1–7.3 Personnel Qualification & Training

Original Text

7.1 The manufacturer should ensure that there are sufficient appropriate personnel, suitably qualified, trained and experienced in the manufacture and testing of sterile products, and any of the specific manufacturing technologies used in the site's manufacturing operations, to ensure compliance with GMP applicable to the manufacture and handling of sterile products.

7.2 Only the minimum number of personnel required should be present in cleanrooms. The maximum number of operators in cleanrooms should be determined, documented and considered during activities such as initial qualification and APS, so as not to compromise sterility assurance.

7.3 All personnel including those performing cleaning, maintenance, monitoring and those that access cleanrooms should receive regular training, gowning qualification and assessment in disciplines relevant to the correct manufacture of sterile products. This training should include the basic elements of microbiology and hygiene, with a specific focus on cleanroom practices, contamination control, aseptic techniques and the protection of sterile products (for those operators entering the grade B cleanrooms and/or intervening into grade A) and the potential safety implications to the patient if the product is not sterile. The level of training should be based on the criticality of the function and area in which the personnel are working.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Minimum personnel principle: every additional person is an additional source of contamination. A human body sheds roughly 100,000 particles per minute, and even sterile garments cannot block this completely. The number of personnel in grade A/B areas must therefore be justified by a formal risk assessment, confirmed during the APS (aseptic process simulation), and a maximum number set in a document.

Three levels of training:

  • Basic level: basic microbiology and personal hygiene (everyone who accesses the cleanroom)
  • Intermediate level: cleanroom behaviour, contamination control and gowning qualification (grade C/D personnel)
  • Advanced level: aseptic technique, APS participation and patient safety awareness (grade A/B personnel)

Analogy

Cleanroom personnel management is like managing a surgical team in an operating theatre. Only the necessary surgeons, anaesthetists and nurses may enter, each has strict aseptic training and certification, and every entry is a risk decision. No extra people are allowed to "watch", because every person is a potential source of contamination. PIC/S 7.2 follows exactly the same logic: a cleanroom is not open for visits, and the qualification and necessity of everyone who enters must be reviewed.

Key Notes

Training must tell operators about the potential impact on patient safety if a product is not sterile; this is a point newly emphasised in the 2022 revision of PIC/S Annex 1. The reasoning is that only when operators understand that each of their actions could affect a patient's life can a true "aseptic mindset" be built, rather than mechanical compliance with procedures.

Cleaning staff, maintenance staff and environmental monitoring staff must also receive the same training and gowning qualification. They do not handle product directly, but entering the cleanroom is itself a contamination risk. This point is often overlooked in audits.

  1. What is the maximum number of people allowed in your site's grade B filling room during an APS? How was this number determined? If routine production needs one more person, what steps are needed to remain compliant?
  2. A newly hired cleaner needs to work in a grade C area. What training and qualification must he complete before being allowed to enter?
7.4–7.6 Aseptic Gowning Qualification & Disqualification

Original Text

7.4 The personnel accessing grade A and B areas should be trained for aseptic gowning and aseptic behaviours. Compliance with aseptic gowning procedures should be confirmed by assessment and periodic reassessment at least annually, and should involve both visual and microbial assessment (using monitoring locations such as gloved fingers, forearms, chest and hood (facemask / forehead). See paragraph 9.30 for the expected limits). The unsupervised access to the grade A and grade B areas where aseptic operations are or will be conducted should be restricted to appropriately qualified personnel, who have passed the gowning assessment and have participated in a successful APS.

7.5 Unqualified personnel should not enter grade B cleanrooms or grade A in operation. If needed in exceptional cases, manufacturers should establish written procedures outlining the process by which unqualified personnel are brought into the grade B and A areas. An authorized person from the manufacturer should supervise the unqualified personnel during their activities and should assess the impact of these activities on the cleanliness of the area. Access by these persons should be assessed and recorded in accordance with the PQS.

7.6 There should be systems in place for the disqualification of personnel from working in or given unsupervised entry into cleanrooms that is based on aspects including ongoing assessment and/or identification of an adverse trend from the personnel monitoring programme and/or after being implicated in a failed APS. Once disqualified, retraining and requalification should be completed before permitting the operator to have any further involvement in aseptic practices. For operators entering grade B cleanrooms or performing intervention into grade A, this requalification should include consideration of participation in a successful APS.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Aseptic gowning qualification: evaluates whether an operator can put on sterile garments correctly without contaminating themselves. The evaluation has two aspects:

  • Visual assessment: observe whether the gowning procedure follows the SOP (sequence, timing of glove disinfection, whether outer surfaces are touched)
  • Microbial assessment: after gowning, sample the gloved fingers, forearms, chest, hood and similar locations, and confirm that microbial counts meet the limits in clause 9.30

Dual qualification requirement for entry to grade A/B: both conditions, "passing the gowning assessment" AND "participating in and passing an APS", must be met before unsupervised access is permitted. Neither alone is sufficient.

Analogy

Gowning qualification is like certifying a surgeon's scrub technique. Not every medical student can automatically enter the operating theatre; they must pass an assessment of correct scrubbing (visual) and have culture results confirmed (microbial). If the scrub is found to be unacceptable (or post-operative cultures are abnormal), retraining is required before re-entering the theatre. The APS (aseptic process simulation) requirement is similar to a surgical resident's "first independent operation": it must be completed successfully under supervision before independent operating rights are granted.

Key Notes

Three triggers for the disqualification system:

  • Problems found during ongoing assessment (for example, repeated failures of the visual assessment)
  • Adverse trends found in the personnel monitoring programme (for example, persistently high microbial counts after an individual's gowning)
  • Involvement in a failed APS (even if not the main operator, participation itself may trigger reassessment)

After disqualification, both retraining AND requalification must be completed. For grade B/A personnel, requalification should include participation in one successful APS, which means regaining qualification may take several weeks. A CDMO must ensure there are enough "backup qualified personnel" to avoid production bottlenecks.

7.7–7.9 Health Monitoring & Personal Hygiene

Original Text

7.7 High standards of personal hygiene and cleanliness are essential to prevent excessive shedding or increased risk of introduction of microbial contamination. Personnel involved in the manufacture of sterile products should be instructed to report any specific health conditions or ailments which may cause the shedding of abnormal numbers or types of contaminants and therefore preclude cleanroom access. Health conditions and actions to be taken with regard to personnel who could be introducing an undue microbial hazard should be provided by the designated competent person and described in procedures.

7.8 Personnel who have been engaged in the processing of human or animal tissue materials or of cultures of micro-organisms, other than those used in the current manufacturing process, or any activities that may have a negative impact to quality (e.g. microbial contamination), should not enter clean areas unless clearly defined and effective decontamination and entry procedures have been followed and documented.

7.9 Wristwatches, make-up, jewellery, other personal items such as mobile phones and any other non-essential items should not be allowed in clean areas. Electronic devices used in cleanrooms, e.g. mobile phones and tablets, that are supplied by the manufacturer solely for use in the cleanrooms, may be acceptable if suitably designed to permit cleaning and disinfection commensurate with the grade in which they are used. The use and disinfection of such equipment should be included in the CCS.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Shedding organisms: the human body continuously sheds skin cells, hair, respiratory secretions and similar material, and these microbe-carrying particles are the main source of microbial contamination in cleanrooms. The following health conditions significantly increase shedding:

  • Skin conditions (eczema, psoriasis, wounds)
  • Upper respiratory tract infections (colds, sinusitis)
  • Gastrointestinal infections (Salmonella, norovirus)
  • Recent contact with animal tissue or microbial cultures

Employees have an obligation to "proactively report" the above conditions. This must become part of the company culture, rather than employees concealing illness for fear of losing pay for sick leave.

Analogy

Personal health control is like the health rules for food safety among chefs. A chef with a gastrointestinal infection cannot work, because his hands, breath and even sweat could contaminate the food. The same applies to aseptic operators in a pharmaceutical plant: if an operator with a cold sneezes, even while wearing a mask, the aerosol particles expelled may still get through the protection and contaminate nearby open containers. This is not excessive; it is a basic safeguard for user safety.

Key Notes

Cross-contamination risk in 7.8: in an R&D centre or multi-purpose facility, the same operator might culture strains in the microbiology laboratory in the morning and enter the aseptic filling area in the afternoon. This is strictly prohibited. Clear "decontamination and entry procedures" must exist and be documented.

7.9 Electronic devices supplied by the company: smartphones and tablets are increasingly common in modern manufacturing, but they must meet these conditions: (1) supplied by the company (not personal devices); (2) designed so that they can be cleaned and disinfected; (3) included in the CCS. Personal mobile phones are strictly prohibited because the effectiveness of disinfection cannot be assured.

Practical Application

In CDMO personnel management, establishing a "health reporting system" is essential, together with supporting measures: for example, paid sick leave for ill employees (so that they do not work while sick for fear of losing income) and a clear reporting process (to whom to report, and how work is arranged after reporting). In addition, for R&D staff who handle animal tissue or bacterial cultures, a "work segregation schedule" should be set up, specifying how long they must wait (or which decontamination procedures they must complete) after such contact before entering the cleanroom.

Practice Questions

  1. One morning an operator handled Salmonella reference standards in the QC laboratory, and in the afternoon he is assigned to assist with cleaning in a grade C area. As shift leader, how would you handle this?
  2. Management is considering installing company-supplied tablets in the grade B gowning room for real-time access to SOPs. What needs to be added to the CCS?
7.10–7.13 Gowning Requirements by Cleanroom Grade

Original Text

7.10 Cleanroom gowning and hand washing should follow a written procedure designed to minimize contamination of cleanroom clothing and/or the transfer of contaminants to the clean areas.

7.11 The clothing and its quality should be appropriate for the process and the grade of the working area. It should be worn in such a way as to protect the product from contamination. When the type of clothing chosen needs to provide the operator protection from the product, it should not compromise the protection of the product from contamination.   • i. Grade B (including access / interventions into grade A): appropriate garments that are dedicated for use under a sterilised suit should be worn before gowning. Appropriately sterilised, non-powdered, rubber or plastic gloves should be worn while donning the sterilised garments. A sterile facemask and sterile eye coverings (e.g. goggles) should be worn to cover and enclose all facial skin and prevent the shedding of droplets and particles. Appropriate sterilised footwear (e.g. over-boots) should be worn. Trouser legs should be tucked inside the footwear. Garment sleeves should be tucked into a second pair of sterile gloves worn over the pair worn while donning the gown. The protective clothing should minimize shedding of fibres or particles and retain particles shed by the body. The particle shedding and the particle retention efficiencies of the garments should be assessed during the garment qualification. Garments should be packed and folded in such a way as to allow operators to don the gown without contacting the outer surface of the garment and to prevent the garment from touching the floor.
  • ii. Grade C: Hair, beards and moustaches should be covered. A single or two-piece trouser suit gathered at the wrists and with high neck and appropriately disinfected shoes or overshoes should be worn. They should minimize the shedding of fibres and particles.
  • iii. Grade D: Hair, beards and moustaches should be covered. A general protective suit and appropriately disinfected shoes or overshoes should be worn. Appropriate measures should be taken to avoid any ingress of contaminants from outside the clean area.
  • iv. Additional gowning including gloves and facemask may be required in grade C and D areas when performing activities considered to be a contamination risk as defined by the CCS.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The full aseptic gowning for grade B is like a "space suit": every gap must be sealed, and any exposed skin is a potential entry point for contamination. Goggles are not just eye protection; they close off the last area of exposed facial skin (around the eyes). The double-glove design is also clever: the first pair is put on when gowning (used to handle the sterilised garment packaging), and the second pair is put on after gowning is complete (so that the gloves in contact with the product environment are donned under fully aseptic conditions). Every design detail serves to block a contamination path.

Key Notes

Three points in time for visual garment inspection (7.11):

  • Before donning: check cleanliness and integrity (make sure the packaging is intact and the garment has no holes)
  • After donning: confirm correct wearing (all gaps closed, cuffs tucked into gloves)
  • At removal: check integrity again (damaged garments may indicate an integrity failure during wear, and an investigation is needed into whether aseptic operations during that shift were affected)

Garment hold time management: sterilised garments have an expiry period (determined by validation studies). Before use, confirm the garment is still within its hold time; expired unused garments must be returned for re-sterilisation. This is easily overlooked in daily management and is a common audit finding.

Practical Application

Garment qualification is an important item before a CDMO facility is commissioned, and usually includes particle shedding tests (ISO 9073 or equivalent), particle retention tests, and laundering durability tests (evaluating performance after the maximum number of washes). When selecting a garment supplier, a CDMO must consider not only price but also whether the supplier can provide complete qualification support data (Certificate of Compliance, particle test reports). If garment quality is not up to standard, every gowning event is a potential contamination event.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Cascade of gowning rooms: gowning must take place in a gowning room of sufficiently clean grade. If sterile garments are put on in a contaminated environment, their outer surfaces are immediately contaminated by environmental particles. A typical gowning cascade:

  • Outdoor clothing → grade D gowning room (change into factory clothing)
  • Factory clothing → grade C gowning room (change into grade C work garments)
  • Grade C work garments → grade B gowning room (change into full sterile garments)

Maximum garment wearing time: determined by validation studies, usually one shift (8-12 hours) or less. Garments must be changed after the maximum wearing time is exceeded, which is an important control point to prevent microbial build-up inside the garment.

Analogy

Aseptic behaviour is like the movement aesthetics of the tea ceremony: every movement must be slow, deliberate and controlled. Rapid movement creates turbulence that disrupts the carefully designed unidirectional airflow in the grade A area and draws air from lower-grade areas into the critical zone. This is not just a rule; it has a clear basis in airflow physics. An airflow visualisation study uses a smoke study to show how personnel movements affect the airflow path, which is exactly why 7.18 requires airflow visualisation studies to be included in the training programme.

Key Notes

Periodic glove disinfection (7.16): periodic disinfection of gloves (usually every 30 minutes or after each touch of a non-sterile surface) is a basic requirement of aseptic operations. The frequency and method (IPA spray or immersion) must be clearly specified in the SOP.

Four compliance points for garment laundry management (7.17):

  • The laundry facility must be adequately segregated from production operations (to prevent cross-contamination)
  • Laundry procedures must be validated (to ensure that garment fibres are not damaged and no contamination is introduced)
  • Visual inspection after laundering (damaged garments are discarded)
  • Maximum numbers of wash and sterilisation cycles are specified (garments exceeding the limits must be scrapped)

The concept of first air: the cleanest air leaving the HEPA filter is called "first air". Any object (personnel, equipment, an arm) crossing the path of first air may create micro-eddies in the critical zone and let microbe-carrying aerosols in. Operators must learn to "let first air reach critical surfaces unobstructed".

Section Summary

  • Personnel are the primary source of contamination in aseptic processing. Grade A/B areas must keep personnel numbers to a minimum, with the maximum number confirmed and documented during the APS. All personnel who access the cleanroom (including cleaning, maintenance and monitoring staff) must be trained regularly and understand the impact of sterility failure on patient safety.
  • Grade A/B personnel must pass aseptic gowning qualification (visual + microbial assessment) and be reassessed at least annually, and must also participate in a successful APS before being allowed unsupervised access. Those with adverse trends or involvement in a failed APS should be disqualified immediately and may be reinstated only after retraining and requalification.
  • Health control: operators are obliged to report health conditions that may increase shedding risk; those who have handled microbial cultures or animal tissue must complete a documented decontamination procedure; personal watches, jewellery and personal mobile phones are prohibited in cleanroom areas.
  • Gowning requirements increase with grade: grade B requires full sterile garments (goggles, mask, double gloves, sterile boot covers); grade C requires a one-piece or two-piece suit with high neck and tightened cuffs; grade D requires general protective clothing. Garments must be visually inspected, used within hold times, and laundered under a validated procedure, with maximum wash and sterilisation cycles set. Gloves must be disinfected periodically, and movements should be slow and orderly so as not to obstruct the unidirectional airflow (first air) in grade A.

8.1–8.6 Terminally Sterilised Products

Original Text

8.1 Preparation of components and materials should be performed in at least a grade D cleanroom in order to limit the risk of microbial, endotoxin/pyrogen and particle contamination, so that the product is suitable for sterilisation. 8.3 Filling of products for terminal sterilisation should be carried out in at least a grade C environment.

8.4 Where the CCS identifies that the product is at an unusual risk of contamination from the environment because, for example, the filling operation is slow, the containers are wide necked or are necessarily exposed for more than a few seconds before closing, then the product should be filled in grade A with at least a grade C background.

8.5 Processing of the bulk solution should include a filtration step with a microorganism retaining filter, where possible, to reduce bioburden levels and particles prior to filling into the final product containers and there should be a maximum permissible time between preparation and filling.

8.6 Examples of operations to be carried out in the various grades are given in Table 3.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Terminal Sterilisation

Terminally sterilised products are sterilised as a whole batch (usually by steam under pressure or dry heat) after filling and sealing. Because a final sterilisation step provides assurance at the end, the grade requirements before filling are less stringent than for aseptic processing.

Grade decision tree:

  • General preparation (compounding, component washing) → at least Grade D
  • Products at high microbial risk (prolonged exposure, wide-necked containers, supporting microbial growth) → at least Grade C
  • Ointments, creams, suspensions, emulsions → at least Grade C
  • General filling (before terminal sterilisation) → at least Grade C
  • High-risk situations (slow filling, wide-necked containers, prolonged exposure) → Grade A (with a Grade C background)

Analogy

Terminal sterilisation is like "reheating in a microwave" (Terminal Sterilisation = microwave reheating): the food is prepared in an ordinary kitchen and then heated at high temperature in the microwave at the end. Because of this final barrier, preparation does not need operating-theatre-level sterile conditions. Aseptic processing (from 8.7 onwards), by contrast, is like preparing the food directly in an operating theatre: there is no later heating step, so every operation must be carried out in a strictly aseptic environment.

Key Notes

CCS drives the decision: 8.4 stresses identification through the CCS (contamination control strategy). The grade is therefore not chosen by looking up a rule and applying it, but from the conclusions of the company's own CCS risk assessment. Slow filling speeds, wide-necked containers, or containers exposed for more than a few seconds all call for an upgrade to Grade A.

Time limit requirement (8.5): A "maximum permissible time" must be set between preparation and filling. This is key to preventing microorganisms from multiplying beyond the bioburden control range; it must be set during process validation and tracked in the batch record.

8.7–8.19 Aseptic Preparation and Processing

Original Text

8.7 The aseptic process should be clearly defined. The risks associated with the aseptic process, and any associated requirements, should be identified, assessed and appropriately controlled. The site's CCS should clearly define the acceptance criteria for these controls, requirements for monitoring and the review of their effectiveness. Methods and procedures to control these risks should be described and implemented. Accepted residual risks should be formally documented.

8.10 Examples of operations to be carried out in the various environmental grades are given in Table 4.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Aseptic Processing

Aseptic processing means maintaining sterility throughout the filling process through environmental controls and operating practices, rather than relying on a sterilisation step afterwards. It is the necessary approach when terminal sterilisation cannot be used (for example, heat-sensitive biological products).

The three main requirements of 8.7:

  • The aseptic process must be clearly defined (each step clearly described)
  • Risks must be identified, assessed and controlled (driven by the CCS)
  • "Accepted residual risks" must be formally documented. This means aseptic processing can never be "zero risk", but every accepted risk must have a written justification

Analogy: Aseptic Processing vs. Terminal Sterilisation

Aseptic processing = preparing food in an operating theatre: The whole cooking process takes place in a sterile environment, all ingredients and tools are pre-sterilised, and the cook wears a sterile surgical gown, because the food will not be heated again once it is served. Every step must maintain this extremely high standard.

RABS = a reinforced cleanroom barrier: If a standard aseptic room is the "operating theatre", RABS is like building a further "reinforced isolated workstation" inside it, raising the distance and barrier between operators' hands and the product by another level. An isolator goes further still, almost completely separating personnel from the product.

Key Notes: Strategic Significance of RABS/Isolators in 8.9

8.9 uses "should be considered" rather than a mandatory requirement. This means the company must formally evaluate in its CCS whether to use RABS or an isolator, and record the reasons for the final decision.

Three routes to reducing personnel intervention (from lowest to highest degree of separation):

  • Open RABS → Closed RABS → Isolator (increasing separation)
  • Robotic automation (such as automatic loading of a lyophilizer)
  • Sterilisation in Place (SIP) (eliminates the risk of manual connections)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Classification of Interventions

Inherent Intervention: An operation that is part of the design of the production process itself, such as routine replenishment of empty containers or changing filling needles when changing format. These are "expected to occur" and must be validated in advance.

Corrective Intervention: An operation carried out to deal with an unexpected problem, such as removing a fallen vial or clearing a spill. These also need to be on the authorised list, but are "non-routine but permitted" operations.

Non-qualified Intervention: An intervention that is not on the authorised list. It may be used only in extreme and exceptional circumstances, requires authorisation from the quality unit, and the batch will not necessarily be released.

Analogy: Interventions

Interventions are like "changing posture during surgery": before the operation, the surgeon has already planned all the standard steps (inherent interventions = planned movements). Sometimes an instrument has a problem and an ad hoc adjustment is needed (corrective intervention = an adjustment in response to a sudden event). But a completely untrained, unplanned new move requires pausing the operation and getting authorisation from the attending physician (non-qualified intervention). The goal of the whole operation is to follow the plan as closely as possible and minimise unplanned actions.

Key Notes: Regulatory Enforcement Focus of 8.16

8.16 requires that interventions:

  • Have an "authorised list": interventions not on the list may not be performed at will
  • Are assessed through risk management and the APS (aseptic process simulation): the APS design must therefore cover all authorised interventions
  • The list must be "kept up to date": any new intervention must be assessed before it is added to the list

During inspections, typical inspector check points: Is the intervention list complete? Do batches involving non-qualified interventions have complete QA investigation records?

8.18: The Seven Maximum Hold Times

Seven points at which a maximum time must be set and validated:

(1) Time between equipment/component washing and sterilisation
(2) Time between sterilisation of equipment/components/containers and their use/filling
(3) Time between decontamination of the RABS/isolator and its use
(4) Time from the start of product preparation to filtration/sterilisation to the end of aseptic filling (overall process time)
(5) Time between product sterilisation and the start of filling
(6) Total duration of the aseptic processing
(7) Total filling time

Each time limit must:
  a. Be scientifically justified (product stability, microbial growth models)
  b. Be validated
  c. Be recorded and tracked in the batch record

Practical Application

In day-to-day CDMO operations, the seven time points in 8.18 usually need to be integrated into the time-stamp fields of the Batch Manufacturing Record. When any time exceeds the validated limit, the system should trigger an alert and require QA to review whether use can continue or the materials/equipment must be discarded. This is the key control for preventing high-risk practices such as continuing to fill a batch beyond its time limit.

8.22 Where final containers are closed by fusion, e.g. Blow-Fill-Seal (BFS), Form-Fill-Seal (FFS), Small and Large Volume Parenteral (SVP & LVP) bags, glass or plastic ampoules, the critical parameters and variables that affect seal integrity should be evaluated, determined, effectively controlled and monitored during operations. Glass ampoules, BFS units and small volume containers (<100 ml) closed by fusion should be subject to 100% integrity testing using validated methods. For large volume containers (>100 ml) closed by fusion, reduced sampling may be acceptable where scientifically justified and based on data demonstrating the consistency of the existing process, and a high level of process control. It should be noted that visual inspection is not considered as an acceptable integrity test method.

8.23 Samples of products using systems other than fusion should be taken and checked for integrity using validated methods. The frequency of testing should be based on the knowledge and experience of the container and closure systems being used. A scientifically justified sampling plan should be used. The sample size should be based on information such as supplier management, packaging component specifications and process knowledge.

8.24 Containers sealed under vacuum should be tested for maintenance of vacuum after an appropriate pre-determined period prior to certification/release and during shelf life.

8.25 The container closure integrity validation should take into consideration any transportation or shipping requirements that may negatively impact the integrity of the container (e.g. by decompression or extreme temperatures).

8.26 Where the equipment used to crimp vial caps can generate large quantities of non-viable particle, measures to prevent particle contamination such as locating the equipment at a physically separate station equipped with adequate air extraction should be taken.

8.27 Vial capping of aseptically filled products can be undertaken as an aseptic process using sterilised caps or as a clean process outside the aseptic processing area. Where the latter approach is adopted, vials should be protected by grade A conditions up to the point of leaving the aseptic processing area, and thereafter stoppered vials should be protected with a grade A air supply until the cap has been crimped. The supporting background environment of grade A air supply should meet at least grade D requirements. Where capping is a manual process, it should be performed under grade A conditions either in an appropriately designed isolator or in grade A with a grade B background.

8.28 Where capping of aseptically filled sterile product is conducted as a clean process with grade A air supply protection, vials with missing or displaced stoppers should be rejected prior to capping. Appropriately qualified, automated methods for stopper height detection should be in place.

8.29 Where human intervention is required at the capping station, appropriate technological and organizational measures should be used to prevent direct contact with the vials and to minimize contamination. RABS and isolators may be beneficial in assuring the required conditions.

8.30 All filled containers of parenteral products should be inspected individually for extraneous contamination or other defects. Defect classification and criticality should be determined during qualification and based on risk and historical knowledge. Factors to consider include, but are not limited to, the potential impact of the defect to the patient and the route of administration. Different defect types should be categorized and batch performance analysed. Batches with unusual levels of defects, when compared with routine defect numbers for the process (based on routine and trend data), should be investigated. A defect library should be generated and maintained which captures all known classes of defects. The defect library should be used for the training of production and quality assurance personnel. Critical defects should not be identified during any subsequent sampling and inspection of acceptable containers. Any critical defect identified subsequently should trigger an investigation as it indicates a possible failure of the original inspection process.

8.31 When inspection is performed manually, it should be conducted under suitable and controlled conditions of illumination and background. Inspection rates should be appropriately controlled and qualified. Operators performing the inspection should undergo visual inspection qualification (whilst wearing corrective lenses, if these are normally worn) at least annually. The qualification should be undertaken using appropriate samples from the manufacturer's defect library sets and taking into consideration worst case scenarios (e.g. inspection time, line speed where the product is transferred to the operator by a conveyor system, container size or fatigue) and should include consideration of eyesight checks. Operator distractions should be minimized and frequent breaks, of an appropriate duration, should be taken from inspection.

8.32 Where automated methods of inspection are used, the process should be validated to detect known defects (which may impact product quality or safety) and be equal to, or better than, manual inspection methods. The performance of the equipment should be challenged using representative defects prior to start up and at regular intervals throughout the batch.

8.33 Results of the inspection should be recorded and defect types and numbers trended. Reject levels for the various defect types should also be trended based on statistical principles. Impact to product on the market should be assessed as part of the investigation when adverse trends are observed.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Two Methods of Vial Capping (8.27-8.29)

Method 1: Aseptic Process: Pre-sterilised caps are used and capping is carried out in an aseptic environment. The whole process is performed in a Grade A environment, which is the highest standard.

Method 2: Clean Process: Partially stoppered vials leave the aseptic filling area under Grade A protection (positive-pressure laminar airflow), and capping is carried out under that Grade A airflow protection (the background need only be Grade D).

Key difference: the premise of the "clean process" is that the stoppers are fully seated (partially stoppered vials are not exposed to a non-sterile environment). 8.28 expressly requires stopper height to be checked, to ensure that no missing or displaced stoppers reach the capping station.

Core Concept: Container Closure Integrity (CCI: 8.22-8.25)

Container Closure Integrity (CCI) testing is key to ensuring that the sterile barrier is maintained. Several important principles in Annex 1:

  • Fusion-sealed containers (glass ampoules, BFS, FFS): Small volumes (<100 mL) must be 100% integrity tested.
  • Large-volume fusion-sealed containers (>100 mL): Sampling may be used, but it must be supported by scientific justification and good process control data.
  • Visual inspection is not a CCI method: This is an explicit statement in Annex 1 (2022): visual inspection shows only the appearance and cannot confirm micro-cracks or micro-leaks.
  • Transport considerations (8.25): CCI validation must include simulation of transport conditions (reduced pressure, temperature extremes).

Analogy: 100% Visual Inspection

100% visual inspection is like "a car with a reversing radar": every parking manoeuvre has a sensor to confirm it is safe. Manual visual inspection is like "judging the parking distance by eye alone", where sometimes you only find the problem when you are already too close. Annex 1 requires 100% inspection of every unit so that every defect that could harm a patient is intercepted, and no "acceptable defect rate" from statistical sampling is allowed to reach the market.

But visual inspection cannot replace CCI: The radar can tell you whether the outside of the car has a dent, but cannot detect problems inside the engine. Visual inspection sees cosmetic defects, while CCI tests seal integrity. The two are complementary and not interchangeable.

Key Notes: 100% Visual Inspection Requirements (8.30-8.33)

The four key requirements of 8.30:

  • Individual visual inspection: Regardless of batch size, every injectable unit must be visually inspected.
  • Defect classification system: There must be a systematic defect classification (Critical/Major/Minor), set at the time of qualification.
  • Defect Library: Must be established and maintained, and used for personnel training and qualification.
  • Trending: An abnormally high defect rate must be investigated; the later discovery of critical defects must trigger an investigation (which may indicate that the original visual inspection failed).

8.31 Key points for qualification of manual visual inspection: At least once a year, using samples from the defect library, and the worst case (speed, fatigue, container size) must be considered.

Practical Application

A CDMO's customer asks for the production of an injectable in 1 mL glass ampoules (fusion-sealed containers). According to 8.22:

  • 100% CCI testing is required (fusion-sealed containers <100 mL)
  • 100% visual inspection (required by 8.30)
  • Visual inspection cannot replace CCI

This means that, when selecting equipment, the CDMO must provide both a high-voltage leak detection machine (a common ampoule CCI method) AND a visual inspection station (manual or an automated AVI system). The validation strategies for the two are independent and must not be confused.

8.34–8.49 Sterilisation

Original Text

8.34 Where possible, finished product should be terminally sterilised, using a validated and controlled sterilisation process, as this provides a greater assurance of sterility than a validated and controlled sterile filtration process and/or aseptic processing. Where it is not possible for a product to undergo terminal sterilisation, consideration should be given to using post-aseptic processing terminal heat treatment, combined with aseptic process to give improved sterility assurance.

8.35 The selection, design and location of the equipment and cycle/programme used for sterilisation should be based on scientific principles and data which demonstrate repeatability and reliability of the sterilisation process. All parameters should be defined, and where critical, these should be controlled, monitored and recorded.

8.36 All sterilisation processes should be validated. Validation studies should take into account the product composition, storage conditions and maximum time between the start of the preparation of a product or material to be sterilised and its sterilisation. Before any sterilisation process is adopted, its suitability for the product and equipment, and its efficacy in consistently achieving the desired sterilising conditions in all parts of each type of load to be processed should be validated notably by physical measurements and where appropriate by Biological Indicators (BI). For effective sterilisation, the whole of the product, and surfaces of equipment and components should be subject to the required treatment and the process should be designed to ensure that this is achieved.

8.37 Particular attention should be given when the adopted product sterilisation method is not described in the current edition of the Pharmacopoeia, or when it is used for a product which is not a simple aqueous solution. Where possible, heat sterilisation is the method of choice.

8.38 Validated loading patterns should be established for all sterilisation processes and load patterns should be subject to periodic revalidation. Maximum and minimum loads should also be considered as part of the overall load validation strategy.

8.39 The validity of the sterilizing process should be reviewed and verified at scheduled intervals based on risk. Heat sterilization cycles should be revalidated with a minimum frequency of at least annually for load patterns that are considered worst case. Other load patterns should be validated at a frequency justified in the CCS.

8.40 Routine operating parameters should be established and adhered to for all sterilisation processes, e.g. physical parameters and loading patterns.

8.41 There should be mechanisms in place to detect a sterilisation cycle that does not conform to the validated parameters. Any failed sterilisation or sterilisation that deviated from the validated process (e.g. have longer or shorter phases such as heating cycles) should be investigated.

8.42 Suitable BIs placed at appropriate locations should be considered as an additional method to support the validation of the sterilisation process. BIs should be stored and used according to the manufacturer's instructions. Where BIs are used to support validation and/or to monitor a sterilisation process (e.g. with ethylene oxide), positive controls should be tested for each sterilisation cycle. If BIs are used, strict precautions should be taken to avoid transferring microbial contamination to the manufacturing or other testing processes. BI results in isolation should not be used to override other critical parameters and process design elements.

8.43 The reliability of BIs is important. Suppliers should be qualified and transportation and storage conditions should be controlled in order that BI quality is not compromised. Prior to use of a new batch/lot of BIs, the population, purity and identity of the indicator organism of the batch/lot should be verified. For other critical parameters, e.g. D-value, Z-value, the batch certificate provided by the qualified supplier can normally be used.

8.44 There should be a clear means of differentiating products, equipment and components, which have not been subjected to the sterilisation process from those which have. Equipment such as baskets or trays used to carry products, other items of equipment and/or components should be clearly labelled (or electronically tracked) with the product name and batch number and an indication of whether or not it has been sterilised. Indicators such as autoclave tape, or irradiation indicators may be used, where appropriate, to indicate whether or not a batch has passed through a sterilisation process. However, these indicators show only that the sterilisation process has occurred; they do not indicate product sterility or achievement of the required sterility assurance level.

8.45 Sterilisation records should be available for each sterilisation run. Each cycle should have a unique identifier. Their conformity should be reviewed and approved as part of the batch certification/release procedure.

8.46 Where required, materials, equipment and components should be sterilised by validated methods appropriate to the specific material. Suitable protection after sterilisation should be provided to prevent recontamination. If sterilised items are not used immediately after sterilisation, these should be stored using appropriately sealed packaging and a maximum hold time should be established. Where justified, components that have been packaged with multiple sterile packaging layers need not be stored in a cleanroom if the integrity and configuration of the sterile pack allows the items to be readily disinfected during transfer by operators into grade A (e.g. by the use of multiple sterile coverings that can be removed at each transfer from lower to higher grade). Where protection is achieved by containment in sealed packaging, this packaging process should be undertaken prior to sterilisation.

8.47 Where materials, equipment, components and ancillary items are sterilised in sealed packaging and then transferred into grade A, this should be done using appropriate validated methods (for example, airlocks or pass-through hatches) with accompanying disinfection of the exterior of the sealed packaging. The use of rapid transfer port technology should also be considered. These methods should be demonstrated to effectively control the potential risk of contamination of the grade A and grade B areas and, likewise, the disinfection procedure should be demonstrated to be effective in reducing any contamination on the packaging to acceptable levels for entry of the item into the grade B and grade A areas.

8.48 Where materials, equipment, components and ancillary items are sterilised in sealed packaging or containers, the packaging should be qualified for minimizing the risk of particulate, microbial, endotoxin/pyrogen or chemical contamination, and for compatibility with the selected sterilisation method. The packaging sealing process should be validated. The validation should consider the integrity of the sterile protective barrier system, the maximum hold time before sterilisation and the maximum shelf life assigned to the sterilised items. The integrity of the sterile protective barrier system for each of the sterilised items should be checked prior to use.

8.49 For materials, equipment, components and ancillary items that are not a direct or indirect product contact part and are necessary for aseptic processing but cannot be sterilised, an effective and validated disinfection and transfer process should be in place. These items, once disinfected, should be protected to prevent recontamination. These items, and others representing potential routes of contamination, should be included in the environmental monitoring programme.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Sterilisation Method Selection Ladder

8.34 establishes a clear "priority order of sterility assurance levels":

Terminal sterilisation (preferred)
Highest sterility assurance
▼
Aseptic processing + post-processing heat treatment
A compromise when the product cannot withstand terminal sterilisation
▼
Aseptic processing (no additional heat treatment)
When the product is heat-sensitive and cannot undergo any heat treatment

The logic of this ladder is that terminal sterilisation provides a higher Sterility Assurance Level than sterile filtration plus aseptic processing alone, so it should be chosen wherever possible.

Core Concept: The Role and Limitations of Biological Indicators (BIs)

A Biological Indicator (BI) is a carrier containing a known number of a specific microorganism (highly resistant spores), used to verify the effectiveness of a sterilisation process.

Important limitation in 8.42: "BI results in isolation should not be used to override other critical parameters and process design elements"

This means that even if all BIs pass, if the physical parameters (temperature, pressure, time) do not meet the validation requirements, the batch still cannot be released. The BI is a supporting tool, not the sole basis for judgement.

Analogy: Sterilisation Validation

Sterilisation validation is like "validating the temperature of an oven for baking bread":

  • Heat Distribution/Penetration testing: Confirms whether the temperature is uniform at every position in the oven; the corners and the middle must not differ too much.
  • Biological indicator (BI): At the "hardest-to-bake" position in the oven, you place a small piece of dough that has been "deliberately made with extra flour" (maximum resistance) and see whether it is cooked after baking. If the hardest spot is cooked, the whole oven load is cooked.
  • Autoclave tape: It only tells you that "the bread went into the oven"; it does not mean the temperature was reached, like a baking indicator sticker.

Explanation of Key BI Parameters

D-value:
  The time required under specific sterilisation conditions
  to reduce a microbial population by 1 log (90%)
  → The higher the D-value, the more heat-resistant the microorganism

Z-value:
  The temperature change (°C) required
  to change the D-value by 1 log
  → Describes the temperature sensitivity of the microorganism

SAL (Sterility Assurance Level):
  Standard for terminally sterilised products = 10⁻⁶
  (no more than 1 non-sterile unit per million products)

8.43 Before a new batch of BIs is used, the following must be confirmed:
  ✓ Population
  ✓ Purity
  ✓ Identity of the indicator organism
  (D-value and Z-value may rely on the supplier's batch certificate)

Key Notes: Management of Materials After Sterilisation (8.44-8.49)

8.44: Limitations of sterilisation indicators: Autoclave tape or radiation indicators can only confirm that an item "has been through a sterilisation process"; they do not show that the required sterility assurance level was achieved. This is a common misunderstanding, and inspectors specifically check whether a company understands and uses such indicators correctly.

8.47: Rapid Transfer Port (RTP): When sterilised packages need to be transferred into Grade A, RTP technology minimises the risk of contamination from the outer packaging during transfer. It is an important technology in modern aseptic facilities.

8.49: Materials that cannot be sterilised: Some ancillary materials (such as special sensors and electronic components) cannot be sterilised, but they must go through an "effective and validated disinfection and transfer-in process" and be included in the environmental monitoring programme. This is a compliance blind spot in Annex 1 and is easily overlooked during inspections.

Practical Application

When a CDMO introduces a new steam autoclave, the validation strategy should include:

  • IQ/OQ: Qualification of equipment installation and operation
  • PQ, heat distribution testing: Empty chamber, minimum load and maximum load
  • PQ, heat penetration testing: Confirm every approved loading pattern
  • PQ, BI challenge testing: Place BIs at the "cold spot" and confirm SAL ≥ 10⁻⁶
  • Annual revalidation: Worst-case load at least once a year (8.39)

In addition, a "loading pattern" document must be established for each approved loading pattern, and operators must load strictly according to it. Any deviation is a deviation event and must be investigated (8.41).

Section Summary (8.1–8.49)

  • Terminal sterilisation (8.1-8.6): Grade requirements are less stringent than for aseptic processing (general filling in Grade C), but in high-risk situations (wide-necked containers, slow filling) an upgrade to Grade A (with a Grade C background) is needed. The CCS drives grade selection.
  • Table 3 vs Table 4: In terminal sterilisation processes, Grade A is an "option for unusually high risk"; in aseptic processing, Grade A is "the standard requirement for filling itself".
  • RABS/Isolator (8.9): Should be assessed within the CCS, with the aim of reducing critical personnel interventions in Grade A. When an isolator is used, the Grade A background requirement is lowered (see paragraph 4.20).
  • Interventions (8.16-8.17): There must be an authorised list (inherent + corrective interventions), validated through the APS; non-qualified interventions require QA authorisation and investigation, and may affect batch release.
  • Time limits (8.18): All seven points in aseptic processing must have maximum times set, validated and tracked.
  • Vial capping (8.27-8.29): Either an aseptic process (Grade A throughout) or a clean process (Grade A airflow protection + Grade D background) may be chosen; the clean process must be combined with automatic stopper height detection.
  • 100% visual inspection (8.30-8.33): All injectables are inspected individually; there must be a defect library, a classification system, annual operator qualification, and trend analysis.
  • CCI (8.22-8.25): Small-volume fusion-sealed containers require 100% CCI testing; visual inspection is not a CCI method; CCI validation must include transport conditions.
  • Sterilisation priority order (8.34): Terminal sterilisation > post-process heat treatment + aseptic processing > aseptic processing alone. Terminal sterilisation should be chosen wherever possible.
  • BI limitations (8.42): The BI is a supporting tool and must not be used in isolation to override other critical physical parameters; autoclave tape can only confirm that "the process took place", not that the SAL was achieved.
  • Management of materials after sterilisation (8.44-8.49): Sterilised materials need clear identification and a maximum storage time; transfer into Grade A requires validated methods (RTP, airlock); materials that cannot be sterilised need a validated disinfection process and must be included in environmental monitoring.

8.50–8.54 — General Requirements: Sterilisation by Heat

Original Text

8.50 Each heat sterilisation cycle should be recorded either electronically or by hardcopy, using equipment with suitable accuracy and precision. The system should have safeguards and/or redundancy in its control and monitoring instrumentation to detect a cycle not conforming to the validated cycle parameter requirements and abort or fail this cycle (e.g. by the use of duplex/double probes connected to independent control and monitoring systems).

8.51 The position of the temperature probes used for controlling and/or recording should be determined during the validation and selected based on system design and in order to correctly record and represent routine cycle conditions. Validation studies should be designed to demonstrate the suitability of system control and recording probe locations, and should include the verification of the function and location of these probes by the use of an independent monitoring probe located at the same position during validation.

8.52 The whole of the load should reach the required temperature before measurement of the sterilising time-period starts. For sterilisation cycles controlled by using a reference probe within the load, specific consideration should be given to ensuring the load probe temperature is controlled within defined temperature range prior to cycle commencement.

8.53 After completion of the high temperature phase of a heat sterilisation cycle, precautions should be taken against contamination of a sterilised load during cooling. Any cooling liquid or gas that comes into contact with the product or sterilised material should be sterilised.

8.54 In those cases where parametric release has been authorized, a robust system should be applied to the product lifecycle validation and the routine monitoring of the manufacturing process. This system should be periodically reviewed. Further guidance regarding parametric release is provided in Annex 17.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Heat Sterilisation Cycle Recording: Every sterilisation cycle must have a complete electronic or paper record, and the autoclave must be fitted with duplex (double) probes connected to independent control and monitoring systems. This is a concrete expression of the GMP principle of redundancy, ensuring that any cycle deviating from the validated parameters is detected immediately and aborted.

Probe Position Validation: Probe positions must be determined during validation and verified with an additional independent monitoring probe placed in the same position. This ensures that the control probe used in routine operation truly represents the actual temperature conditions in the load.

Parametric Release: A release approach in which no routine sterility test is performed and sterility is instead demonstrated by fully documented process parameters. It applies to terminally sterilised products with a highly robust sterilisation process, requires special authorisation from the regulatory authority, and must comply with Annex 17.

Analogy

Imagine a high-end oven. It has not just one thermometer but two independent thermometers (duplex probes) monitoring the oven temperature at the same time. If the two readings disagree, the oven stops automatically and sounds an alarm. This is exactly the logic of the duplex probe design in an autoclave: making sure no abnormality slips through and lets a sub-standard batch be released.

Parametric release is like this: you don't need to wait until after the meal to know the pot got hot. You know how long the gas was on and how high the temperature was, so you are confident of the result. This is the concept of replacing the final sterility test with process parameters.

Key Notes

Contamination risk during cooling: 8.53 specifically warns that the cooling phase after sterilisation is also a critical risk point. If the cooling water or cooling gas is not sterile, it can introduce contamination while the product is still in the chamber. This is often overlooked in practice, but it is an explicit inspection item in regulatory audits.

Start of the sterilisation hold time: Timing must start only after the entire load has reached the required temperature (8.52). Only when the coldest point (worst case location) of the load has reached target does the whole load count as having reached target.

8.55–8.65 — Moist Heat Sterilisation

Original Text

8.55 Moist heat sterilisation can be achieved using steam, (direct or indirect contact), but also includes other systems such as superheated water systems (cascade or immersion cycles) that could be used for containers that may be damaged by other cycle designs (e.g. Blow-Fill-Seal containers, plastic bags).

8.56 The items to be sterilised, other than products in sealed containers, should be dry, packaged in a protective barrier system which allows removal of air and penetration of steam and prevents recontamination after sterilisation. All loaded items should be dry upon removal from the steriliser. Load dryness should be confirmed by visual inspection as a part of the sterilisation process acceptance.

8.57 For porous cycles (hard goods), time, temperature and pressure should be used to monitor the process and be recorded. Each sterilised item should be inspected for damage, packaging material integrity and moisture on removal from the autoclave. Any item found not to be fit for purpose should be removed from the manufacturing area and an investigation performed.

8.58 For autoclaves capable of performing prevacuum sterilisation cycles, the temperature should be recorded at the chamber drain throughout the sterilisation period. Load probes may also be used where appropriate but the controlling system should remain related to the load validation. For steam in place systems, the temperature should be recorded at appropriate condensate drain locations throughout the sterilisation period.

8.59 Validation of porous cycles should include a calculation of equilibration time, exposure time, correlation of pressure and temperature and the minimum/maximum temperature range during exposure. Validation of fluid cycles should include temperature, time and/or F0. Critical processing parameters should be subject to defined limits (including appropriate tolerances) and be confirmed as part of the sterilisation validation and routine cycle acceptance criteria.

8.60 Leak tests on the steriliser should be carried out periodically (normally weekly) when a vacuum phase is part of the cycle or the system is returned, post-sterilisation, to a pressure lower than the environment surrounding the steriliser.

8.61 There should be adequate assurance of air removal prior to and during sterilisation when the sterilisation process includes air purging (e.g. porous autoclave loads, lyophilizer chambers). For autoclaves, this should include an air removal test cycle (normally performed on a daily basis) or the use of an air detector system. Loads to be sterilised should be designed to support effective air removal and be free draining to prevent the build-up of condensate.

8.62 Distortion and damage of non-rigid containers that are terminally sterilised, such as containers produced by Blow-Fill-Seal or Form-Fill-Seal technologies, should be prevented by appropriate cycle design and control (for instance setting correct pressure, heating and cooling rates and loading patterns).

8.63 Where steam in place (SIP) systems are used for sterilisation (e.g. for fixed pipework, vessels and lyophilizer chambers), the system should be appropriately designed and validated to assure all parts of the system are subjected to the required treatment. The system should be monitored for temperature, pressure and time at appropriate locations during routine use to ensure all areas are effectively and reproducibly sterilised. These locations should be demonstrated as being representative of, and correlated with, the slowest to heat locations during initial and routine validation. Once a system has been sterilised by steam in place, it should remain integral and where operations require, maintained under positive pressure or otherwise equipped with a sterilising vent filter prior to use.

8.64 In fluids load cycles where superheated water is used as the heat transfer medium, the heated water should consistently reach all of the required contact points. Initial qualification studies should include temperature mapping of the entire load. There should be routine checks on the equipment to ensure that nozzles (where the water is introduced) are not blocked and drains remain free from debris.

8.65 Validation of the sterilisation of fluids loads in a superheated water autoclave should include temperature mapping of the entire load and heat penetration and reproducibility studies. All parts of the load should heat up uniformly and achieve the desired temperature for the specified time. Routine temperature monitoring probes should be correlated to the worst case positions identified during the qualification process.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Moist Heat Sterilisation: The most common terminal sterilisation method, which kills microorganisms using steam (by direct or indirect contact). Its main mechanism is that steam condenses and releases latent heat, which heats and denatures proteins. Typical conditions are 121°C / 15 min or higher temperatures.

Porous vs Fluid Cycles:

  • Porous cycle: used for hard goods, packaging materials, textiles, etc. Air must be removed (air is a barrier to heat transfer). Critical parameters: time, temperature, pressure, equilibration time
  • Fluid cycle: used for liquids in bottles. The liquid itself is the heat transfer medium. Critical parameters: temperature, time, F0 value

F0 Value (Lethality Value): The cumulative lethality calculated with 121°C as the reference temperature (Z = 10°C). F0 = 8 min is generally regarded as the minimum acceptance standard, but it must be determined according to the product bioburden and the sterility assurance level (SAL = 10⁻⁶).

Steam-in-Place (SIP): Sterilisation by passing steam through fixed piping, vessels or freeze dryer chambers without dismantling the equipment. After SIP the system must remain closed and be kept under positive pressure or fitted with a sterilising vent filter to prevent external contamination.

Analogy

Autoclave = industrial-grade pressure cooker: Just as a household pressure cooker uses steam pressure to cook food above 120°C, a pharmaceutical autoclave uses the same principle to "cook" microorganisms to death. The difference is that an autoclave must record temperature and pressure precisely every second, and automatically detect abnormalities and abort the cycle.

Air removal in porous cycles: Imagine heating a tightly rolled towel with steam. If there are air bubbles in the middle, the steam cannot penetrate. This is why porous loads require a pre-vacuum to remove the air, so that steam can penetrate evenly into every corner.

SIP = disinfecting the hot water pipes at home: SIP is like letting hot steam flow from one end of the piping to the other without dismantling it, so that the whole line is heated and sterilised. The key question is: how do you confirm that the "hardest-to-heat corner" of the piping (the slowest to heat location) has really reached sterilisation temperature?

Formula and Calculation

F0 Value Calculation:

F0 = ∫ 10^((T-121)/10) dt

Where:
  T = actual temperature (°C)
  121 = reference temperature (°C)
  Z = 10°C (standard Z value for steam sterilisation)

Examples:
  If 121°C is maintained throughout for 8 minutes
  → F0 = 10^((121-121)/10) × 8 = 1 × 8 = 8 min

  If the temperature rises to 125°C and is held for 3 minutes
  → F0 = 10^((125-121)/10) × 3
       = 10^0.4 × 3 ≈ 2.51 × 3 ≈ 7.5 min

Common design target: F0 ≥ 8 min (depending on bioburden)
SAL target: 10⁻⁶ (i.e. a one-in-a-million probability of a non-sterile unit)

Key Notes

Leak test frequency (8.60): When the autoclave cycle includes a vacuum phase, a leak test must be performed weekly. This requirement is often overlooked, especially in preventive maintenance programmes. An autoclave that leaks cannot remove air effectively, leading to sterilisation failure.

Air removal test (8.61): Performing an air removal test (Bowie-Dick test or equivalent) once a day is standard industry practice. For SIP of freeze dryer chambers, air removal must be specifically confirmed for the whole chamber (including the condenser area).

Special challenges of BFS/FFS containers (8.62): Plastic containers (BFS, FFS) deform easily under high-pressure steam. Container damage must be prevented through appropriate pressure compensation (pressurised water cooling) and control of heating and cooling rates; a superheated water autoclave is the preferred choice for these products.

Maintaining pressure after SIP (8.63): After SIP is completed, the system must immediately be placed under positive pressure protection or fitted with a sterilising vent filter. If the system returns to atmospheric pressure without protection, external microorganisms may be drawn back into the freshly sterilised piping system.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: A client asks for terminal sterilisation of an IV bag (Blow-Fill-Seal container). A conventional steam autoclave may deform the plastic bag in hot steam. You need to consider:

  • Selecting a superheated water autoclave, using water as the heat transfer medium to provide more uniform temperature distribution
  • Designing an appropriate pressure compensation (overpressure) programme to prevent the container from expanding and deforming
  • Validating temperature mapping of the entire load to identify the coldest point (worst case location)
  • Confirming that the nozzles are not blocked and drainage is unobstructed, and including them in the routine maintenance programme

8.66–8.70 — Dry Heat Sterilisation & Depyrogenation

Original Text

8.66 Dry heat sterilisation utilizes high temperatures of air or gas to sterilise a product or article. Dry heat sterilisation is of particular use in the thermal removal of difficult-to-eliminate thermally robust contaminants such as endotoxin/pyrogen and is often used in the preparation of components for aseptic filling. The combination of time and temperature to which product, components or equipment are exposed should produce an adequate and reproducible level of lethality and/or endotoxin/pyrogen inactivation/removal when operated routinely within the established limits. The process may be operated in an oven or in a continuous tunnel process, e.g. for sterilisation and depyrogenation of glass containers.

8.67 Dry heat sterilisation/depyrogenation tunnels should be configured to ensure that airflow protects the integrity and performance of the grade A sterilising zone by maintaining appropriate pressure differentials and airflow through the tunnel. Air pressure difference profiles should be assessed. The impact of any airflow change should be assessed to ensure the heating profile is maintained. All air supplied to the tunnel should pass through at least a HEPA filter and periodic tests (at least biannually) should be performed to demonstrate air filter integrity. Any tunnel parts that come into contact with sterilised components should be appropriately sterilised or disinfected. Critical process parameters that should be considered during validation and/or routine processing should include, but are not limited to:

  1. belt speed or dwell time within the sterilising zone,
  2. temperature — minimum and maximum temperatures,
  3. heat penetration of the material/article,
  4. heat distribution/uniformity,
  5. airflows determined by air pressure difference profiles correlated with the heat distribution and penetration studies.

8.68 When a thermal process is used as part of the depyrogenation process for any component or product contact equipment/material, validation studies should be performed to demonstrate that the process provides a suitable Fh value and results in a minimum 3 log10 reduction in endotoxin concentration. When this is attained, there is no additional requirement to demonstrate sterilisation in these cases.

8.69 Containers spiked with endotoxin should be used during validation and should be carefully managed with a full reconciliation performed. Containers should be representative of the materials normally processed (in respect to composition of the packaging materials, porosity, dimensions, nominal volume). Endotoxin quantification and recovery efficiency should also be demonstrated.

8.70 Dry heat ovens are typically employed to sterilise or depyrogenate primary packaging components, starting materials or active substances but may be used for other processes. They should be maintained at a positive pressure relative to lower grade clean areas throughout the sterilisation and post sterilisation hold process unless the integrity of the packaging is maintained. All air entering the oven should pass through a HEPA filter. Critical process parameters that should be considered in qualification and/or routine processing should include, but are not limited to:

  1. temperature,
  2. exposure period/time,
  3. chamber pressure (for maintenance of over pressure),
  4. air speed,
  5. air quality within the oven,
  6. heat penetration of material/article (slow to heat spots),
  7. heat distribution/uniformity,
  8. load pattern and configuration of articles to be sterilised/depyrogenated including minimum and maximum loads.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Key differences between dry heat and moist heat sterilisation: Dry heat relies on air to conduct heat, and its heat transfer efficiency is far lower than that of steam (latent heat). Dry heat therefore requires higher temperatures (typically 160–250°C) and longer times. Its advantage is that it effectively degrades pyrogens/endotoxins, which steam sterilisation cannot achieve.

Depyrogenation: Pyrogens refer mainly to the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) of Gram-negative bacteria, i.e. endotoxin. Even after the microorganisms have been killed, the endotoxin remaining from their cell walls can still cause a febrile reaction in patients. Dry heat is the standard method for depyrogenating glass containers.

Fh Value (Heat Lethality for Depyrogenation): Similar to the F0 value, Fh is calculated with 250°C as the reference temperature (Z = 46.4°C) and expresses the cumulative effect of a dry heat process. Acceptance criterion: Fh must be sufficient to achieve a 3-log10 endotoxin reduction (i.e. from 1000 EU/container to <1 EU/container).

Analogy

Dry heat depyrogenation = baking off the toxin in an oven: Imagine the dead bodies of bacteria (endotoxin) on a glass ampoule that need to be "burned off". Steam can kill bacteria but has no effect on heat-resistant endotoxin, much like rinsing a burnt baking tray with hot water: the residue is still there. Only prolonged baking in a high-temperature oven (250°C or above) can completely break down these heat-resistant substances.

Depyrogenation tunnel = conveyor-belt oven: Industrial-scale depyrogenation does not use batch ovens but continuous tunnel ovens: glass vials move on a conveyor belt and receive enough heat exposure as they pass through the high-temperature zone. Belt speed is the critical process parameter: too fast means insufficient heat exposure; too slow means low efficiency and possible damage to the containers.

Formula and Calculation

Depyrogenation Acceptance Criteria:

Must achieve: at least a 3-log10 endotoxin reduction

Example:
  Validation challenge: 1000 EU/container (spiked amount)
  Acceptance criterion: ≤ 1 EU/container

Fh calculation (reference temperature 250°C, Z = 46.4°C):
  Fh = ∫ 10^((T-250)/46.4) dt

  Held at 250°C for 30 min → Fh = 30 min
  Held at 260°C for 30 min
  → Fh = 10^((260-250)/46.4) × 30 ≈ 1.65 × 30 ≈ 50 min

Typical dry heat depyrogenation conditions:
  - Oven: 250°C × 30 min (or equivalent Fh)
  - Tunnel: 320–350°C × several minutes (high temperature, short time)

Key Notes

What 3-log10 means (8.68): This is a PIC/S / EU GMP regulatory requirement and must be demonstrated during validation with endotoxin-spiked containers. It is not a calculated value but experimental data. If Fh is sufficient, no additional sterilisation confirmation is needed.

Control of spiked containers (8.69): The endotoxin-spiked containers used for validation must be strictly controlled and fully reconciled: every spiked container must be traced to its final result. The quantification of the spiked endotoxin and the recovery rate must also be validated to ensure the reliability of the test method.

Positive pressure requirement for dry heat ovens (8.70): A dry heat oven must maintain positive pressure relative to the lower-grade cleanroom areas during sterilisation and the post-sterilisation holding period, to prevent environmental contamination from entering. Operators often overlook this, especially when opening the door to remove items.

HEPA filtration (8.67, 8.70): All air entering the tunnel or oven must pass through at least one HEPA filter, and an integrity test (PAO/DOP test) must be performed at least every six months.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: A new client commissions filling of a water-for-injection-based (WFI-based) solution into glass ampoules. Your QA needs to confirm that:

  • The glass ampoules have undergone depyrogenation (≥3-log10 endotoxin reduction), or the supplier provides a qualified CoA that meets USP/EP standards
  • If depyrogenation is performed in-house: the validation report must include the Fh calculation, the endotoxin-spiked container study (including recovery) and complete process parameter records
  • Belt speed verification of the depyrogenation tunnel is a key parameter for ongoing annual monitoring, and any change requires re-confirmation

8.71–8.72 — Sterilisation by Radiation

Original Text

8.71 Sterilisation by radiation is used mainly for the sterilisation of heat sensitive materials and products. Ultraviolet irradiation is not an acceptable method of sterilisation. Guidance regarding ionising radiation sterilisation can be found within Annex 12.

8.72 Validation procedures should ensure that the effects of variation in density of the product and packages are considered.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Radiation Sterilisation:Uses ionising radiation to destroy the DNA of microorganisms. The main forms are:

  • Gamma rays (γ rays): emitted by cobalt-60 (Co-60) or caesium-137, with strong penetration, suitable for bulk sterilisation of large packages
  • Electron beam (E-beam): generated by accelerated electrons, with weaker penetration but a high dose rate, suitable for thin or single-layer items
  • X-rays: intermediate between the two, with strong penetration, and increasing commercial use in recent years

UV radiation is not accepted: UV is non-ionising radiation with very poor penetration; it can only kill microorganisms on surfaces and cannot assure sterility inside the packaging, so it is expressly excluded from the sterilisation methods in PIC/S Annex 1.

Dosimetry: Radiation sterilisation must use dosimeters to measure the actual absorbed dose (kGy), ensuring that the minimum dose zone receives the design dose and that the maximum dose does not exceed the product's tolerance limit.

Analogy

Radiation sterilisation is like "irradiating" a packaged product with X-rays: without opening the package and without high temperature, the microorganisms inside can be killed. Products of uneven density are like walls of different thickness blocking X-rays to different degrees: areas of higher density let less radiation through and can form under-dosed "shadow zones", which is exactly why validation must take density variation into account.

Key Notes

Density affects dose distribution (8.72): Variation in product density and packaging density directly affects how uniformly the radiation dose is distributed. Validation must test the maximum and minimum density cases (worst case) to ensure that, across the whole possible density range, the minimum absorbed dose is still sufficient to achieve a SAL of 10⁻⁶.

Radiation sterilisation is usually outsourced (contract sterilisation): Most pharmaceutical manufacturers do not build their own radiation sterilisation facilities but commission specialist providers (such as Sterigenics, BGS, Ionisos). The CDMO needs to pay particular attention to supplier qualification, control of transport conditions and the completeness of dose confirmation records, all of which are audit focus areas.

See Annex 12 for detailed guidance: PIC/S Annex 1 gives only brief requirements on radiation sterilisation; detailed requirements (including dose setting, biological indicators and dosimeter calibration) are in Annex 12 (Manufacture of Radiopharmaceuticals and radiation sterilisation).

  1. Why is UV irradiation not accepted as a sterilisation method, yet acceptable for environmental surface disinfection (such as UV lamps in cleanrooms)? What is the essential difference between the two applications?
  2. If product density within the same batch varies by ±15% because of uneven filling, how should the radiation sterilisation validation be designed?
8.73–8.79 — Sterilisation with Ethylene Oxide

Original Text

8.73 This method should only be used when no other method is practicable. During process validation, it should be shown that there is no damaging effect on the product and that the conditions and time allowed for degassing result in the reduction of any residual ethylene oxide (EO) gas and reaction products to defined acceptable limits for the given product or material.

8.74 Direct contact between gas and microbial cells is essential, precautions should be taken to avoid the presence of organisms likely to be enclosed in material such as crystals or dried protein. The nature, porosity and quantity of packaging materials can significantly affect the process.

8.75 Before exposure to the gas, materials should be brought into equilibrium with the humidity and temperature required by the process. Where steam is used to condition the load for sterilisation, it should be of an appropriate quality. The time required for this should be balanced against the opposing need to minimize the time before sterilisation.

8.76 Each sterilisation cycle should be monitored with suitable BIs (Biological Indicators), using the appropriate number of test units distributed throughout the load at defined locations that have been shown to be worst case locations during validation.

8.77 Critical process parameters that could be considered as part of the sterilisation process validation and routine monitoring include, but are not limited to:

  1. EO gas concentration,
  2. pressure,
  3. amount of EO gas used,
  4. relative humidity,
  5. temperature,
  6. exposure time.

8.78 After sterilisation, the load should be aerated to allow EO gas and/or its reaction products to desorb from the packaged product to predetermined levels. Aeration can occur within a steriliser chamber and/or in a separate aeration chamber or aeration room. The aeration phase should be validated as part of the overall EO sterilisation process validation.

8.79 If the product cannot be sterilised in its final container, solutions or liquids should be sterilised by filtration through a sterile sterilising grade filter (with a nominal pore size of a maximum of 0.22 µm that has been appropriately validated to obtain a sterile filtrate) and subsequently aseptically filled into a previously sterilised container. The selection of the filter used should ensure that it is compatible with the product and as described in the marketing authorization (see paragraph 8.135).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Ethylene Oxide (EO) Sterilisation: EO is an alkylating agent that reacts with microbial DNA so that it can no longer replicate. EO is a gas that can penetrate packaging materials to reach all surfaces, making it especially suitable for complex medical devices that cannot withstand heat or radiation (such as catheters containing electronic components, and prefilled syringes).

Last-resort principle (8.73): PIC/S expressly specifies that EO should be used only when "no other method is practicable". The reasons: EO is toxic, carcinogenic and explosive, residue control is complex, and a lengthy aeration procedure is needed, posing health risks to both the environment and operators.

The four key process parameters of EO (8.77):

  • EO concentration (mg/L): insufficient concentration leads to incomplete sterilisation
  • Relative humidity (RH): EO needs moisture to alkylate DNA effectively, typically 40–80% RH
  • Temperature: affects the EO penetration rate and microbial activity, typically 37–63°C
  • Exposure time: determined by concentration and temperature

Aeration (8.78): EO and its reaction products (ethylene chlorohydrin, ethylene glycol) are all toxic, and sufficient aeration is required to bring the residues below the safe limits (defined in ISO 10993-7). Aeration is itself part of the process and must be validated.

Filtration sterilisation (8.79): When a product cannot undergo any form of terminal sterilisation, a 0.22 µm sterilising grade filter is used for filtration sterilisation, followed by aseptic filling. This is a core step of aseptic processing; see the later paragraphs of Section 8 for details.

Analogy

EO sterilisation = a tent for chemical fumigation: Imagine a large tent (the sterilisation chamber) filled with packaged medical devices that need sterilising. A toxic gas (EO) is introduced into the tent and permeates every gap in the packaging, killing the microorganisms inside. When finished, the tent must be thoroughly ventilated (aeration), and the products can be safely removed only after the toxic gas has completely dissipated.

The difficulty with EO is that you must confirm not only that "the gas gets in" but also that "the residual toxic substances get out". Both steps require independent validation.

Key Notes

Importance of direct contact (8.74): EO must come into direct contact with microbial cells to kill them effectively. Microorganisms embedded in crystalline material or dried protein cannot be reached by EO, which is a known failure mode of EO sterilisation. This is also why the bioburden and form of the product before EO must be controlled.

Pre-conditioning (8.75): Humidity equilibration before EO is critical because the lethality of EO depends heavily on moisture. If pre-conditioning time is too short (insufficient water inside the microorganisms), EO alkylation efficiency drops; if it is too long, the risk of microbial growth may increase. The optimal balance must be found during validation.

Use of biological indicators (BI) (8.76): Every EO sterilisation cycle must use biological indicators (usually Bacillus atrophaeus, i.e. B. subtilis var. niger). The BI must be placed in the "positions hardest for EO to reach" (worst case locations), and these positions must be confirmed during validation.

EO residue limits (post-aeration):According to ISO 10993-7, EO residue limits depend on the type of product contact:

  • Cardiovascular and eye-contact devices: strictest (EO < 25 mg/device)
  • Prolonged contact (>30 days): stricter
  • Limited contact (<24 h): relatively lenient
In every case, aeration validation must reliably achieve the target limit.

8.79–8.95 Sterilisation by Filtration

Original Text

8.79 If the product cannot be sterilised in its final container, solutions or liquids should be sterilised by filtration through a sterile sterilising grade filter (with a nominal pore size of a maximum of 0.22 µm that has been appropriately validated to obtain a sterile filtrate) and subsequently aseptically filled into a previously sterilised container. The selection of the filter used should ensure that it is compatible with the product and as described in the marketing authorization.

8.80 Suitable bioburden reduction prefilters and/or sterilising grade filters may be used at multiple points during the manufacturing process to ensure a low and controlled bioburden of the liquid prior to the final sterilising filter. Due to the potential additional risks of a sterile filtration process, as compared with other sterilisation processes, an additional filtration through a sterile sterilising grade filter, as close to the point of fill as possible, should be considered as part of an overall CCS.

8.81 The selection of components for the filtration system and their interconnection and arrangement within the filtration system, including pre-filters, should be based on the critical quality attributes of the product, justified and documented. The filtration system should minimize the generation of fibres and particles, not cause or contribute to unacceptable levels of impurities, or possess characteristics that otherwise alter the quality and efficacy of the product. Similarly, the filter characteristics should be compatible with the fluid and not be adversely affected by the product to be filtered. Adsorption of product components and extraction/leaching of filter components should be evaluated.

8.82 The filtration system should be designed to:

  • i. allow operation within validated process parameters;
  • ii. maintain the sterility of the filtrate;
  • iii. minimize the number of aseptic connections required between the final sterilising grade filter and the final filling of the product;
  • iv. allow cleaning procedures to be conducted as necessary;
  • v. allow sterilisation procedures, including sterilisation in place, to be conducted as necessary;
  • vi. permit in-place integrity testing, of the 0.22 µm final sterilising grade filter, preferably as a closed system, both prior to, and following filtration as necessary.

8.83 Sterile filtration of liquids should be validated in accordance with relevant Pharmacopeia requirements. Validation can be grouped by different strengths or variations of a product but should be done under worst case conditions. The rationale for grouping should be justified and documented.

8.84 During filter validation, wherever possible, the product to be filtered should be used for bacterial retention testing of the sterilising grade filter. Where the product to be filtered is not suitable for use in bacterial retention testing, a suitable surrogate product should be justified for use in the test. The challenge organism used in the bacterial retention test should be justified.

8.85 Filtration parameters that should be considered and established during validation should include, but are not limited to:

  • i. The wetting fluid used for filter integrity testing (based on filter manufacturer's recommendation or the fluid to be filtered; the appropriate integrity test value specification should be established)
  • ii. Filtration process conditions including: fluid pre-filtration holding time and effect on bioburden; filter conditioning; maximum filtration time/total time filter is in contact with the fluid; maximum operating pressure; flow rate; maximum filtration volume; temperature; the time taken to filter a known volume of bulk solution and the pressure difference across the filter

8.86 Routine process controls should be implemented to ensure adherence to validated filtration parameters. Results of critical process parameters should be included in the batch record, including but not limited to the minimum time taken to filter a known volume of bulk solution and pressure difference across the filter. Any significant difference from critical parameters during manufacturing should be documented and investigated.

"8.87 The integrity of the sterilised filter assembly should be verified by integrity testing before use (pre-use post sterilisation integrity test or PUPSIT), to check for damage and loss of integrity caused by the filter preparation prior to use. A sterilising grade filter that is used to sterilise a fluid should be subject to a non-destructive integrity test post-use prior to removal of the filter from its housing..."

8.87 (continued) It is recognized that PUPSIT may not always be possible after sterilisation due to process constraints (e.g. the filtration of very small volumes of solution). In these cases, an alternative approach may be taken providing that a thorough risk assessment has been performed. Points to consider in such a risk assessment include:

  • i. in depth knowledge and control of the filter sterilisation process to minimize damage potential
  • ii. in depth knowledge of the supply chain (contract sterilisation facilities, transport, packaging)
  • iii. process knowledge: specific product type including particle burden; pre-filtration steps that remove particle burden

8.88 The integrity of critical sterile gas and air vent filters (directly linked to product sterility) should be verified by testing after use, with the filter remaining in the filter assembly or housing.

8.89 The integrity of non-critical air or gas vent filters should be confirmed and recorded at appropriate intervals. Where gas filters are in place for extended periods, integrity testing should be carried out at installation and prior to replacement. The maximum duration of use should be specified and monitored based on risk.

8.90 For gas filtration, unintended moistening or wetting of the filter or filter equipment should be avoided.

8.91 If the sterilising filtration process has been validated as a system consisting of multiple filters to achieve the sterility for a given fluid, the filtration system is considered to be a single sterilising unit and all filters within the system should satisfactorily pass integrity testing after use.

8.92 In a redundant filtration system (where a second redundant sterilising grade filter is present as a backup but the sterilising process is validated as only requiring one filter), post-use integrity test of the primary sterilising grade filter should be performed and if demonstrated to be integral, then a post-use integrity test of the redundant (backup) filter is not necessary. However, in the event of a failure of the post-use integrity test on the primary filter, post-use integrity test on the secondary (redundant) filter should be performed, in conjunction with an investigation and risk assessment.

8.93 Bioburden samples should be taken from the bulk product and immediately prior to the final sterile filtration. In case where a redundant filtration set-up is used, it should be taken prior to the first filter. Systems for taking samples should be designed so as not to introduce contamination.

8.94 Liquid sterilising grade filters should be discarded after the processing of a single batch and the same filter should not be used continuously for more than one working day unless such use has been validated.

8.95 Where campaign manufacture of a product has been appropriately justified in the CCS and validated, the filter user should: (i) assess and document risks for filter duration; (ii) conduct validation studies; (iii) document maximum validated duration; (iv) implement controls to remove contaminated or defective filters.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Sterilising Filtration Systems

Sterilising grade filter: a filter with a nominal pore size of 0.22 µm or smaller that has been validated to retain microorganisms. It is the last line of sterility assurance for products that cannot be terminally sterilised.

Bioburden reduction prefilter: placed upstream of the sterilising grade filter to reduce the bioburden of the liquid, lessen the load on the final sterilising filter, extend filter life and improve overall sterility assurance.

System design principle (8.82): a sterilising filtration system must minimise the number of aseptic connections between the sterilising filter and filling. Every connection point is a potential contamination risk.

Adsorption & leaching: the filter itself may adsorb the active ingredient (affecting concentration) or release chemicals from the filter materials into the product. Both must be assessed.

Analogy: Sterilising Filtration as the "Last Security Gate"

Think of a sterilising filtration system as airport security. The baggage X-ray machine at the front end (the bioburden reduction prefilter) screens out the large dangerous items first, and the final manual check (the 0.22 µm sterilising filter) is the formal "clearance to proceed". Annex 1 requires this last security gate to be as close to the boarding gate (the point of fill) as possible: the shorter the distance, the lower the risk of re-contamination after the check.

★ Key Clause: PUPSIT (8.87), Pre-Use Post-Sterilisation Integrity Testing

PUPSIT = Pre-Use Post-Sterilisation Integrity Test

After the filter has been sterilised (usually by autoclave or gamma irradiation) and before use, an integrity test must be performed to confirm that the filter was not damaged during installation and sterilisation. In the past many manufacturers performed only post-use testing; the 2022 version of Annex 1 explicitly requires testing before use as well.

Test methods: bubble point, diffusive flow, water intrusion and pressure hold test

Conditions for waiver: only where there is sound scientific justification and a thorough risk assessment has been completed (for example, very small filtration volumes), and compensating controls must be in place.

Analogy: PUPSIT Is Like Checking the Gas Before You Leave Home

PUPSIT is like checking that the gas stove is off before leaving the house. The stove (the filter) may already have been faulty when it was installed. If you do not check beforehand and only find the problem when you come home (testing only after use), the sterility assurance for the whole batch of product may already be lost.

The old practice was to test only after use (post-use only). If the filter was already breached when that batch was filtered, the sterility assurance behind the release of the whole batch was invalid. PUPSIT confirms that the filter is intact before use.

Key Point: Post-Use Integrity Testing Is Equally Necessary

8.87 also requires the sterilising filter to undergo a post-use integrity test after filtration is complete and before the filter is removed. This is the final confirmation that the filter remained intact during filtration. Neither test can be omitted:

  • Pre-use (PUPSIT): confirms the filter was not damaged during sterilisation and installation
  • Post-use: confirms the filter remained intact throughout filtration

Key Concept: Redundant Filtration System (8.92)

Redundant filtration system: two sterilising filters installed in series, although validation requires only one of them to achieve sterility assurance. The second (back-up) filter is a safeguard.

Practical logic: if the primary filter passes the post-use integrity test, the redundant filter does not need to be tested. If the primary filter fails, the redundant filter is tested and an investigation is started at the same time. This means that, as long as the redundant filter is intact, batch release may still be possible.

Key Point: Timing of Bioburden Sampling (8.93)

Bioburden sampling must be performed "immediately prior to" the final sterilising filtration. For a redundant system, the sample is taken before the first filter.

Purpose: to confirm that the bioburden of the liquid entering the sterilising filter is within acceptable limits, as an input control for sterility assurance. High bioburden may block or damage the filter and increase the risk of filtration failure.

Key Point: Single-Use Principle for Filters (8.94)

As a rule, a sterilising liquid filter is used for a single batch only and the same filter must not be used for more than one working day, unless this has been validated.

Exception (8.95): in campaign manufacture, a longer period of use is possible after adequate assessment, documented risk evaluation and validation. The maximum validated period of use must be clearly defined and tightly controlled.

Practical Application: PUPSIT Implementation Challenges at a CDMO

In a CDMO environment, PUPSIT brings practical challenges:

  • Equipment design: the filtration system must be designed so that an integrity test can be performed after sterilisation and before filling (this usually requires additional pressure sensors and valves)
  • SOP updates: after the 2022 version of Annex 1 was published, existing SOPs must be updated and operators retrained
  • Waiver requests: for small-batch client products where PUPSIT cannot be performed, a formal documented risk assessment is needed, and the compensating controls must be recorded in the CCS

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Characteristics of BFS Technology

Blow-Fill-Seal (BFS): starting from polymer granulate, a single machine carries out extrusion → blow-moulding → filling → sealing. The process does not rely on pre-made containers, making it a highly integrated aseptic filling technology.

Two main machine types:

  • Shuttle type: the parison is open to the external environment during forming, so the critical zone must be grade A
  • Rotary type: the parison is usually in an enclosed environment after forming, but the rotating structure makes environmental monitoring of the critical filling zone more difficult

Analogy: BFS Is Like "Casting the Bottle on the Spot and Filling It Immediately"

BFS is like a mineral water factory: plastic granules go in, and the machine melts them and blows them into a bottle (blow-moulding). While the bottle neck has not yet fully cooled, water is injected immediately and the bottle is sealed straight away. The whole process takes a few seconds, and the container is never exposed to the external environment.

This is the core sterility assurance advantage of BFS: the container is filled and sealed at the very moment it is "born", leaving almost no opportunity for contamination. The trade-off is that high-temperature extrusion and cutting generate large numbers of particles, which makes in-operation total particle monitoring almost impossible. Annex 1 explicitly recognises this in 8.107.

Key Point: Exemption from Particle Monitoring for BFS (8.107)

8.107 is one of the few clauses in Annex 1 that acknowledges a monitoring limitation: because the BFS process itself generates large numbers of polymer extrusion and cutting particles, and the critical filling zone is small, in-operation total particle monitoring during filling is not expected.

This does not mean responsibility is removed entirely. The manufacturer must hold design qualification data demonstrating that the equipment design ensures the critical filling zone achieves grade A conditions during operation.

Key Point: Sterility Assurance of Polymer Granulate (8.112)

Sterility assurance for BFS does not lie only in the process; the polymer granulate on the raw material side must also be controlled. Annex 1 requires that:

  • The ability of the extrusion system to provide sterility assurance for the formed container must be understood and validated
  • The bioburden and endotoxin/pyrogen levels of the raw polymer must be defined and controlled
  • These controls must be integrated into the PQS and CCS

The high-temperature extrusion process itself provides some thermal sterilising effect, but this must be explicitly validated and cannot simply be assumed.

  Background environment: minimum grade D
  Point of fill: meets the requirements of 8.3/8.4

BFS for aseptic filling (8.106):
  Shuttle type equipment:
    Critical zone (extrusion/blow-moulding/sealing): grade A
    Background environment: minimum grade C (with A/B cleanroom garments)
  Rotary type equipment:
    Filling environment inside the parison: grade A
    Background environment: minimum grade C (with A/B cleanroom garments)

In-operation particle monitoring (8.107):
  In-operation total particle monitoring is not expected
  But design qualification data is needed to support achievement of grade A

Key Concept: Moulds Are Critical Equipment (8.120)

BFS moulds are explicitly defined as critical equipment. Any change or modification to the moulds must:

  • Trigger a reassessment of the integrity of the finished container
  • Be followed by validation if the assessment shows an impact

This is because the mould directly determines container wall thickness, volume and seal quality. Any wear or modification of the mould may change these critical parameters and in turn affect container closure integrity (CCI) and sterility.

  • The in-line 100% container closure integrity (CCI) test method and its compliance with 8.22

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Lyophilisation as an Extension of the Aseptic Process

Lyophilisation (freeze-drying): under low temperature and vacuum, water in the product is sublimated directly from solid ice to water vapour, removing moisture and extending product stability. The lyophiliser is an extension of the whole aseptic process: partially stoppered vials that have been filled remain "unsealed" and are only truly closed once lyophilisation is complete.

8.121 explicitly defines lyophilisation as an extension of aseptic processing. This means that all loading, transfer and unloading operations must maintain the same level of contamination control as aseptic filling.

Analogy: The Lyophiliser Is the "Extended Operating Theatre" of the Aseptic Process

A lyophiliser is like a "recovery room" attached to an operating theatre: after the operation (filling) is finished, the patient (the partially stoppered vials) still needs to continue treatment (drying) in another sterile space (the lyophiliser). Throughout this time the wound (the vial opening) remains exposed, and the sterile environment requirements cannot be relaxed for a moment.

This is why the lyophiliser itself needs to be sterilised regularly, and why the transfer path from the filling station to the lyophiliser must maintain grade A conditions throughout.

Deferred Implementation Notice: Clause 8.123 (Important!)

The requirement in clause 8.123 that a manually loaded lyophiliser without barrier technology must be sterilised before each loading has been deferred to 25 August 2024.

The original Annex 1 (2022) required lyophilisers that are manually loaded/unloaded without barrier separation to be sterilised before each load. For many sites that are not yet automated, this is a major operational burden, and PIC/S therefore granted a grace period.

Practical implication: manufacturers should review how their lyophilisers are currently loaded, assess whether they meet this requirement, and clearly document the sterilisation frequency decision in the CCS. If loading is still manual without barriers, compliance improvements or documentation updates should be completed before 25 August 2024.

Key Point: Lyophiliser Integrity Testing (8.124)

Integrity control of a lyophiliser has two aspects:

  • Filter integrity: filters used to maintain the integrity of the lyophiliser must be sterilised before each use, and the results of their integrity tests must form part of batch certification/release
  • Chamber vacuum/leak integrity: the frequency of chamber vacuum/leak integrity testing must be documented, and the maximum permitted leakage must be confirmed before the start of each cycle

This means that lyophiliser integrity test results are required documents for batch release, not optional monitoring.

Key Point: Loading Design and Protection of Partially Stoppered Vials (8.126)

8.126 lists the key considerations for lyophiliser loading design. The core principles are:

  • Transfer of partially stoppered vials from the filling station to the lyophiliser must take place under grade A throughout
  • Unsealed containers must be physically separated from operators by a barrier
  • All items that contact the lyophiliser (trays, fixtures, forceps, etc.) must be sterile
  • After the lyophiliser chamber is opened, if the stoppers have not yet been fully pressed in, the product must continue to be handled under grade A

If the closed system is opened (e.g. for maintenance of a bulk manufacturing line) then this should be performed in a classified area appropriate to the materials (e.g. grade C for terminal sterilisation processes, or grade A for aseptic processing) or be subject to further cleaning and disinfection (and sterilisation in case of aseptic processes).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Definition of a Closed System

Closed system: in aseptic processing, a liquid handling system that is completely isolated from the external environment, so that the product is not exposed to the surrounding environment. Typical examples: sterile transfer tubing, single-use bag systems and integrated filtration-filling lines.

Three main protective benefits of closed systems:

  • Reduced risk of microbial contamination
  • Reduced risk of particle contamination
  • Reduced risk of chemical contamination

Core design principle: minimise manual manipulations. Every human intervention increases the risk of contamination.

Analogy: A Closed System Is Like a "Closed Transfusion Line"

A closed system is like a hospital intravenous infusion line: the whole path from the blood bag to the patient's arm is closed, and the blood (the product) is never exposed to air during transfer. If another line needs to be connected midway (an aseptic connection), a special sterile connector must be used; the system must not be opened to the air.

The core requirement of 8.128 is that connections downstream of the sterilising filter must use a method that keeps the system sterile (for example a sterile weld or an aseptic connector), not an ordinary threaded fitting or a manually opened connection.

Key Point: Aseptic Connection Devices (8.128/8.129)

All connections downstream of the sterilising filter must be designed as "intrinsically sterile" connections. Common compliant solutions include:

  • Sterile weld (tube welding): tubing is joined in a closed environment using thermal welding technology, so the connection point itself is sterile
  • Aseptic connector: a dedicated connector designed so that the connection can be made in a non-sterile environment while keeping the connection point sterile
  • Rapid Transfer Port (RTP): a rapid transfer port used for aseptic transfer between closed systems

Supplier assessment must include the collection of data on potential failure modes. This is a necessary input to the CCS.

9.4 An environmental monitoring programme should be established and documented. The purpose of the environmental monitoring programme, is to:

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Dual Purpose of an Environmental Monitoring Programme

The Environmental Monitoring (EM) programme has two core missions:

  • Ongoing assurance: confirm that cleanrooms and clean air equipment remain at the cleanliness grade required by their design and by regulation.
  • Detecting deviations: effectively detect excursions in the environment and trigger an investigation and a product quality risk assessment.

Note: EM is "ongoing assurance", not a one-time validation. It is a dynamic quality system tool.

Core Concept — Alert Level vs. Action Limit

Alert Level: an early warning signal. Exceeding it indicates that the environment may be deteriorating and requires evaluation and preventive measures, but does not necessarily require an immediate production stop. Alert levels should be set from cleanroom qualification data and reviewed periodically against trend data.

Action Limit: the maximum acceptable upper limit, with maximum values given in Table 5 (non-viable particles) and Table 6 (viable microorganisms). Exceeding an action limit must trigger a root cause investigation, an assessment of product impact, and corrective and preventive actions (CAPA).

Key difference: alert levels focus on trend management; action limits focus on urgent response.

Analogy — Infection Control in a Hospital

Imagine a hospital's infection control programme (analogous to the Contamination Control Strategy, CCS): the alert level is like a "yellow warning" — a few patients on the ward have colds, so the hospital increases hand washing and disinfection frequency; the action limit is like a "red warning" — a cluster of hospital-acquired infections appears, so isolation procedures start immediately, non-essential surgeries stop, and the source of infection is investigated.

The EM programme is the hospital's infection surveillance dashboard, letting managers intervene early, before a problem escalates.

Key Point — Risk-Assessment-Driven Sampling Design

PIC/S Annex 1 explicitly requires that the sampling locations, frequency, methods and incubation conditions of the EM programme be derived from a risk assessment, not from subjective decisions. The risk assessment should cover:

  • Knowledge of the process and product (process inputs, final product)
  • Layout and design of the facility and equipment
  • Criticality of specific steps
  • Historical data from routine monitoring and qualification
  • Typical microbial flora known to be present in the environment

A common inspector question: "How did you decide on this sampling point?" The answer must be supported by a documented risk assessment.

Key Point — The Four Trend Indicators

Clause 9.11 lists four trend patterns that require attention:

  • Increasing numbers of excursions: absolute values may still be within limits, but the frequency is rising.
  • Consecutive excursions above the alert level: indicates a systemic problem, not a sporadic event.
  • Periodic excursions with a common cause: for example, excursions that always appear after preventive maintenance, showing that the maintenance activity itself needs improvement.
  • A change in the microbial species: the appearance of spore-forming microorganisms or mould is especially dangerous, because these organisms are highly resistant to common disinfectants.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Four Methods of Viable Monitoring

Viable Particle Monitoring has four main sampling tools, each suited to different situations:

  • Active Air Sampling: an impaction sampler actively draws a known volume of air, with results expressed in CFU/m³. Advantage: accurate quantification; disadvantage: the equipment itself may disturb airflow.
  • Settle Plate: a passively exposed culture dish that lets airborne microorganisms settle naturally. In Grade A/B it should be exposed for the whole operation (up to 4 hours); the result reflects overall contamination during the exposure period.
  • Contact Plate: used to collect microorganisms from surfaces; suitable for equipment, walls, and personnel gloves/gowns.
  • Swab: suitable for irregular surfaces such as valves and seals.

In practice, Grade A/B usually combines several of these methods to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Core Concept — Key Points for Reading Table 6

The most critical rule in Table 6 is that any growth in Grade A requires investigation. This means the microbial action limit for Grade A is effectively "zero tolerance", unlike the numerical limits one normally expects.

Other key points:

  • Glove prints in Grade A/B are <1 and 5 CFU/glove respectively (including all 10 fingers of both hands).
  • Grade C/D has no glove monitoring requirement (shown as "—").
  • CFU = Colony Forming Unit. If a rapid microbiological method (RMM) is used, its correlation with CFU must be scientifically validated.

Analogy — The Complementary Roles of Settle Plates and Active Sampling

Active air sampling is like "measuring the dust in a room with a vacuum cleaner" — active, quantitative and immediate. A settle plate is like "putting a sheet of white paper on a table and seeing how much dust lands on it after a few hours" — passive, but it reflects the cumulative contamination risk over the whole operation.

Each has strengths and weaknesses: active sampling is highly sensitive, but the equipment may affect the Grade A unidirectional airflow; settle plates do not disturb airflow, but media performance can be affected by temperature and humidity. This is why Annex 1 requires the two to be used in combination.

Key Point — Enhanced Personnel Monitoring Requirements (9.25–9.26)

Personnel are one of the biggest sources of contamination in a sterile environment. Annex 1 requires:

  • After a critical intervention: monitor at least the outer gloves, and the relevant gown sites where necessary.
  • Each time personnel leave the Grade B cleanroom: monitor gloves and gown.
  • If a glove result exceeds the limit: change the outer gloves immediately before continuing work.
  • If a gown result exceeds the limit: change the gown before continuing work.

Manual operations (manual aseptic compounding/filling) carry higher risk than automated operations, so the CCS should place particular emphasis on gown monitoring for personnel.

Core Concept — Rapid Microbiological Methods

Clause 9.28 encourages manufacturers to evaluate Rapid Microbial Methods (RMM), for example:

  • ATP bioluminescence
  • Flow cytometry
  • Automated incubation and imaging systems

Precondition for adoption: the method must be validated to show that it is equivalent or superior to the traditional culture method. From a regulatory standpoint, RMM must not "cut corners" — it may replace the traditional method only when it can be scientifically demonstrated to be no worse.

All microorganisms detected in Grade A/B must be identified to species level, and the potential impact on manufactured batches must be assessed. This is not only a technical requirement but also an important basis for batch release decisions.

9.32 Periodic verification of the effectiveness of the controls in place for aseptic processing should include an APS using a sterile nutrient media and/or surrogate in place of the product. The APS should not be considered as the primary means to validate the aseptic process or aspects of the aseptic process. The effectiveness of the aseptic process should be determined through process design, adherence to the pharmaceutical quality system and process controls, training, and evaluation of monitoring data.

Selection of an appropriate nutrient media and/or surrogate should be made based on the ability of the media and/or surrogate to imitate physical product characteristics assessed to pose a risk to product sterility during the aseptic process. Where processing stages may indirectly impact the viability of any introduced microbial contamination, (e.g. aseptically produced semi-solids, powders, solid materials, microspheres, liposomes and other formulations where product is cooled or heated or lyophilized), alternative procedures that represent the operations as closely as possible should be developed. Where surrogate materials, such as buffers, are used in parts of the APS, the surrogate material should not inhibit the growth of any potential contamination.

9.33 The APS should imitate as closely as possible the routine aseptic manufacturing process and include all the critical manufacturing steps, specifically:

  1. The APS should assess all aseptic operations performed subsequent to the sterilisation and decontamination cycles of materials utilised in the process to the point where the container is sealed.
  2. For non-filterable formulations, any additional aseptic steps should be assessed.
  3. Where aseptic manufacturing is performed under an inert atmosphere, the inert gas should be substituted with air in the process simulation unless anaerobic simulation is intended.
  4. Processes requiring the addition of sterile powders should use an acceptable surrogate material in the same containers as those used in the process under evaluation.
  5. Separate simulations of individual unit operations (e.g. processes involving drying, blending, milling and subdivision of a sterile powder) should be avoided. Any use of individual simulations should be supported by a documented justification and ensure that the sum total of the individual simulations continues to fully cover the whole process.

For instance, boiling-over or actual freezing of the solution should be avoided. 9.36 In developing the APS plan, consideration should be given to the following:

  1. Identification of worst case conditions covering the relevant variables, such as container size and line speed, and their impact on the process.
  2. Determining the representative sizes of container/closure combinations to be used for validation. Bracketing or matrix approach may be considered for validation of the same container/closure configuration for different products where process equivalence is scientifically justified.
  3. Maximum permitted holding times for sterile product and equipment exposed during the aseptic process.
  4. The volume filled per container, which should be sufficient to ensure that the media contacts all equipment and component surfaces that may directly contaminate the sterile product. The volume used should provide sufficient headspace to support potential microbial growth and ensure that turbidity can be detected during inspection.
  5. The requirement for substitution of any inert gas used in the routine aseptic manufacturing process by air unless anaerobic simulation is intended.
  6. The selected nutrient media should be capable of growing a designated group of reference microorganisms as described by the relevant pharmacopeia and suitably representative local isolates.
  7. The method of detection of microbial contamination should be scientifically justified to ensure that contamination is reliably detected.
  8. The process simulation should be of sufficient duration to challenge the process, the operators that perform interventions, shift changes and the capability of the processing environment.
  9. Where the manufacturer operates different or extended shifts, the APS should be designed to capture factors specific to those shifts that are assessed to pose a risk to product sterility, for example the maximum duration for which an operator may be present in the cleanroom.
  10. Simulating normal aseptic manufacturing interruptions where the process is idle (e.g. shift changeovers, recharging dispensing vessels, introduction of additional equipment).
  11. Ensuring that environmental monitoring is conducted as required for routine production, and throughout the entire duration of the process simulation.
  12. Where campaign manufacturing occurs, such as in the use of Barrier Technologies or manufacture of sterile active substances, consideration should be given to designing and performing the process simulation so that it simulates the risks associated with both the beginning and the end of the campaign.
  13. The performance of "end of production or campaign APS" may be used as additional assurance or investigative purposes; however, their use should be justified in the CCS and should not replace routine APS.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Role and Limits of APS

Aseptic Process Simulation (APS), also called Media Fill, uses a sterile culture medium (usually TSB, tryptic soy broth) in place of the real drug product for a simulated fill, to verify the effectiveness of aseptic operations.

Key understanding: APS is not the "primary means of validating" an aseptic process, but a tool for periodic verification. The foundation of sterility assurance is: process design + quality system + process controls + training + monitoring data. APS is only one part, but it is the most convincing integrated assessment tool.

Analogy — APS Is a "Whole-System Stress Test"

APS is like a bank's disaster recovery drill: everything looks normal day to day, but only by simulating real scenarios (including various unexpected events) can you truly confirm that the emergency procedures work.

In a media fill, "contamination" is equivalent to a gap found in the bank's drill — not a bad thing, but an opportunity for improvement. The question is: is your drill realistic enough and demanding enough?

Key Point — Critical Requirements for Media Selection

The selection of the medium or surrogate should consider:

  • Simulating the characteristics of the real product: for example viscosity and pH, which may affect the survival of potential contaminating microorganisms.
  • Not inhibiting microbial growth: the purpose of a media fill is to "let contamination show itself". If the medium itself is bacteriostatic (such as some buffers), it will mask real contamination.
  • Special dosage forms: lyophilised products, semi-solids, microspheres and similar forms require specially designed surrogate procedures, because the lyophilisation process (sublimation, vacuum break) may affect microbial viability.
  • A lyophilisation simulation cannot actually lyophilise (to avoid the medium freezing or boiling), but must simulate worst cases such as the longest loading time and chamber opening time.

Core Concept — Simulating Interventions

APS must include all types of interventions that occur in routine production:

  • Inherent Interventions: operations unavoidable in the process itself, such as changing needles or replenishing components.
  • Corrective Interventions: manual operations required by equipment malfunctions, rejected containers and similar events.

Key principle: clause 9.35 states clearly that "APS should not be used to justify practices that introduce unnecessary contamination risks" — if an operation is performed with special care during APS but not in daily production, it goes against the spirit of APS.

Formulas and Calculations — Worst-Case Matrix for APS Design

Worst-case design considerations for APS:

Container size: largest + smallest (bracketing approach)
Filling speed: slowest line speed (longest exposure time)
Number of operators: maximum
Shift change: include handover procedures
Process interruptions: simulate changing containers/reservoirs, adding materials, equipment failures
Fill volume: sufficient to cover all contact surfaces + leave enough room for incubation
Medium concentration: meets USP/EP/JP requirements
Environmental monitoring: same frequency as routine production

Special requirements for lyophilisation APS:
  Longest interval from lyophilizer sterilisation to use
  Longest interval from filtration to filling
  Maximum number of trays + longest chamber opening time

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, the same filling line may take on different products from several clients (such as liquid vials, lyophilised products and powder injectables). How can an effective APS programme be designed?

Recommended strategy:

  • Use a bracketing/matrix approach, selecting representative container formats, rather than running an independent APS for every format.
  • For lyophilised products, establish a separate lyophilisation APS procedure covering the entire chain from filling to opening the chamber door.
  • Ensure that every line, every working shift and every operator is covered by the APS programme.
  • After each change to the line configuration (such as changing container format), assess whether the APS needs to be repeated.

  2. In an APS, an operator performed an intervention that does not exist in routine production. Is the result of such an APS valid?

9.37–9.49 APS Execution, Acceptance Criteria and Failure Investigation

Original Text

9.37 For sterile active substances, batch size should be large enough to represent routine operation, simulate intervention operation at the worst case, and cover all surfaces that may come into contact with the sterile product. In addition, all the simulated materials (surrogates or growth medium) should be subjected to microbial evaluation. The simulation materials should be sufficient to satisfy the evaluation of the process being simulated and should not compromise the recovery of micro-organisms.

9.38 APS should be performed as part of the initial validation, with at least three consecutive satisfactory simulation tests that cover all working shifts that the aseptic process may occur in, and after any significant modification to operational practices, facilities, services or equipment which are assessed to have an impact on the sterility assurance of the product (e.g. modification to the HVAC system, equipment, changes to process, number of shifts and numbers of personnel, major facility shut down).

Normally, APS (periodic revalidation) should be repeated twice a year (approximately every six months) for each aseptic process, each filling line and each shift. Each operator should participate in at least one successful APS annually. Consideration should be given to performing an APS after the last batch prior to shut down, before long periods of inactivity or before decommissioning or relocation of a line.

9.39 Where manual operation (e.g. aseptic compounding or filling) occurs, each type of container, container closure and equipment train should be initially validated with each operator participating in at least 3 consecutive successful APS and revalidated with one APS approximately every 6 months for each operator. The APS batch size should mimic that used in the routine aseptic manufacturing process.

9.40 The number of units processed (filled) for APS should be sufficient to effectively simulate all activities that are representative of the aseptic manufacturing process. Justification for the number of units to be filled should be clearly captured in the CCS. Typically, a minimum of 5,000 to 10,000 units are filled. For small batches (e.g. those under 5,000 units), the number of containers for APS should at least equal the size of the production batch.

9.41 Filled APS units should be agitated, swirled or inverted before incubation to ensure contact of the media with all interior surfaces in the container. All integral units from the APS should be incubated and evaluated, including units with cosmetic defects or those which have gone through non-destructive in-process control checks. If units are discarded during the process simulation and not incubated, these should be comparable with units discarded during a routine fill, and only if production SOPs clearly specify that units must be removed under the same circumstances. In no case should more units be removed during a media fill intervention than would be cleared during a production run.

9.42 Where processes include materials that contact the product contact surfaces but are then discarded (e.g. product flushes), the discarded material should be simulated with nutrient media and be incubated as part of the APS, unless it can be clearly demonstrated that this waste process would not impact the sterility of the product.

9.43 Filled APS units should be incubated in a clear container to ensure visual detection of microbial growth. Where the product container is not clear (e.g. amber glass, opaque plastic), clear containers of identical configuration may be substituted to aid in the detection of contamination. When a clear container of identical configuration cannot be substituted, a suitable method for the detection of microbial growth should be developed and validated. Microorganisms isolated from contaminated units should be identified to the species level when practical, to assist in the determination of the likely source of the contaminant.

9.44 Filled APS units should be incubated without unnecessary delay to achieve the best possible recovery of potential contamination. The selection of the incubation conditions and duration should be scientifically justified and validated to provide an appropriate level of sensitivity of detection of microbial contamination.

9.45 On completion of incubation:

  1. Filled APS units should be inspected by personnel who have been appropriately trained and qualified for the detection of microbiological contamination.
  2. Samples of the filled units should undergo positive control by inoculation with a suitable range of reference organisms and suitably representative local isolates.

9.46 The target should be zero growth. Any contaminated unit should result in a failed APS and the following actions should be taken:

  1. an investigation to determine the most probable root cause(s);
  2. determination and implementation of appropriate corrective measures;
  3. a sufficient number of successful, consecutive repeat APS (normally a minimum of 3) should be conducted in order to demonstrate that the process has been returned to a state of control;
  4. a prompt review of all appropriate records relating to aseptic production since the last successful APS;
    • The outcome of the review should include a risk assessment of potential sterile breaches in batches manufactured since the last successful APS.
    • All other batches not released to the market should be included in the scope of the investigation. Any decision regarding their release status should consider the investigation outcome.
  5. all products that have been manufactured on a line subsequent to a process simulation failure should be quarantined until a successful resolution of the process simulation failure has occurred;
  6. where the root cause investigation indicates that the failure was related to operator activity, actions to limit the operator's activities, until retrained and requalified, should be taken;
  7. production should resume only after completion of successful revalidation.

9.47 All APS runs should be fully documented and include a reconciliation of units processed (e.g. units filled, incubated and not incubated). Justification for filled and non-incubated units should be included in the documentation. All interventions performed during the APS should be recorded, including the start and end time of each intervention and the involved person. All microbial monitoring data as well as other testing data should be recorded in the APS batch record.

9.48 An APS run should be aborted only under circumstances in which written procedures require commercial lots to be equally handled. An investigation should be documented in such cases.

9.49 An aseptic process should be subject to a repeat of the initial validation when:

  1. the specific aseptic process has not been in operation for an extended period of time; or
  2. there is a change to the process, equipment, procedures or environment that has the potential to affect the aseptic process or an addition of new product containers or container-closure combinations.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — APS Frequency and Personnel Qualification

PIC/S Annex 1 sets clear and strict requirements for APS frequency:

  • Initial validation: at least 3 consecutive satisfactory runs, covering all working shifts.
  • Periodic revalidation: for each process, each filling line and each shift, once every six months (approximately).
  • Operators: each operator must participate in at least one successful APS per year (automated operations); manual operations require one every 6 months per operator.
  • Initial validation for manual operations: each operator must complete at least 3 consecutive successful APS runs.

Linking APS frequency to personnel management reflects the core understanding that "personnel are the biggest contamination risk".

Formulas and Calculations — APS Fill Quantity Rules

Standard fill quantity:
  Minimum 5,000 to 10,000 units

Small batch rule:
  Batch size < 5,000 units → APS quantity = production batch size

Acceptance criteria:
  Target = zero contamination (zero growth)
  Any contamination = failed APS

Revalidation requirement after a failure:
  At least 3 consecutive successful APS runs before production can resume

Important calculation:
  The APS fill volume must be sufficient to:
  1. Contact all inner container surfaces
  2. Retain enough headspace to support microbial growth
  3. Ensure turbidity is visible during visual inspection

Key Point — The Chain Effect of an APS Failure (9.46)

An APS failure is one of the most serious quality events for a pharmaceutical company. Its chain effects include:

  • Immediate production stop: production may not resume until the problem is resolved and revalidation is complete.
  • Batch quarantine: all batches produced on that line since the last successful APS must be quarantined.
  • Retrospective investigation: all aseptic production records since the last successful APS must be reviewed, with a sterility risk assessment.
  • Unreleased batches: these must be included in the scope of the investigation, and release decisions must be based on the investigation results.
  • Operator-related: if the root cause points to a specific operator, that person's activities must be restricted until retraining and requalification are complete.

Business impact: a single APS failure can cause weeks of lost production and the potential scrapping of many filled batches — the strongest motivation for rigorous APS programme design.

Analogy — The "Butterfly Effect" of an APS Failure

Imagine a restaurant health inspection that finds bacterial contamination in the kitchen. Not only must all of that day's food be pulled, but all food sold since the last successful hygiene inspection must be traced and assessed. The restaurant must close until it passes three consecutive inspections.

The "butterfly effect" of an APS failure is exactly this: the problem does not affect just one batch, but every batch that "may have been contaminated" must be reviewed. This is why preventive APS programme design matters far more than after-the-fact remediation.

Core Concept — Incubation Conditions and Positive Controls

There are several key points about APS incubation:

  • No unnecessary delay: incubation should begin as soon as possible after filling to ensure optimal microbial recovery.
  • Incubation conditions must be validated: temperature and time must be supported by scientific rationale to ensure sensitivity to the expected contaminating microorganisms. This usually includes two stages, 20–25°C and 30–35°C, to cover different types of microorganisms (mould/yeast vs. bacteria).
  • Positive Control: inoculate some of the filled units with known reference strains (including local environmental isolates) to confirm the growth-promoting capability of the medium. If the positive controls fail to grow, the data for the entire APS are invalid.
  • Transparent containers: use clear containers so that turbidity after incubation can be detected visually. Amber vials require clear substitutes of the same construction, or other detection methods must be developed.

Key Point — Strict Control of Non-incubated Units (9.41)

The number of filled units that are "not incubated" in an APS must match the number rejected in routine production in the same situations. The pass rate must not be raised artificially by removing "suspect" units. This is a frequent finding in FDA and EMA inspections:

  • Units discarded in an APS must not exceed the number discarded in routine production.
  • Discards must have a clear SOP basis (such as removing a fixed number of units after an intervention).
  • Discarded units associated with aseptic assembly are usually incubated separately and not counted toward the formal acceptance criteria.

Practical Application — CDMO Business Scenario

A CDMO accepts a fill contract from a new client for a 2 mL prefilled syringe (PFS) product, with a planned batch size of 4,000 units.

According to Annex 1, how should the initial APS programme be designed?

  • Fill quantity: the small batch rule applies (<5,000 units) → the APS fill quantity should be no less than 4,000 units.
  • Number of runs: at least 3 consecutive successful APS runs, covering all working shifts.
  • Operators: all operators who may take part in filling this product must participate.
  • Surrogate containers: if the PFS barrel is clear glass, it can be used directly; if it has a light-blocking coating, a clear substitute or another validated detection method is required.
  • Medium: select a TSB that simulates the viscosity characteristics of a 2 mL aqueous injectable, and confirm that the fill volume ensures the medium contacts all contact surfaces.

  • Environmental monitoring programme (9.4–9.13): must be established on the basis of a risk assessment, covering sampling locations, frequency, methods and incubation conditions; alert levels are early warnings and action limits are the maximum acceptable limits; trend analysis is the core of an EM programme, and trend monitoring procedures must be defined and documented.
  • Non-viable particle monitoring and Table 5 (9.14–9.21): Grade A requires continuous monitoring (≥28 L/min), with alarms triggered when the alert level is exceeded; Grade A at-rest and in-operation particle limits are the same (3,520/m³, 29/m³); the Grade D in-operation limit must be set by the manufacturer based on a risk assessment.

  • APS execution and failure (9.37–9.49): the target is zero growth; any contamination is a failed APS, requiring a full investigation, quarantine of the related batches, and at least 3 consecutive successful APS runs before production can resume; once every six months per line and per shift; each operator must complete at least one successful APS per year.
  • Decision priority: Sterility Assurance is always the highest priority, and all decisions on EM programme design and APS must be guided by it.

10.1 – 10.2 Personnel and Specification Requirements

Original Text

10.1 There should be personnel available with appropriate training and experience in microbiology, sterility assurance and knowledge of the processes to support the design of the manufacturing activities, environmental monitoring regime and any investigation assessing the impact of microbiologically linked events to the safety of the sterile product.

10.2 Specifications for raw materials, components and products should include requirements for microbial, particulate and endotoxin/pyrogen limits when the need for this has been indicated by monitoring and/or by the CCS.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The dual role of the QC department in sterile manufacturing: Section 10 of PIC/S Annex 1 opens by requiring that QC personnel have core competence in three areas: microbiology, sterility assurance and process knowledge. This is not just about accepting or rejecting test results; it is an active role in designing (the EM programme) and investigating (root cause analysis of microbiological events).

The Contamination Control Strategy (CCS) is a core new concept in the 2022 version of Annex 1. Section 2 defines the CCS, and Section 10.2 stresses that whether microbial, particulate or endotoxin limits need to be set in material specifications must be driven by the CCS, not by traditional habit.

Analogy

Think of a hospital infection control team. They do not step in only after a patient is infected; they proactively design isolation arrangements, set cleaning frequencies and review environmental data. This is exactly the role of the QC microbiologist in a sterile facility. The CCS is the complete "Hospital Infection Control Plan", and specification limits are its clear action trigger points.

Key Notes

The key phrase in 10.2 is "when the need has been indicated by monitoring and/or CCS". It means that not every material needs a microbial limit, but once the CCS assessment concludes one is needed, it must be included in the specification. This reflects a risk-based approach, not a one-size-fits-all rule.

10.3 – 10.4 Bioburden Testing Requirements

Original Text

10.3 The bioburden assay should be performed on each batch for both aseptically filled product and terminally sterilised products and the results considered as part of the final batch review. There should be defined limits for bioburden immediately before the final sterilising grade filter or the terminal sterilisation process, which are related to the efficiency of the method to be used. Samples should be taken to be representative of the worst case scenario (e.g. at the end of hold time). Where overkill sterilisation parameters are set for terminally sterilised products, bioburden should be monitored at suitable scheduled intervals.

10.4 For products authorised for parametric release, a supporting pre-sterilisation bioburden monitoring programme for the filled product prior to initiating the sterilisation cycle should be developed and the bioburden assay should be performed for each batch. The sampling locations of filled units before sterilisation should be based on a worst case scenario and be representative of the batch. Any organisms found during bioburden testing should be identified and their impact on the effectiveness of the sterilising process determined. Where appropriate, the level of endotoxin/pyrogen should be monitored.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Bioburden is the total amount of microorganisms present on a given item (for example, a filled product) before sterilisation. Bioburden is not a contamination indicator but a critical process input parameter: sterilisation efficiency is calculated on the basis of the initial bioburden.

Why test every batch? Section 10.3 requires bioburden testing for both aseptically filled and terminally sterilised products on every batch. This differs from the logic of an overkill process: an overkill process may reduce the frequency (periodic testing), but a standard (non-overkill) process must be monitored every batch.

Parametric Release means using process parameters (temperature, time, pressure, etc.) instead of the sterility test as the basis for release. It is permitted only when the process has been thoroughly validated and bioburden is controlled for every batch. This represents the highest level of process understanding and trust.

Formula and Concept: Relationship between Bioburden and SAL

Target SAL (Sterility Assurance Level) = 10⁻⁶

SAL = Initial Bioburden × e^(-D × t/D-value)

A more intuitive way to look at it:
  If initial bioburden = 1 CFU/unit
  → a 6-log reduction is needed to reach SAL 10⁻⁶

  If initial bioburden = 100 CFU/unit
  → an 8-log reduction is needed to reach SAL 10⁻⁶

Conclusion: the higher the bioburden, the greater the
sterilisation intensity needed to reach the same SAL,
or the lower the confidence.

Key Notes

Sampling must represent the worst case: Section 10.3 stresses that samples must represent the worst case, for example samples taken at the end of the hold time. Microorganisms may continue to multiply during the waiting period, so the end of the hold is the point at which bioburden in the batch is highest.

Additional obligations for parametric release (10.4): Isolates found must be identified and their impact on sterilisation efficiency assessed. The presence of heat-resistant organisms (such as Bacillus spores), even in small numbers, may compromise the validity of the SAL calculation.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: the client's product uses steam sterilisation at 121°C for 15 minutes (non-overkill). During validation you need to establish a bioburden limit (typically ≤ 10 CFU/100 mL), and confirm it by sampling after each batch is filled and before sterilisation starts. If a batch exceeds the bioburden limit, it cannot simply proceed to sterilisation and release; you must investigate the cause and assess the risk, and the batch may need to be rejected. This is how bioburden data enters the batch release decision.

10.5 – 10.8 The Correct Role of Sterility Testing

Original Text

CRITICAL CONCEPTUAL POINT — Read Carefully

10.5 The sterility test applied to the finished product should only be regarded as the last in a series of critical control measures by which sterility is assured. It cannot be used to assure sterility of a product that does not meet its design, procedural or validation parameters. The test should be validated for the product concerned.

10.6 The sterility test should be performed under aseptic conditions. Samples taken for sterility testing should be representative of the whole of the batch but should in particular include samples taken from parts of the batch considered to be most at risk of contamination, for example:

  • i. For products which have been filled aseptically, samples should include containers filled at the beginning and end of the batch. Additional samples, e.g. taken after critical interventions should be considered based on risk.
  • ii. For products which have been heat sterilised in their final containers, samples taken should be representative of the worst case locations (e.g. the potentially coolest or slowest to heat part of each load).
  • iii. For products which have been lyophilized, samples taken from different lyophilization loads.
Note: Where the manufacturing process results in sub-batches (e.g. for terminally sterilised products) then sterility samples from each sub-batch should be taken and a sterility test for each sub-batch performed. Consideration should also be given to performing separate testing for other finished product tests.

10.7 For some products it may not be possible to obtain a sterility test result prior to release because the shelf life of the product is too short to allow completion of a sterility test. In these cases, the additional considerations of design of the process and additional monitoring and/or alternative test methods required to mitigate the identified risks should be assessed and documented.

10.8 Any process (e.g. Vaporized Hydrogen Peroxide, Ultra Violet) used to decontaminate the external surfaces of sterility samples prior to testing should not negatively impact the sensitivity of the test method or the reliability of the sample.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes: The Most Important Shift in Thinking

Sterility testing cannot guarantee sterility! This is the point many practitioners misunderstand most easily. The logic of 10.5 is as follows:

  • In a batch of 10,000 units, 20 are randomly sampled for sterility testing
  • Even if all 20 pass, 9,980 units remain untested
  • Statistically, if the contamination rate is 0.1%, these 20 samples still have about a 98% probability of all passing

Sterility testing is therefore a "confirmatory" final step, not the primary "protective" line of defence. True sterility assurance comes from process design, validation and ongoing control.

Analogy

Sterility testing is like tasting a spoonful of soup just before the restaurant serves it. It can tell you whether that spoonful is salty, but it cannot guarantee that the whole pot is fine. What really protects food safety is the chef's training, the hygiene of the kitchen, the freshness of the ingredients and a standardised cooking process. Tasting is only a final confirmation, not the source of quality.

Key Concepts: Sampling Strategy

Sampling design principles in 10.6: samples must represent the "highest-risk time points and locations":

  • Aseptic filling: the start of the batch (equipment just exposed) and the end (after prolonged exposure) are the highest-risk points; additional samples should also be taken after critical interventions.
  • Heat sterilisation: the cold spot in the autoclave or the slowest-heating location. These points have the lowest SAL and represent the worst case for the batch.
  • Freeze-drying: samples from different freeze-drying batches, because heat distribution may differ between loads.

Alternative Strategies for Short Shelf-Life Products (10.7)

Standard sterility test: takes 14 days (membrane filtration method)
Problem: if the product shelf life is only 24-48 hours (e.g. radiopharmaceuticals)
→ the product expires before the sterility test is complete!

Alternatives (require a documented assessment):
  1. Enhanced process validation (more frequent APS)
  2. Increased real-time environmental monitoring
  3. Rapid Microbial Methods
     e.g. fluorescence techniques, ATP bioluminescence (results in 2-4 hours)
  4. Parametric release (where applicable)

Key Notes: VHP Decontamination Must Not Compromise Test Sensitivity (10.8)

When Vaporized Hydrogen Peroxide (VHP) or ultraviolet light is used to decontaminate the outer surface of samples, it must be verified that these treatments do not penetrate into the container or leave residues that affect the culture medium. If the decontamination step kills microorganisms that are actually present, the sterility test becomes a source of "false negatives". This is more dangerous than having no test at all, because it creates a false sense of security.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Local isolates: growth promotion testing of EM culture media should use not only the pharmacopoeial standard strains but also strains isolated from your own facility environment. Organisms specific to a facility may have particular culture requirements, and if the medium is not sensitive to their growth, the false-negative rate of EM will increase.

Environmental monitoring data as part of batch release (10.10): this is a strengthened requirement in the 2022 version of Annex 1. EM data is not only a tool for environmental management but also an input to the batch release decision. When signing off batch release, the QP (Qualified Person) must have reviewed the relevant EM trend data.

Key Notes: Out of Trend (OOT) vs. Out of Specification (OOS)

OOT (out of trend): data does not exceed the action limit but shows an upward trend, which is an early warning signal. The alert level is used to trigger an OOT investigation. OOS (out of specification): the action limit is exceeded, requiring immediate investigation and corrective action (CAPA). Section 10.10 requires written procedures defining the follow-up actions for both situations.

Validation requirements for Rapid Microbial Methods (RMM) (10.11): the method must be validated for your specific product or process and cannot rely solely on the vendor's generic validation data. Validation should include sensitivity, specificity and an assessment of matrix effects.

Analogy

Growth promotion testing of culture media is like a chef checking that the stove lights properly before service. If the stove cannot light at all, the results of every "freshness test" on the ingredients are meaningless. Adding local isolates is like testing with ingredients commonly found in your own kitchen rather than exotic ingredients you have never seen.

Practical Application

Integration into the CDMO batch release process: when designing the Batch Certification SOP, you must clearly define which EM data must be complete and within specification before release is allowed. This usually includes: (1) the continuous Grade A particle monitoring trend during filling; (2) the Grade B/C viable sampling results for the week of filling; (3) the personnel monitoring results around the filling date. If the data show an OOT, the batch does not necessarily have to be rejected, but there must be a written assessment explaining why the product risk is acceptable.

Practice Questions

  1. Your facility has introduced ATP bioluminescence as a rapid method for EM. According to 10.11, what validation work do you need to do before it can be used for batch release decisions?
  2. After a batch is manufactured, there is one OOT environmental monitoring result (out of trend but not exceeding the alert level) in a Grade C area. Can the QP release the batch? What documentation is needed to support this?

Section Summary

  • The fundamental limitation of sterility testing: sterility testing is the "last step" in a series of control measures and statistically cannot guarantee that a whole batch is sterile; true sterility assurance comes from process design, validation and ongoing control
  • Bioburden is a process parameter, not just a test: it must be performed for every batch and the results must be included in batch release review; the bioburden limit is determined by the efficiency of the sterilisation method and reflects the validity of the SAL calculation
  • Prerequisites for parametric release: a complete pre-sterilisation bioburden monitoring programme must be in place, and isolates must be identified and assessed for their impact on sterilisation
  • EM data is an input to batch release: the QP must review EM trend data before release; both OOT and OOS must have written follow-up action procedures
  • Media quality testing must include local isolates: to ensure that EM is sensitive to organisms specific to the facility; supplier testing cannot directly replace end user testing
  • Rapid microbial methods must be validated for the product: generic validation is not sufficient, matrix effects must be assessed, and this is of particular importance for short shelf-life products

Glossary: Key Terms in Sterile Manufacturing
PIC/S PE 009-16 Annex 1 (2022) — Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products | p52 - p58

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the precise definitions of all official terms in PIC/S Annex 1 (2022), so that concepts are not mixed up in regulatory documents or audits
  • Understand the specific role of each key term (Grade A, CCS, RABS, Isolator, APS, PUPSIT, Bioburden, etc.) within the sterility assurance system
  • Distinguish between easily confused pairs of terms: Action limit vs Alert level, Classified area vs Cleanroom, Corrective intervention vs Inherent intervention
  • Build systematic links between terms, and understand how the glossary underpins the logical structure of the Annex 1 sections

How to Use This Glossary

In each entry, the left column gives the original English definition (reproduced verbatim), and the right column gives the Chinese term, an explanation of the entry and teaching commentary. Terms marked CRITICAL are core concepts of the 2022 version of Annex 1 and are the ones most often asked about in assessments or audits. Terms marked 2022 NEW were loosely defined in the previous version and were significantly clarified in the 2022 version.

A
Airlock
An enclosed space with interlocked doors, constructed to maintain air pressure control between adjoining rooms (generally with different air cleanliness standards). The intent of an airlock is to preclude ingress of particle matter and microorganism contamination from a lesser controlled area.
Airlock
A transition space between rooms of different cleanliness grades, fitted with interlocked doors so that both doors cannot be open at the same time, in order to maintain pressure differential control.
Key design principle: the pressure differential must run from the higher cleanliness grade to the lower one (positive pressure protection), so that contaminants cannot "flow back" into the higher-grade area. Airlocks are a fundamental element of the physical barriers in the CCS.
Action limit CRITICAL
An established relevant measure (e.g. microbial, or airborne particle limits) that, when exceeded, should trigger appropriate investigation and corrective action based on the investigation.
Action Limit
An absolute limit which, once exceeded, requires an investigation and corrective action (CAPA). It corresponds to the microbial and particle count limits in the Annex 1 tables.
Relationship with Alert Level: an Alert Level is an early warning (a trend has been exceeded but not the limit), whereas an Action Limit is the mandatory trigger for action. An Action Limit excursion must be investigated before batch release, otherwise the QP may not certify the batch.
Alert level
An established relevant measure (e.g. microbial, or airborne particle levels) giving early warning of potential drift from normal operating conditions and validated state, which does not necessarily give grounds for corrective action but triggers appropriate scrutiny and follow-up to address the potential problem. Alert levels are established based on routine and qualification trend data and are periodically reviewed. The alert level can be based on a number of parameters including adverse trends, individual excursions above a set limit and repeat events.
Alert Level
An early warning signal that the process may be drifting from its normal state but has not yet reached the point requiring mandatory corrective action. It calls for investigation and review, but does not necessarily lead to batch rejection.
The Alert Level is dynamic (reviewed periodically based on trend data) and is not a static specification. In a well-run facility, Alert Levels are usually well below Action Limits. If Alert Levels are triggered frequently, the process is drifting and you must intervene proactively rather than wait until the Action Limit is exceeded.
Aseptic preparation/processing
The handling of sterile product, containers and/or devices in a controlled environment in which the air supply, materials and personnel are regulated to prevent microbial, endotoxin/pyrogen and particle contamination.
Aseptic Processing
The handling of sterilised product, containers or equipment in a controlled environment, with control of air, materials and personnel to prevent contamination by microorganisms, endotoxins and particles.
The essential difference between aseptic processing and terminal sterilisation: aseptic processing means "maintaining sterility throughout the process", whereas terminal sterilisation means "sterilising after manufacture". When a product cannot withstand terminal sterilisation conditions (for example heat-sensitive biologics), aseptic processing is the only option, and it is the higher-risk path with higher regulatory expectations.
Aseptic Process Simulation (APS) CRITICAL
A simulation of the entire aseptic manufacturing process in order to verify the capability of the process to assure product sterility. Includes all aseptic operations associated with routine manufacturing, e.g. equipment assembly, formulation, filling, lyophilization and sealing processes as necessary.
Aseptic Process Simulation (APS) / Media Fill
Simulating the entire aseptic manufacturing process, filling with culture medium in place of product, to demonstrate that the process can maintain sterility. Commonly known as "Media Fill".
APS is the most important periodic validation activity for aseptic processing. Annex 1 requires it at least twice a year for each filling line; the batch size is typically ≥ 5,000 units, and the acceptance criterion is zero growth (0 CFU). An APS must include all routine operations, including personnel interventions, which is why intervention management is the core challenge in APS design.
Asepsis
A state of control attained by using an aseptic work area and performing activities in a manner that precludes microbial contamination of the exposed sterile product.
Asepsis
A state of control attained by using an aseptic work area and performing activities in a manner that precludes microbiological contamination of the exposed sterile product.
Asepsis is a "state", not a "test result". It is the outcome of personnel, environment and equipment working together, and it must be actively maintained rather than passively confirmed.
B
Bacterial retention testing
This test is performed to validate that a filter can remove bacteria from a gas or liquid. The test is usually performed using a standard organism, such as Brevundimonas diminuta at a minimum concentration of 107 Colony Forming Units/cm2.
Bacteria Retention Test
A test that verifies whether a filter can effectively retain bacteria, typically using the challenge organism Brevundimonas diminuta(one of the smallest bacteria), at a challenge level of ≥ 10⁷ CFU/cm².
This is the core test for validating a sterilising grade filter, and it is a safeguard in addition to PUPSIT (pre-use post-sterilisation integrity testing). Filter validation must be performed under conditions representative of actual use (same differential pressure, flow rate and product matrix).
Barrier
A physical partition that affords aseptic processing area (usually grade A) protection by separating it from the background environment. Such systems frequently use in part or totally the Barrier Technologies known as RABS or isolators.
Barrier
A physical barrier that separates the Grade A aseptic processing area from the background environment (Grade B cleanroom). The two main technologies are RABS and isolators.
Barrier technology is the core direction promoted by the 2022 version of Annex 1. Compared with a traditional open Grade A area (protected by unidirectional airflow alone), RABS and isolators greatly reduce the contamination risk from personnel and are the standard configuration of modern aseptic manufacturing facilities.
Bioburden CRITICAL
The total number of microorganisms associated with a specific item such as personnel, manufacturing environments (air and surfaces), equipment, product packaging, raw materials (including water), in-process materials, or finished products.
Bioburden
The population of viable microorganisms present on a specific item, including personnel, the environment (air and surfaces), equipment, packaging materials, raw materials, in-process materials or finished product.
Bioburden is not a "contamination event"; it is a permanently present process parameter that needs to be controlled. In sterile manufacturing, bioburden must be kept within validated limits before the sterilisation step, otherwise the basis for calculating sterilisation efficacy no longer holds. Section 10 requires it to be determined for every batch. Bioburden sampling points should represent the "worst case" (for example the end of the hold time).
Bio-decontamination
A process that eliminates viable bioburden via use of sporicidal chemical agents.
Bio-decontamination
A process that eliminates viable bioburden by means of a sporicidal agent. It is mainly used for periodic decontamination of the internal surfaces of isolators (for example VHP, vaporised hydrogen peroxide treatment).
Bio-decontamination is a key advantage of isolator technology: a thorough sporicidal decontamination can be performed regularly, achieving a higher level of microbial control than conventional cleanroom disinfection. Every bio-decontamination cycle must be validated (log reduction data) and its effect confirmed with BIs.
Biological Indicators (BI)
A population of microorganisms inoculated onto a suitable medium and placed within a steriliser or load or room locations to determine the sterilisation or disinfection cycle efficacy of a physical or chemical process. The challenge microorganism is selected and validated based upon its resistance to the given process. Incoming lot D-value, microbiological count and purity define the quality of the BI.
Biological Indicators (BI)
Microorganisms inoculated at a known population and highly resistant to a specific process, placed in the sterilizer or at decontamination locations to verify the performance of the sterilisation / decontamination cycle.
BI quality is defined by three parameters: D-value (a measure of the resistance of each BI strain), initial population (typically ≥ 10⁶ CFU/BI) and purity. These three parameters must be verified on receipt of each BI lot, otherwise the validation conclusion is invalid. Geobacillus stearothermophilus is commonly used for steam sterilisation, and Bacillus atrophaeus for VHP decontamination.
Blow-Fill-Seal (BFS) 2022 NEW
A technology in which containers are formed from a thermoplastic granulate, filled with product, and then sealed in a continuous, integrated, automatic operation. The two most common types of BFS machines are the Shuttle type (with Parison cut) and the Rotary type (Closed Parison).
Blow-Fill-Seal (BFS)
A fully automated, integrated technology that forms containers directly from thermoplastic granules, fills them immediately and seals them immediately. There are two main machine types: shuttle type (with parison cutting) and rotary type (closed parison).
BFS is the mainstream technology for terminally sterilised products (large-volume parenterals, ophthalmic preparations), and its "form, fill and seal in one operation" design inherently minimises environmental exposure time. The 2022 version of Annex 1 has a dedicated section requiring Grade A protection of the critical zone of the BFS process (around the product nozzle).
C
Campaign manufacture
A manufacture of a series of batches of the same product in sequence in a given period of time with strict adherence to established and validated control measures.
Campaign Manufacture
The continuous manufacture of a series of batches of the same product over a given period, with strict adherence to established and validated control measures.
Campaign manufacture allows multiple batches to be produced consecutively without cleaning the equipment, provided it has been validated that no cross-contamination occurs during the campaign and a maximum campaign duration (related to microbial control capability) has been set.
Classified area / Clean area / Cleanroom / Cleanroom classification / Cleanroom qualification
Classified area: An area that contains a number of cleanrooms.

Clean area: An area with defined particle and microbiological cleanliness standards usually containing a number of joined cleanrooms.

Cleanroom: A room designed, maintained, and controlled to prevent particle and microbial contamination of drug products, assigned and reproducibly meets an appropriate air cleanliness level.

Cleanroom classification: A method of assessing air cleanliness against a specification by measuring total particle concentration.

Cleanroom qualification: A method of assessing the level of compliance of a classified cleanroom with its intended use.
Terms for Clean Areas and Cleanrooms
Classified area:An area comprising multiple cleanrooms.
Clean area:An area with defined particulate and microbial cleanliness standards, usually made up of several connected cleanrooms.
Cleanroom:A room specifically designed, maintained and controlled to meet a specified air cleanliness grade.
Classification:The technical activity of confirming the grade by particle counting (ISO 14644-1).
Qualification:A comprehensive assessment confirming that the cleanroom is fit for its intended use (including microbiological aspects, airflow patterns, etc.).
Important distinction: classification is a physical test (particle counting), whereas qualification is a broader assessment of compliance. Both must be repeated periodically; Annex 1 requires Grade A/B to be re-qualified every 6 months and Grade C/D every 12 months.
Closed system
A system in which the product is not exposed to the surrounding environment. For example, achieved by bulk product holders (tanks or bags) connected by pipes or tubes, where the full system is sterilised after connections are made. Note: "closed systems" in this Annex does not refer to systems such as RABS or isolator systems.
Closed System
A system in which the product is not exposed to the surrounding environment, for example reservoirs or bag systems connected by tubing and sterilised as a whole after the connections are made.
Note that Annex 1 explicitly states that "closed system" in this document does not refer to a RABS or an isolator. This is to avoid confusion with the naming of barrier technologies. A typical example of a closed system is a single-use system (SUS) bag and tubing assembly, sterilised as a whole after connection and before filling, and not opened during operation.
Colony Forming Unit (CFU)
A microbiological term that describes a single detectable colony that originates from one or more microorganisms. Expressed as CFU per ml (liquids), CFU per m³ (air), or CFU per sample (settle/contact plates).
Colony Forming Unit (CFU)
A microbiological unit of measurement in which one identifiable colony is counted as one CFU; it may originate from one or more microbial cells.
A CFU is not the same as the "number of bacteria", because bacteria can exist in clusters and one colony may grow from several cells. This is an inherent limitation of environmental monitoring data, and is why EM results need to be assessed with trend analysis rather than in isolation.
Contamination Control Strategy (CCS) CRITICAL 2022 NEW
A planned set of controls for microorganisms, endotoxin/pyrogen and particles, derived from current product and process understanding that assures process performance and product quality. The controls can include parameters and attributes related to active substance, excipient and drug product materials and components, facility and equipment operating conditions, in-process controls, finished product specifications, and the associated methods and frequency of monitoring and control.
Contamination Control Strategy (CCS): The Core New Concept of the 2022 Version of Annex 1
A planned set of controls for microorganisms, endotoxin / pyrogen and particles, derived from current product and process understanding, that assures process performance and product quality. The CCS integrates controls for materials, facilities, equipment, process controls, finished product specifications and the monitoring programme.
The CCS is the most important new concept in the 2022 version of Annex 1 and runs through Sections 2 to 10. Its logic is that problems are no longer found by "testing" alone, but contamination is prevented systematically from the design stage. A complete CCS document should cover: identification of contamination sources → control measures → monitoring methods → review cycle. The CCS is part of the PQS (pharmaceutical quality system) and must be reviewed and updated periodically.
Corrective intervention
An intervention that is performed to correct or adjust an aseptic process during its execution. These may not occur at a set frequency in the routine aseptic process. Examples include clearing component jams, stopping leaks, adjusting sensors, and replacing equipment components.
Corrective Intervention
An intervention performed during aseptic processing to correct or adjust the process. It does not occur at regular intervals, for example clearing jams, dealing with leaks, adjusting sensors or replacing equipment parts.
The key difference from an inherent intervention: a corrective intervention is unplanned and indicates that something unexpected has occurred in the process. Annex 1 stresses that every corrective intervention must be recorded and simulated in the APS (media fill) to assess its potential impact on sterility. A high frequency of corrective interventions is a signal of process problems.
Critical surfaces
Surfaces that may come directly into contact with, or directly affect, a sterile product or its containers or closures. Critical surfaces are rendered sterile prior to the start of the manufacturing operation, and sterility is maintained throughout processing.
Critical Surfaces
Surfaces that may come into direct contact with, or directly affect the state of, the sterile product, containers or closures. They must be sterilised before the process starts and kept sterile throughout the process.
Identifying critical surfaces is part of the risk assessment. Filling needles, the inner surfaces of containers and closures are all critical surfaces. If the integrity of any critical surface is compromised (for example cracks or corrosion), the line must be stopped immediately for evaluation.
Critical zone
A location within the aseptic processing area in which product and critical surfaces are exposed to the environment.
Critical Zone
The location within the aseptic processing area where the product and critical surfaces are exposed to the environment, usually the innermost position within the Grade A area, such as directly below the filling needle or directly above open containers.
The critical zone must maintain the strictest Grade A air quality and is the location with the most intensive environmental monitoring. The "first air" principle requires HEPA-filtered clean airflow to reach the critical zone directly, without being interrupted by any obstruction.
Critical intervention
An intervention (corrective or inherent) into the critical zone.
Critical Intervention
Any intervention that enters the critical zone, whether planned (inherent) or unplanned (corrective). This is the highest-risk type of operation in aseptic processing.
Critical interventions are the hardest part to simulate in an APS and are operations that Annex 1 requires to be specially recorded. Additional sampling should be considered after each critical intervention. The APS must include simulation of worst-case critical interventions.
D
D-value
The value of a parameter of sterilisation (duration or absorbed dose) required to reduce the number of viable organisms to 10 per cent of the original number.
D-value (Decimal Reduction Value)
The amount of sterilisation conditions (time or dose) required to reduce the microbial population by 90% (that is, a 1 log₁₀ reduction).
The D-value is a fundamental parameter of sterilisation science. SAL 10⁻⁶ calculation: if the initial bioburden is 10⁶ CFU, a kill of 12 D-values (12 logs) is required. The smaller the D-value, the more sensitive the microorganism is to the sterilisation conditions. The D-value of the BI must be higher than that of the target bioburden organisms, to ensure the validation is conservative.
Dead leg
Length of non-circulating pipe (where fluid may remain static) that is greater than 3 internal pipe diameters.
Dead Leg
A static section of piping in which fluid does not circulate, with a length exceeding 3 times the inner diameter of the pipe. A dead leg is a high-risk area for microbial growth.
In pharmaceutical piping design, dead legs are design flaws that the CCS must identify and eliminate (or minimise). WFI and buffer systems in particular must avoid dead legs, because stagnant fluid at room temperature can form biofilm within hours. Design principle: the 3D rule (dead leg length ≤ 3 times the inner pipe diameter).
Depyrogenation
A process designed to remove or inactivate pyrogenic material (e.g. endotoxin) to a specified minimum quantity.
Depyrogenation
A process that removes or inactivates pyrogenic substances (such as endotoxin) to a specified minimum level, usually by dry heat (≥ 250°C / 30 minutes) or by washing with strong acids or alkalis.
Depyrogenation and sterilisation are two different concepts: sterilisation kills microorganisms, but the cell wall fragments of dead Gram-negative bacteria (LPS, lipopolysaccharide) remain pyrogenically active. Glass containers and stainless steel equipment therefore need to be depyrogenated before sterilisation, or processed using a validated depyrogenation process. Depyrogenation is usually expected to achieve at least a 3-log reduction (USP <1230>).
E – F
Endotoxin CRITICAL
A pyrogenic product (i.e. lipopolysaccharide) present in the Gram negative bacterial cell wall. Endotoxin can lead to reactions in patients receiving injections ranging from fever to death.
Endotoxin
Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria, which remains strongly pyrogenic even after the bacterial cells have died, and which can cause severe reactions ranging from fever to death after injection.
Endotoxin is one of the most dangerous quality risks for sterile injectables because standard sterilisation cannot eliminate it. Control strategy: (1) control of endotoxin limits in raw materials (including water); (2) depyrogenation validation of containers and equipment; (3) minimising Gram-negative bioburden during the process. Endotoxin limits for finished products are set by ICH Q6A / the pharmacopoeias, usually expressed in "endotoxin units, EU/dose".
Extractables / Leachables
Extractables: Chemical entities that migrate from process equipment surfaces under extreme conditions (aggressive solvent, elevated temperature).

Leachables: Chemical entities that migrate into products from product contact surfaces under normal conditions of use and/or storage.
Extractables / Leachables (E&L)
Extractables:Chemical substances that migrate from the surface of equipment or containers under extreme conditions (strong solvents, high temperature), representing the potential maximum risk boundary.
Leachables:Chemical substances that actually migrate into the product under normal use or storage conditions, which are the true subject of safety risk assessment.
E&L analysis is a core part of the assessment of single-use systems (SUS) and container closure systems (CCS). Extractables are a superset of leachables (extreme conditions ≥ normal conditions). Regulatory requirements (such as USP <661> and ICH Q3E) require E&L studies on the final packaging system, to confirm that migrants do not exceed the safety threshold (Permitted Daily Exposure, PDE).
First Air
Refers to filtered air that has not been interrupted prior to contacting exposed product and product contact surfaces with the potential to add contamination to the air prior to reaching the critical zone.
First Air
Air coming directly from the HEPA filter to the product or critical surfaces without being interrupted by any obstruction. First air should not be contaminated by personnel, equipment or other surfaces before it reaches the product.
The first air principle is at the core of Grade A design. Violations of first air include an arm passing over an open container, or equipment reaching into the airflow path directly below the filling needle; these actions direct "second-hand air" (airflow that may carry particles or microorganisms) towards the critical zone. Both APS observer training and operator training must emphasise this principle.
Filter Integrity test
A test to confirm that a filter (product, gas or HVAC filter) retains its retentive properties and has not been damaged during handling, installation or processing.
Filter Integrity Test
A test confirming that a filter still retains its retention performance and has not been damaged after handling, installation or processing. Main methods: Bubble Point Test, Diffusive Flow Test and pressure hold test.
PUPSIT (pre-use post-sterilisation integrity testing) is an extension of this concept: it requires an integrity test before use (that is, before filling) and immediately after sterilisation, to confirm that the sterilisation process itself has not damaged the filter. The 2022 version of Annex 1 strengthened the PUPSIT requirement, which is one of its major regulatory updates.
Form-Fill-Seal (FFS) 2022 NEW
An automated filling process for terminally sterilised products which constructs the primary container from a continuous flat roll of packaging film while simultaneously filling and sealing. May use single web or dual web systems.
Form-Fill-Seal (FFS)
An automated technology that continuously forms containers from rolled film while filling and sealing them, mainly used for terminally sterilised products (IV infusion bags, eye drops).
FFS and BFS are both "form first, then fill" technologies; the difference lies in the material (FFS uses rolled film, BFS uses thermoplastic granules). Both technologies carry a lower contamination risk because the containers are exposed for a very short time, but Annex 1 still requires the filling area to be protected to an appropriate cleanliness grade.
G – H
Gowning qualification
A programme that establishes, both initially and on a periodic basis, the capability of an individual to don the complete gown.
Gowning Qualification
A programme for the initial assessment, and periodic reassessment, of the ability of individual operators to correctly don the complete aseptic gowning.
Personnel are the largest contamination source in aseptic processing. Gowning qualification is not just "passing an exam once"; it is an ongoing programme of confirming competence (Annex 1 recommends that Grade A/B operators be reassessed every 6 months, including surface microbial sampling after gowning). Any person who fails the gowning technique must immediately stop entering higher-grade cleanrooms until retrained and reassessed successfully.
Grade A air supply 2022 NEW
Air which is passed through a filter qualified as capable of producing grade A total particle quality air, but where there is no requirement to perform continuous total particle monitoring or meet grade A viable monitoring limits. Specifically used for the protection of fully stoppered vials where the cap has not yet been crimped.
Grade A Air Supply: A New Concept in 2022
A supply of clean air through Grade A HEPA filters, but without the requirement for continuous particle monitoring or compliance with the Grade A microbial limits. It is specifically used to protect stoppered but not yet capped vials.
This is a new intermediate-state concept in the 2022 version of Annex 1 that solves a practical problem: while stoppered vials are waiting to be capped, the container seal is already established and the microbial risk is greatly reduced, so the full Grade A monitoring requirements are not needed. Introducing this concept reduces the monitoring burden on facilities while retaining reasonable risk control.
HEPA filter
High efficiency particulate air filter specified in accordance with a relevant international standard.
HEPA Filter
A high efficiency particulate air filter specified according to relevant international standards (EN 1822 / IEST-RP-CC001), with a retention efficiency for 0.3 µm particles of ≥ 99.97% (H13 class) or ≥ 99.995% (H14 class).
Grade A requires H14 HEPA filters (99.995% efficiency), while Grade B/C/D typically use H13. The HEPA filters themselves need periodic integrity testing (DOP/PAO testing) to confirm there are no pinholes or installation leaks. HEPA filter replacement is a key maintenance activity and must be performed under a change control framework.
I
Inherent interventions
An intervention that is an integral part of the aseptic process and is required for either set-up, routine operation and/or monitoring (e.g. aseptic assembly, container replenishment, environmental sampling). Inherent interventions are required by procedure or work instruction for the execution of the aseptic process.
Inherent Interventions
Planned operations specified in the process SOP or work instructions and necessary to carry out the aseptic process, such as equipment assembly, container replenishment and environmental sampling.
Inherent interventions are predictable, so they must be performed in the APS simulation to verify that these "necessary interventions" do not compromise sterility. Key difference from corrective interventions: inherent interventions are planned, trainable and can be validated by the APS; corrective interventions are unexpected and indicate a failure of process control.
Isolator CRITICAL
An enclosure capable of being subject to reproducible interior bio-decontamination, with an internal work zone meeting grade A conditions that provides uncompromised, continuous isolation of its interior from the external environment.

i. Closed isolator: Material transfer via aseptic connection; remains sealed throughout operations.

ii. Open isolator: Allows continuous/semi-continuous ingress/egress via engineered openings (e.g. continuous overpressure).
Isolator
An enclosed working environment capable of repeatable internal bio-decontamination, whose internal working area meets Grade A conditions and is continuously and completely separated from the external environment (cleanroom air, personnel).
Core differences between an isolator and a RABS:
• Isolator: fully enclosed, operated by personnel through gloves, capable of sporicidal bio-decontamination, and the background cleanroom can be reduced to Grade D or even unclassified.
• RABS: has rigid walls but is not fully enclosed, and the background must be Grade B.
Analogy: Isolator = a space station (fully sealed, so you can work in space); RABS = a sterile workbench in an operating theatre (more precise, but still in the same larger environment).
The 2022 version of Annex 1 explicitly encourages the adoption of isolator technology, regarding it as the highest level of sterility assurance measure.
L – M
Local isolates
Suitably representative microorganisms of the site that are frequently recovered through environmental monitoring within the classified zone/areas especially grade A and B areas, personnel monitoring or positive sterility test results.
Local Isolates
Representative microbial strains isolated on site from cleanroom environmental monitoring, personnel monitoring or positive sterility test results at the facility.
The importance of local isolates for media quality testing (growth promotion testing) is explicitly required in Section 10.9. The facility must build its own library of local isolates and update it regularly (because the microbial ecology changes over time). Local isolates are also used to verify the effectiveness of cleaning and disinfectant agents: if a disinfectant is ineffective against the strains actually present in your facility, continuing to use the same disinfection regimen means an ineffective CCS.
Lyophilization
A physical-chemical drying process designed to remove solvents by way of sublimation, primarily to achieve product or material stability. Synonymous with freeze-drying.
Lyophilization / Freeze-Drying
A physicochemical drying process that removes solvent by sublimation. Its main purpose is to improve product stability, and it is suitable for heat-sensitive or water-unstable biologics.
During lyophilization, filled vials are transferred into the freeze dryer under Grade A, and are stoppered under Grade A after freeze-drying is complete. This is the longest step, with the most complex environmental exposure, in the entire aseptic filling process. Annex 1 has strict requirements for Grade A protection during freeze dryer loading/unloading, and the APS must simulate lyophilization operations (usually using a growth-supporting medium processed under lyophilization conditions).
O – P
Overkill sterilisation
A process that is sufficient to provide at least a 12 log₁₀ reduction of microorganisms having a minimum D-value of 1 minute.
Overkill Sterilisation
A sterilisation process that provides at least a 12 log₁₀ reduction of microorganisms having a minimum D-value of 1 minute, so that SAL 10⁻⁶ is achieved even if the bioburden is zero.
The logic of overkill: because a 12-log kill capability far exceeds any realistic bioburden, bioburden does not need to be monitored for every batch (only periodically, see Section 10.3). This is a "conservative excess" design that sacrifices some process efficiency in exchange for simpler quality control. It applies to products that can withstand harsher sterilisation conditions (such as glass containers and equipment parts).
Post-aseptic processing terminal heat treatment 2022 NEW
A terminal moist heat process employed after aseptic processing which has been demonstrated to provide a sterility assurance level (SAL) 10⁻⁶ but where the requirements of steam sterilisation (e.g. F₀≥8 min) are not fulfilled. May also benefit in destruction of viruses.
Post-Aseptic Processing Terminal Heat Treatment (PAT)
An additional terminal moist heat treatment applied after aseptic filling, validated to achieve SAL 10⁻⁶ but not meeting the full requirements of standard steam sterilisation (F₀ ≥ 8 minutes), and which may also help inactivate viruses.
This is a hybrid approach newly introduced in the 2022 version of Annex 1: it combines the flexibility of aseptic filling (suitable for heat-sensitive products) with the extra safety assurance of terminal treatment (especially for viral risk). The approach requires full validation, including worst-case heat distribution studies and SAL calculations, and cannot be used in place of an adequately designed aseptic filling process.
R
Rapid Transfer System/Port (RTP)
A system used for the transfer of items into RABS or isolators that minimizes the risk to the critical zone. An example would be a rapid transfer container with an alpha/beta port.
Rapid Transfer System / Port (RTP)
A system used to transfer items into a RABS or isolator that minimises the risk to the critical zone. Typical design: an alpha/beta double-door system (the outer beta door docks with the alpha port on the equipment, and only then can the inside be opened).
The RTP is a core accessory of barrier technology (RABS/isolator). Its design ensures that the transfer does not break the isolation. Principle of the alpha/beta system: the two ports can only be opened after docking, so there is physically never a state of "simultaneous opening to the outside" during the whole operation, greatly reducing the contamination risk during transfer.
Restricted Access Barrier System (RABS) CRITICAL
System that provides an enclosed, but not fully sealed, environment meeting defined air quality conditions (grade A for aseptic processing), using a rigid-wall enclosure and integrated gloves to separate its interior from the surrounding cleanroom environment. Inner surfaces disinfected with sporicidal agent. Operators use gloves, half suits, RTPs and other integrated transfer ports for manipulations. Doors are rarely opened and only under strictly pre-defined conditions.
Restricted Access Barrier System (RABS)
A rigid-wall enclosure system providing Grade A air quality, operated by reaching in through gloves, with internal surfaces disinfected with sporicidal agents, and materials transferred through RTPs and integrated transfer ports. There is physical separation, but not complete enclosure, between the inside of the enclosure and the external cleanroom (Grade B).
Active RABS vs. Passive RABS:
• Active RABS: has its own HEPA filtration and airflow system, and can maintain positive pressure even when the doors are open.
• Passive RABS: relies on the HEPA airflow of the background cleanroom and has no independent filtration of its own.
The 2022 version of Annex 1 places strict limits on opening RABS doors: door opening must be governed by an SOP, recorded, and take into account the nature of the intervention and its impact on the APS. The RABS background environment must be at least Grade B (this is the main difference from an isolator).
S
Single Use Systems (SUS) 2022 NEW
Systems in which product contact components are used only once to replace reusable equipment such as stainless steel transfer lines or bulk containers. Typically made up of disposable components such as bags, filters, tubing, connectors, storage bottles and sensors.
Single-Use Systems (SUS)
Systems that use single-use disposable components (bags, filters, tubing, connectors) in place of traditional reusable stainless steel equipment.
Core advantages of SUS: (1) eliminate the risk of batch-to-batch cross-contamination (no CIP/SIP validation needed); (2) shorten changeover time; (3) reduce the cleaning validation burden. Main risks: E&L (extractables/leachables), supply chain disruption and integrity defects (pinhole leaks). The 2022 version of Annex 1 added a dedicated section on SUS (Section 8.74-8.78), requiring a full E&L assessment and integrity validation programme.
Sterile Product
For purpose of this guidance, sterile product refers to one or more of the sterilised elements exposed to aseptic conditions and ultimately making up the sterile active substance or finished sterile product. These elements include the containers, closures, and components of the finished drug product. Or, a product that is rendered sterile by a terminal sterilisation process.
Sterile Product
Within the scope of this document, all sterilised elements (including containers, closures and components) exposed under aseptic conditions that ultimately make up the sterile active substance or finished product, or a product rendered sterile by a terminal sterilisation process.
Note that the Annex 1 definition extends "sterile" to the entire container-closure system, not just the drug itself. Vials, rubber stoppers, aluminium caps, syringe plungers: all components that contact or enclose the product must meet sterility requirements.
Sterilising grade filter CRITICAL
A filter that, when appropriately validated, will remove a defined microbial challenge from a fluid or gas producing a sterile effluent. Usually such filters have a pore size equal or less than 0.22 µm.
Sterilising Grade Filter
A filter that, when appropriately validated, removes a defined microbial challenge from a fluid or gas, producing a sterile effluent. The pore size is usually ≤ 0.22 µm.
"0.22 µm" is the nominal pore size, and the actual filtration mechanisms include mechanical retention and electrostatic adsorption. Key point: the filter must be validated against the specific product matrix (because some organic solvents, extreme pH or high temperature may damage the filter's retention performance). PUPSIT ensures the filter is intact before use, and the post-use integrity test confirms it was not damaged during the process; both are necessary and neither can replace the other.
Terminal Sterilisation CRITICAL
The application of a lethal sterilising agent or conditions to a product in its final container to achieve a predetermined sterility assurance level (SAL) of 10⁻⁶ or better (i.e. the theoretical probability of there being a single viable microorganism present on or in a sterilised unit is equal to or less than 1 × 10⁻⁶).
Terminal Sterilisation
The application of a lethal sterilising agent or condition to a product in its final container to achieve a predetermined sterility assurance level (SAL) of ≤ 10⁻⁶ (that is, a theoretical probability of no more than 1 viable microorganism in 1 million units).
Terminal sterilisation is the "gold standard" of sterility assurance because sterilisation is the last step of the whole process, so earlier contamination can be compensated for (within bioburden limits). Annex 1 explicitly requires: "where the nature of the product permits, terminal sterilisation should be the preferred option". Common methods: steam sterilisation (most common), dry heat sterilisation, radiation sterilisation and ethylene oxide (EtO). Meaning of SAL 10⁻⁶: a theoretical probability of at most 1 non-sterile unit in 1 million; note that this is a theoretical probability, not a standard that permits 1 non-sterile unit.
T – Z
Turbulent airflow / Unidirectional airflow (UDAF)
Turbulent airflow: Air that is not unidirectional. Should flush the cleanroom via mixed flow dilution.

Unidirectional airflow: Airflow moving in a single direction, in a robust and uniform manner, at sufficient speed to reproducibly sweep particles away from the critical processing or testing area.

UDAF unit: A cabinet supplied with filtered unidirectional airflow (previously referred to as Laminar Airflow Unit or LAF).
Turbulent Airflow / Unidirectional Airflow (UDAF)
Turbulent airflow:Non-unidirectional mixed airflow that reduces the particle concentration in a cleanroom by dilution, suitable for Grade C/D.
Unidirectional airflow:Airflow with a consistent direction and uniform velocity that continuously sweeps particles away from the critical zone, and must be used for Grade A/B.
UDAF unit:Equipment that provides filtered unidirectional airflow (formerly known as a laminar air flow hood, LAF).
"Laminar flow" has been replaced by "unidirectional airflow" in the 2022 version of Annex 1, because true laminar flow is hard to achieve in pharmaceutical facilities. The key requirement is "a single direction and uniform velocity", not perfect laminar flow. Typical unidirectional airflow velocity for Grade A: 0.36–0.54 m/s (vertically downwards). Airflow velocity needs to be validated to confirm that it sweeps particles away effectively and does not create eddies because of equipment interference.
Worst case
A set of conditions encompassing processing limits and circumstances, including those within standard operating procedures, that pose the greatest chance of process or product failure (when compared with ideal conditions). Such conditions have the highest potential to, but do not necessarily always result in product or process failure.
Worst Case
The combination of conditions, within the normal range of process operation, most likely to cause process or product failure. It does not necessarily lead to failure, but represents the greatest risk exposure.
Worst case is a core principle of validation design. Examples: bioburden sampling at the end of the hold time (worst case); sterility testing of the last containers filled in a batch (longest exposure time); sterilisation validation using the thermocouple at the cold spot / slowest-heating point (lowest SAL location). If the worst case passes validation, there is reasonable confidence in all better conditions.
Water system
A system for producing, storing and distributing water, usually compliant to a specific pharmacopeia grade (e.g. purified water and water for injection (WFI)).
Water System
A system for the production, storage and distribution of pharmaceutical water, usually complying with pharmacopoeial specifications (such as Purified Water or Water for Injection, WFI).
WFI is the high-specification water most commonly used in sterile manufacturing, with strict requirements for microorganisms (≤ 10 CFU/100 mL), endotoxin (≤ 0.25 EU/mL) and chemical purity. EU GMP (including PIC/S) allows WFI to be produced by membrane filtration (multi-effect distillation + RO/UF); the 2017 revision relaxed the mandatory requirement for distillation, but all methods must meet the same WFI specification. The water system is one of the most complex critical utilities in a sterile facility, and dead leg control and continuous circulation are the core of microbial control.
Z-value
The temperature difference that leads to a 10-fold change in the D-value of the biological indicators.
Z-value
The temperature difference required to cause a 10-fold change (1 log₁₀) in the D-value, used to describe the sensitivity of a microorganism to changes in temperature.
The Z-value is a key parameter for calculating the F₀ value (sterilisation efficacy). In steam sterilisation, the Z-value of Geobacillus stearothermophilus = 10°C. F₀ calculation: F₀ = ∫₀ᵗ 10^[(T-121)/Z] dt, which integrates the equivalent lethal effect of all temperature-time combinations across the whole sterilisation cycle. F₀ ≥ 8 minutes (European standard) is equivalent to the lethality of 8 minutes of sterilisation at 121°C.

Section Review: Glossary Summary

  • CCS (contamination control strategy) is the soul of the 2022 version of Annex 1: all control measures must be integrated into one systematic plan, rather than scattered tests and specifications
  • Hierarchy of barrier technologies: Isolator (fully enclosed) > Active RABS (independent airflow) > Passive RABS (relies on background) > traditional open Grade A; Annex 1 encourages preferring the higher-level barrier technologies
  • Alert vs Action: an Alert Level is an "early warning that triggers investigation"; an Action Limit is a "mandatory CAPA that affects the batch release decision"; both must have a written procedure
  • First Air + Critical Zone: the physical protection logic of Grade A: HEPA airflow must reach the product exposure point directly and undisturbed; any intervention is a challenge to this protection
  • SAL, D-value, Z-value, F₀ form the quantitative framework of sterilisation science: bioburden (initial load) × process efficacy (D-value × cycles) = final SAL; this is the mathematical basis of terminal sterilisation validation
  • SUS and E&L: the advantages of single-use systems (no cross-contamination, fast changeover) are conditional on an E&L assessment; the supplier CoC cannot replace the user's own compatibility assessment
